§ 25. 英诺森二世，1130–1143年，与尤金三世，1145–1153年

Bernhardi)：《康拉德三世》 (Konrad III)。莱比锡，1883年，2卷。——赫费勒—克诺普弗勒 (Hefele-Knöpfler)，第V卷，385–532。——吉泽布雷希特 (Giesebrecht)，第IV卷，54及后。——格雷戈罗维乌斯 (Gregorovius)，IV. 403及后。豪克 (Hauck)，IV. 130及后。——圣伯尔纳的诸种传记。

加里斯都二世 (Calixtus II.) 的继任者是何诺里乌斯二世 (Honorius II.)，他在1124–1130年间执政六年，并无大事发生。他去世后，英诺森二世（1130–1143年）与阿纳克莱图斯二世 (Anacletus II.，1130–1138年) 之间爆发了一场危险的分裂；两人分别代表罗马两个强大的家族：弗兰吉帕尼家族 (Frangipani)，即“制饼者” (Breadmakers)，1以及皮耶尔莱奥尼家族 (Pierleoni)。

英诺森曾任乌尔班二世 (Urban II.) 的枢机使节，也是沃尔姆斯协约的调停者，素以学识卓越、为人虔诚著称，连他的反对者也无法否认。他还有先行当选的优势，但这次选举的法律效力颇成疑问，因为它只是由少数枢机匆忙聚集在一处不为人知的地方进行的，目的是抢在竞争者之前完成选举。2

阿纳克莱图斯是皮耶尔莱奥内 (Pierleone)，即彼得鲁斯·莱奥尼斯 (Petrus Leonis) 的儿子，是利奥 (Leo) 的孙子。利奥是一位受过洗的犹太银行家，在希尔德布兰德派教皇治下获得了巨大的经济、社会和政治影响力。一个数百人的犹太社群获准居住在特拉斯提弗列 (Trastevere) 和台伯河 (Tiber) 的河心岛周围，作为基督教真实性的一个长存见证；这个社群中产生了一些最优秀的医生和最富有的银行家，在贵族和教皇陷入经济困境时给予他们帮助。阿纳克莱图斯的相貌显露出他的闪族血统，在品德上也不如英诺森；但他获得了多数枢机的选票，以及主要贵族家族和罗马市民的支持。在诺曼人的帮助下，他占据罗马，驱逐对手，罢免敌对的枢机，并让自己的友人充斥枢机团。

英诺森被迫逃往法国，在那里获得克吕尼的彼得 (Peter of Cluny) 和克莱尔沃的伯尔纳 (Bernard of Clairvaux) 的有力支持；这两人是他们那个时代最伟大的修士，也是最具权威的发言人。所有修会以及法国和英格兰的国王都承认他是合法教皇。

双方都向萨克森 (Saxony) 的洛泰尔二世（亦称三世）(Lothaire II. (III.)，1125–1137年) 求助。他决定支持英诺森，以武力护送他和圣伯尔纳进入罗马，并于1133年6月4日由教皇加冕为皇帝，作为回报。

然而，洛泰尔离去后，阿纳克莱图斯在诺曼公爵罗杰 (Roger) 和对立皇帝康拉德三世 (Conrad III.) 一党的帮助下，重新占据罗马。他立罗杰二世为西西里国王，从而协助建立了一个延续七百三十年的王国，直到1860年被并入意大利王国。英诺森退往比萨 (Pisa，1135年)。洛泰尔第二次远征意大利，击败罗杰二世。伯纳德再次来到罗马，成功地巩固了英诺森的地位。就在此时，阿纳克莱图斯于1138年去世。1139年，第二次拉特兰会议庄严宣告分裂已经弥合。不久，英诺森与罗杰之间爆发战争，英诺森被俘。他获释后，确认罗杰为西西里国王。洛泰尔已于1137年返回德国，并在那里去世。英诺森曾将玛蒂尔达 (Matilda) 的领地授予他，条件是每年缴纳一笔款项。后来的教皇以这项交易为根据，声称皇帝是教皇的封臣。

塞莱斯廷二世 (Coelestin II.，1143–1144年) 和卢修斯二世 (Lucius II.，1144–1145年) 短暂在位之后，圣伯尔纳的学生兼友人尤金三世于1145年2月15日当选，任职至1153年7月8日。他在紫袍之下穿着西托 (Citeaux) 修士的粗布衫。由于布雷西亚的阿诺德 (Arnold of Brescia) 引起的动乱，他不得不逃离罗马，大部分时间都在流亡中度过。在他任内，埃德萨失陷，第二次十字军东征展开。尤金之所以主要引人关注，在于他与圣伯尔纳的关系；这位明智而忠诚的顾问为他写下了论教皇制的著名论文《论省察》 (de consideratione)。3

§ 26. 布雷西亚的阿诺德

在英诺森二世、尤金三世和阿德里安四世 (Adrian IV.) 任教皇期间，发生了布雷西亚的阿诺德这一引人注目的插曲。他是一位未获成功的教会与政治鼓动者，抗议教会的世俗化，试图使教会恢复使徒时代的贫穷和纯洁。这两个观念在他心中紧密相连。他宣扬的原则是：教会、教士以及修士都应当像基督和使徒一样，不拥有任何世俗财产，靠民众的什一奉献和自愿捐献生活。他们的职分纯属灵性。地上的一切事物都属于平信徒和世俗政府。

他身体力行自己的教导，挨家挨户乞讨每日所需的食物。他是一位严守苦修、虔诚度日的修士，性情热烈，口才深得民心，精通圣经，不安于现状，激进而无所畏惧。4除了圣餐与婴儿洗礼的教义之外，他赞同天主教的正统信仰；但他对这两项圣礼的具体见解却不得而知。5

他将这套教会方案与一套政治方案结合起来。他投身于罗马人摆脱教皇权威、恢复古代共和国的运动。他把一切世俗权力都交给平信徒，因此赢得了平信徒的拥戴，却失去了教士的支持。正是政治上的纠葛导致了他的覆亡。

阿诺德生于伦巴第 (Lombardy) 的布雷西亚 (Brescia)，在教会中受任为诵读员。他是阿伯拉尔 (Abaelard) 的学生，被称为这个歌利亚的拿兵器者。6他同情老师那种独立自主、敌视教会权威的精神，也可能像尼安德所推想的那样，受到这位富有感召力的教师的伦理原则影响。可以确定的是，后来他曾站在阿伯拉尔一边，反对圣伯尔纳，而后者成了他的死敌。不过，除了共同反对教阶制度之外，两人的差异极大。阿伯拉尔是哲学家，阿诺德是政治家；阿伯拉尔是思辨的思想家，阿诺德是务实的传道者；阿伯拉尔是理性主义者，阿诺德是热情奔放者。前者动摇传统的正统信仰，后者抨击教士的道德和教会的世俗权力。阿诺德的才智远不及阿伯拉尔，但其教导在实际趋向上却危险得多，因为它倾向于使教会陷于贫困，并使社会发生革命。巴罗尼乌斯 (Baronius) 称他为“政治异端之父”。

阿诺德以苦修的热忱致力于教士的道德改革，在这一点上，他与希尔德布兰德派志同道合；但在教皇世俗权力的问题上，他却走向另一个极端。希尔德布兰德力求确立教会凌驾于国家之上的神权至上地位；阿诺德则认为，教会的福祉在于教会与国家完全分离，教职也摆脱世俗事务的纠缠。我们可以说，帕斯卡二世愿意将授职权让给皇帝时，已为这一理论预备了道路。希尔德布兰德派的改革几乎消逝，旧日的腐败重新出现。教会的世俗权力助长了教士的世俗化。《教皇史》 (Historia Pontificalis) 的作者说，阿诺德的教义与福音相合，却与实际状况形成刺目的对照。他的对手圣伯尔纳，同样反对主教的奢华排场和教皇的世俗操劳，并表示希望有朝一日能看见“教会像古时一样，撒网是为得灵魂，而不是为得金钱”。7所有修会都抗议教会的世俗化，并在修道院的围墙之内实践使徒式贫穷的原则。但阿诺德将这一原则也推广到在俗教士，甚至将贫穷定为神父和修士得救的条件。8

阿诺德的讲道在伦巴第博得民众的热烈赞许，并在民众与布雷西亚主教之间引发激烈争执。在1139年的拉特兰会议上，他被控煽动平信徒反对教士，因而被视为分裂者（而非异端）而革职，奉命缄默，并被逐出意大利。

他再次前往法国，卷入阿伯拉尔与伯纳德的争论。1140年，教皇英诺森判令阿伯拉尔和阿诺德二人保持缄默，并隐居修道院。阿伯拉尔已厌倦争斗，也厌倦人生，于是顺服，退往克吕尼修道院，两年后在那里安然去世。9但阿诺德却在巴黎开始了一系列公开讲演，抨击教士的世俗作风与不道德行为。他尤其揭露主教的贪婪，也指控圣伯尔纳怀有不圣洁的野心，并嫉妒学者。伯纳德称他是一个言语如蜜、教义如毒的人。应伯纳德的请求，国王将阿诺德逐出法国。

阿诺德逃往苏黎世，受到教皇使节圭多枢机 (Cardinal Guido) 的友善接待和保护；圭多从前是他在巴黎的同学。10但伯纳德甚至追逼他到那里，向康斯坦茨 (Constance) 主教告发他。

经过几年行踪不明的流亡生活，阿诺德以一场政治运动领袖的身份出现在罗马。英诺森二世曾准许他返回意大利；尤金三世也赦免了他，条件是他须在罗马的圣地行补赎礼。但这位教皇逃往法国之后，阿诺德又宣讲使徒式贫穷的教义，称教皇和枢机为法利赛人和文士，称他们的教会为买卖之所和贼窝。他受到罗马元老院的保护，人民则奉他为偶像。罗马人已经否认教皇的权威，驱逐教皇，仿照古代的模式，代之以纯粹的世俗政府，并邀请康拉德三世扮演君士坦丁一世或查士丁尼的角色。他们沉迷于政体的梦想之中。古罗马统治的传统以各种形式支配着中世纪：它在德意志帝国中作为普世君主政体而存续，在教皇制中作为普世神权政治而存续，在罗马人民中则表现为一个短命的共和国。现代反对教皇世俗权力的意大利人要明智一些：他们只是要求意大利人民自我治理的自然权利，并将罗马的统治范围限于意大利。

阿诺德从教会领域走进政治领域，为这个新共和国披上了宗教的光环。他身穿修士长袍，在卡庇托尔 (Capitol) 的废墟上向元老 (patres conscripti) 讲道，劝他们重建卡庇托尔，恢复古时元老和骑士的等级制度。他消瘦的面容使他看起来如同幽灵，更增强了他雄辩的感染力。

但共和制的试验失败了。人民终于在教皇阿德里安四世的禁令 (interdict) 之下被迫屈服。1154年，阿诺德被逐出罗马，不久之后，憎恨民主和共和主义的皇帝腓特烈一世 (Frederick I.) 下令将他绞死。1155年，他的尸体被焚烧，骨灰撒入台伯河，以免他的仰慕者崇拜他的遗骨。11

阿诺德的声音，是对教皇制的世俗目标和教士世俗作风的抗议，至今仍有人聆听。格雷戈罗维乌斯说：“罗马至今仍受中世纪的禁咒束缚，这禁咒如此顽固，以致一位十二世纪异端的灵魂尚未得着安息，还必须继续徘徊于罗马。”天主教主教赫费勒不肯把他列入“真正的异端”之中。121883年，布雷西亚为这位杰出的乡梓之子树立了一座纪念碑。

阿诺德派有一段时期继续捍卫他们老师的教义，1184年在维罗纳召开的一次会议上被宣布为异端，此后便消失了。

然而，使徒式贫穷的观念和对教皇制世俗权力的反对，又在方济各会的属灵派 (Spirituals) 中重新出现。阿诺德恢复罗马共和国的政治方案，两百年后由科拉·迪·里恩齐 (Cola di Rienzi) 再度提出（1347年），但结果并未更好；因为里恩齐被杀害，尸体被焚烧，骨灰随风飘散（1354年）。

随着康拉德三世的即位，强大的霍亨斯陶芬家族登上帝位，从1138年一直统治到1254年。家族名称来自其世袭城堡霍亨斯陶芬 (Hohenstaufen)，位于施瓦本 (Swabia) 格平根 (Göppingen) 附近鲁厄阿尔卑山 (Rough Alp) 的一座山丘上。13他们的祖先是十一世纪的骑士比伦的腓特烈 (Friedrich von Büren) 及其子施陶芬的腓特烈 (Friedrich von Staufen)；后者忠心拥护皇帝亨利四世，皇帝立他为施瓦本公爵（1079年），并将女儿阿格尼丝 (Agnes) 嫁给他。因此，他们与教皇希尔德布兰德的对手有了血缘关系，并在德国和意大利的血腥纷争中，与反对归尔甫派的吉伯林派站在同一阵线上。亨利六世 (Henry VI.)（1190–1197年）通过婚姻取得那不勒斯和西西里王国。他的儿子腓特烈二世使家族的繁荣达到顶峰；然而，就文化修养和趣味而言，他更像一位意大利君主，而非德国君主，他大部分时间都在意大利度过。

霍亨斯陶芬家族，即施瓦本诸帝，坚持帝权主义 (imperialism) 的原则，也就是维护君主政体作为神所设立的制度之尊严与独立，一方面反对教皇的祭司权至上主义 (sacerdotalism)，另一方面反对人民的自由。

他们与教皇联合，并在十字军东征中为教皇的目标效力：其中三人——康拉德三世、腓特烈一世和腓特烈二世——都曾发动反对撒拉逊人的十字军东征；康拉德三世参加了第二次东征，结果归于失败；腓特烈一世死于叙利亚；腓特烈二世夺取了耶路撒冷。霍亨斯陶芬家族也与教皇联合，反对政治与教义上的异议：巴巴罗萨把布雷西亚的阿诺德当作危险的煽动者，将他牺牲，处以死刑；腓特烈二世虽然自己很可能是不信者，却迫害异端。

然而，在最高权力的问题上，霍亨斯陶芬家族始终与教皇或暗中、或公开交战，最终败下阵来。这场冲突在腓特烈·巴巴罗萨治下爆发；他经过多年争斗，临终时已与教会和解。他的孙子腓特烈二世继续这场争斗，死时已遭教皇制绝罚并废黜。这一王朝在最后一位男性代表康拉丁 (Conradin) 身上，以悲剧性的衰弱告终；他于1268年以大逆罪被斩首。霍亨斯陶芬皇室的这场冲突，比亨利四世与格列高利及其继任者的冲突更为壮阔，因为斗争的层次更高，参与对抗的世俗君主也更有才干。它延续了一百多年，构成中世纪最庄严宏伟的景象之一，并提供了君王所参与的最富戏剧性的若干场面。历史学家格雷戈罗维乌斯认为，他有充分理由说：“中世纪这场泰坦巨人般的战争充满并贯穿了几个世纪，构成有史以来最伟大的景观。”

霍亨斯陶芬家族覆亡以后，德意志帝国直到1806年灭亡为止，仍与教皇制保持着名义上的联系，却不再是欧洲的政治中心力量；惟有宗教改革时期查理五世 (Charles V.) 治下的1519–1558年间例外。当时，它与奥地利 (Austria)、低地诸国 (Low Countries) 及西班牙的王权，以及新发现的美洲土地联系在一起；而这位强大的君主，忠于自己的奥地利和西班牙血统，阻碍了新教争取民族独立与宗教自由的运动。新的德意志帝国建立在旧帝国的废墟和法国的战败（1870年）之上，由一位世袭的新教皇帝统治。

尤金三世之后，继位的是阿纳斯塔修斯四世，他的统治只持续了十六个月。

他的继任者是尼古拉·布雷克斯皮尔，他是第一位、也是（迄今为止）唯一一位戴上三重冠的英国人。他是圣奥尔本斯 (St. Albans) 一位贫穷神父的儿子。他为谋生和求学前往法国，成为阿尔勒与阿维尼翁 (Avignon) 之间圣鲁福 (St. Rufus) 修道院的修士，继而任副院长、院长。他研习神学与教会法。尤金三世任命他为阿尔巴诺 (Albano) 枢机主教，并派他出使挪威 (Norway) 和瑞典 (Sweden)；他在那里整顿教会，使其与罗马的联系更加紧密。

他以阿德里安四世之名，于1154至1159年在位，以卓越的才干和精力执掌教皇之职。一个乞丐竟被提升到基督教世界最高的尊位！命运的两个极端在这个英国人身上相遇。然而，他觉得贫穷时比掌权时更加幸福。他在祝圣后不久便宣称：“教皇的宝座满是荆棘，教皇的披风满是破洞，而且沉重得足以压垮最强壮的人。”在这一崇高职位上稍有阅历之后，他又说：“世上还有谁像教皇这样悲惨吗？我在圣彼得的座位上遭遇了这么多烦恼，相比之下，我从前生活中的一切苦楚都显得甘甜了。”14

罗马人在阿诺德领导下，要求他放弃对世俗统治的一切权利主张；但他拒绝了。一名阿诺德派信徒在大街上袭击一位枢机，造成流血事件之后，他向全城颁布了禁令——这是历史上的第一次。他凭借这种不流血、却可畏而极其有效的武器，迫使人民屈服。他废除共和政府，驱逐阿诺德及其追随者，并接管了拉特兰。

这时，腓特烈一世 (Frederick I.)——意大利人因他胡须的颜色而称他为巴巴罗萨 (Barbarossa，红胡子)，他是德意志诸帝中最勇敢、最强悍、也最专横的君主之一，即沉睡在基夫霍伊泽山 (Kyffhäuser) 中的那一位15——率领强大的军队，首次远征意大利，要从伦巴第人手中接受象征王权的铁冠，并从教皇手中接受象征帝权的金冠（1154年）。

教皇提出，交出阿诺德 (Arnold) 是为他加冕的首要条件。巴巴罗萨欣然答应，并下令处死这位民众鼓动者。他初次会见阿德里安时，亲吻了教皇的脚趾，却没有遵行在教皇下坐骑时扶镫的礼仪。阿德里安感到愤慨，拒绝给他平安之吻。巴巴罗萨得知这是一项古老惯例之后，次日便照办了，但采取了含糊的方式，扶的是左镫而不是右镫。他以武力占领特拉斯提弗列，随后依照规定的仪式，在教廷、教士和军队的欢呼声中，于圣彼得大教堂庄严地接受授职、膏立和加冕（1155年6月13日）。罗马人民发动的起义迅速被镇压，皇帝亲自率军冲入叛军阵中。然而，次日清晨，他便同教皇退往蒂布尔山区 (Tiburtine hills)。由于补给不足，加上伦巴第发生叛乱的传闻，他不得不勉强率军返国。教皇被拒于罗马城外，既无外国盟友，也无国内盟友，便退往贝内文托，在那里遭到西西里国王威廉 (William，即罗杰二世的儿子和继承人) 围困；由于众叛亲离和饥荒所迫，他不得不接受征服者的条件，将西西里王国、阿普利亚公国和卡普阿 (Capua) 亲王国授予威廉为封地。这使他与皇帝发生争执，因为皇帝视阿普利亚和卡普阿为帝国的一部分。1156年，他又反对皇帝与第一任妻子离婚并另娶第二任妻子。

除了这些冒犯之外，阿德里安又添上一件腓特烈无法容忍的事。事情起于隆德 (Lund) 大主教从罗马途经德国、返回斯堪的纳维亚 (Scandinavian) 教区途中遭强盗虐待。16阿德里安将腓特烈的帝国称为一项恩赐，beneficium；这个词既可以指封地，也可以指赠礼。无论取哪一种含义，其中的暗示都令德意志人感到受辱，而他们选择将其理解为一种宣称，即皇帝的帝国是从使徒教座领受的封地。阿德里安派来的两位使节试图缓和这一轻率措辞的含义。

教皇的教权意识太强，腓特烈的帝王意识也太强，两人无法和平相处。1158年，腓特烈率军越过阿尔卑斯山，迫使米兰及其他桀骜不驯的伦巴第城市臣服。完成此事后，他在皮亚琴察附近的龙卡利亚 (Roncaglia) 平原召开帝国议会。这次会议因博洛尼亚法学家作出的裁决而留名于史：皇帝统治帝国，凭的是独立的神授权利，而非教皇的意志。这是自亨利四世时期冲突爆发以来，帝国所赢得的最具决定性的胜利。但法学教授们的裁决并未改变教皇制的政策。

阿德里安再次冒犯了皇帝：他否认皇帝有权在教皇声称拥有的领地上征收军需税（fodrum），并要求皇帝承认教皇对玛蒂尔达赠地、撒丁岛 (Sardinia)、科西嘉岛 (Corsica)、费拉拉 (Ferrara) 和斯波莱托 (Spoleto) 公国所主张的封建权利。腓特烈傲然反驳说，不是皇帝应向教皇效忠，反倒是教皇应向皇帝效忠，因为教皇西尔维斯特 (Sylvester) 正是凭借君士坦丁 (Constantine) 皇帝的赠予，才取得了罗马。随后双方展开书信论战。阿德里安正打算以绝罚惩治他的皇帝仇敌，却在阿纳尼猝然去世。他葬于圣彼得大教堂的一具古代红花岗岩石棺中，至今仍供人瞻观。这个人的生涯就这样结束了：他凭借自己的道德品格和个人魅力，从一个贫穷教士之子的境况，一跃登上基督教世界的最高尊位，并敢以高于君王者的身份面对最骄傲的君主，称帝国的冠冕为教皇的一项恩赐（beneficium）。17

这位英籍教皇曾对罗马全城颁布禁令，做了意大利籍或德意志籍教皇都不敢做的事；他又将爱尔兰赠予英格兰王室，理由是根据君士坦丁赠予，基督教世界的一切岛屿都属于教皇。那道奇特的《值得称赞》诏书（Laudabiliter）鼓励亨利二世入侵并征服这片土地，将它赐给他及其后嗣为产业；这份诏书可能并非真品，但阿德里安确曾作出这一授权，约于1159年写作的索尔兹伯里的约翰对此有所见证。亚历山大三世后来重申了这一授权，而它于1171年付诸实行。18爱尔兰的忠诚子民大概不会愿意再领教一次英籍教皇了。

§ 29. 亚历山大三世与巴巴罗萨的冲突

到了亚历山大三世时期（1159–1181年），阿德里安任内开始的皇权至上主义 (Caesarism) 与祭司权至上主义之间的冲突，变得更加严重。这不是一场旨在毁灭对方的战争，而是一方要求居于至上地位、另一方必须服从的战争。“谁应当更大？”这才是问题所在。这是教会与国家之间古老争端的新形态。凯撒与教皇，只要他们还算有宗教信仰，就同样都是天主教基督徒。他们彼此不可或缺。皇帝或国王需要一位教皇，充当某种首席随侍司铎和听告解神父，以控制臣民的良心；教皇则需要皇帝的世俗力量，来保护教会的财产与权利，并追究异端。皇帝们选立对立教皇，教皇们则支持与皇帝抗衡的帝位争夺者。霍亨斯陶芬家族的雄心是使德国与意大利保持统一；教皇们的利益却在于使两者分离，并按照“分而治之”（Divide et impera）的格言，在德国和意大利煽动分裂。

1159年9月7日，罗马教廷的教务长、杰出的教会法学家罗兰 (Roland) 枢机登上教皇宝座，称亚历山大三世。他先前曾任博洛尼亚的教授，并写了第一部研究《格拉提安教令集》（Decretum Gratiani）的著作。他是由尤金三世擢升为枢机的。他曾以这样一个问题冒犯巴巴罗萨：“皇帝的尊位若不是从教皇领受的，又是从谁领受的呢？”他也曾劝阿德里安将皇帝处以绝罚。他是一位学者、政治家，也是希尔德布兰德派神权政治的有力捍卫者。他在教皇位上长达二十一年，任期之长异乎寻常，是格列高利七世与英诺森三世之间最显赫的教皇。他在与皇帝及四位对立教皇的冲突中，经历了吉凶交错的生涯；但他始终坚持自己的原则，最终凭借道德力量，以及南方诺曼人和北方伦巴第人的物质援助，战胜了敌人。

十四位枢机选出罗兰之后，紧接着又有人选出圣塞西莉亚的奥克塔维安 (Octavian) 枢机，作为皇帝一方的对立教皇。他自称维克托四世，并立即占据梵蒂冈。罗兰在宁法 (Ninfa) 受祝圣，奥克塔维安则在法尔法 (Farfa) 修道院受祝圣。他们驻留在坎帕尼亚 (Campagna)，彼此只相距数英里，各自发表互相矛盾的报告，指责对方在选举中使用可耻的暴力。19

皇帝当时正在围攻克雷莫纳城，双方都向他提出申诉（尽管心情不同）。他遂援用君士坦丁、狄奥多西、查士丁尼、查理曼和奥托 (Otto) 曾经行使的权利，于1160年在帕维亚召集会议，调查并裁决此事。20使者受命邀请两位相互争立的教皇亲自出席。一向拥护皇帝的奥克塔维安接受了邀请。罗兰不信任皇帝，抗议他无权未经自己许可便召集会议。他说，他尊重皇帝，将他视为高于其他一切君王的教会特殊保护者，但上帝已将教皇置于诸王之上。

这次带有党派性的会议，主要由德国和意大利北部的主教组成；经过严肃辩论，于1160年2月11日一致裁定支持奥克塔维安，并将罗兰处以绝罚。皇帝向维克托四世致以惯常的敬礼，为他扶镫，并亲吻他的脚趾。1160年3月24日，亚历山大从阿纳尼发布反制的绝罚令，将对立教皇和皇帝处以绝罚。他由此助长了伦巴第的反叛与德国的分裂。又一次分裂撕裂了教会。

两位相互争立的教皇向欧洲各国宫廷派遣使节。法国、西班牙和英格兰站在亚历山大一边。他在法国避难三年（1162–1165年），受到热情接待。法国国王路易七世 (Louis VII.) 和英格兰国王亨利二世分行在他的坐骑两侧，牵着缰绳，领他进入卢瓦尔河畔的库尔西 (Courcy) 城。德国、匈牙利 (Hungary)、波希米亚 (Bohemia)、挪威和瑞典支持维克托。意大利则分裂了：罗马和托斯卡纳处于皇帝的权力之下；西西里支持这位格列高利派教皇；伦巴第那些商业和制造业繁荣的城市，则不满巴巴罗萨的专横统治，称他为城市的毁灭者。他以铁腕镇压叛乱；经过漫长而残酷的围攻，于1162年3月将米兰夷为平地，驱散居民，并将备受尊崇的东方博士圣髑送往科隆大教堂。

维克托四世于1164年4月去世。帕斯卡三世不顾教会法的规定，被选为他的继任者。应皇帝的请求，他于1165年将查理大帝封为圣徒。

亚历山大三世亲自领导伦巴第同盟，反对皇帝；一个又一个城市宣布支持他。1165年9月，他借助西西里的援助，以及法国和英格兰提供的金钱，返回意大利，占据了罗马。

1166年11月，腓特烈率领一支强大的军队第四次越过阿尔卑斯山，进军罗马，攻占利奥城区 (Leonine city)，使帕斯卡三世据有圣彼得大教堂，并于1167年8月1日与贝雅特丽齐一同再次接受加冕。亚历山大据守台伯河另一岸的城区，但不久便撤往贝内文托。皇帝虽能战胜军队，却在罗马热病面前遇到更可怕的敌人；这病在他的主教、贵族和士兵中造成了可怕的蹂躏。短短数周内，他便因瘟疫失去了最勇敢的骑士和两千名兵士。他仓促拔营，向帕维亚进军（1167年9月）。21他发现整个伦巴第都已结盟反对他，便为求安全，独自一人、几乎如逃亡者一般，重新越过阿尔卑斯山；但他的意志并未屈服，仍决心卷土重来。

第二位对立教皇于1168年9月20日去世，分裂势力也随之崩溃。卡利克斯图斯三世被选为他的继任者，但不过是个影子人物，在位于1168至1178年。22

巴巴罗萨于1174年第五次出征意大利。他摧毁了苏萨 (Susa)，经皮埃蒙特南下，围攻新城亚历山德里亚 (Alessandria)；该城为纪念亚历山大三世而得名，防御极为坚固。他在这里遇到了顽强的抵抗。严冬削弱了他的兵力。他最强大的盟友、撒克逊公爵狮子亨利 (Henry the Lion) 也离弃了他。1176年5月29日，他在莱尼亚诺 (Legnano) 附近与伦巴第人展开会战。他照例冲入战斗最激烈之处，但经过惨烈的杀戮后终于败北，失去了盾牌、旗帜、十字架、长矛和装满金银的箱柜。他率残部退往帕维亚。此时他已没有一个盟友，而在德国又受到狮子亨利这一危险对手的威胁。他如今开始采取切实的步骤，谋求与敌人的属灵领袖亚历山大和解。

皇帝派遣美因茨大主教克里斯蒂安 (Christian of Mainz)（他的教务长、最能干的将领和外交家）、马格德堡大主教维希曼 (Wichmann of Magdeburg)、沃尔姆斯主教康拉德和首席书记官沃特温 (Wortwin) 前往阿纳尼，授予他们与教皇谈判的全权（1176年10月）。亚历山大以极为尊重的礼节接待这些使节，并在持续两周有余的秘密会谈中，与他们议定了初步的和约条款，准备在威尼斯由他与皇帝亲自会晤时批准。

教皇取得皇帝颁发的安全通行证后，于1176年圣诞节离开阿纳尼，同行的有他的枢机，以及西西里王国的两位使节——萨莱诺 (Salerno) 大主教罗穆阿尔德和安德里亚伯爵罗杰 (Roger of Andria)；1177年3月24日，他们抵达威尼斯。皇帝则在威尼斯附近的基奥贾 (Chioggia) 逗留至7月23日。教皇与皇帝使节之间的和平谈判始于5月，持续到7月，以先前在阿纳尼的谈判为基础。

§ 30. 威尼斯和约，1177年

谈判达成了《威尼斯和约》，共列二十八条。23亚历山大被承认为合法教皇。对立教皇卡利克斯图斯 (Calixtus) 被送回一所修道院，他的枢机则降回获任教廷职务以前的职位。贝雅特丽齐被承认为腓特烈的合法妻子，他的儿子亨利 (Henry) 被承认为罗马人的国王。罗马与教皇领地 (patrimonium) 归还教皇，斯波莱托、罗马涅 (Romagna) 和安科纳 (Ancona) 则被承认为帝国的一部分。

和约在中世纪最庄严的大会之一上获得批准。皇帝获解除绝罚后，于1177年7月24日在圣马可大教堂 (St. Mark’s) 前与教皇相会，结束了十八年的冲突。广场上人山人海。教皇身穿礼服，坐在大教堂正门前的宝座上；在他欣慰的心情中，想必也夹杂着自当选、戴上三重冠以来种种痛苦遭遇的回忆。枢机、大主教、主教和其他显贵，依各自品级坐在较低的席位上。

皇帝乘坐总督华丽的贡多拉船，在一队高级教士和贵族的随从下抵达；手持旗帜和十字架的司祭行列，以及民众的欢呼声，迎接着他。他缓步走向大教堂。他对这位德高望重的教皇满怀敬意，不禁脱下披风，俯身拜倒在他脚前。24亚历山大含泪扶起他，25与他行平安之吻，并为他祝福。成千上万的人齐唱《赞美颂》(Te Deum) 作为回应。26

随后，皇帝牵着教皇的手，与他及总督一同走进教堂，在祭坛前献上丰厚的礼物，屈膝跪下，再次领受宗座的祝福。

次日（25日）是圣雅各节，教皇应皇帝的请求举行大礼弥撒，并作了一篇讲道，命阿奎莱亚牧首当场译成德语。皇帝陪他从祭坛走到门口，按惯例为他扶镫，以示敬意。27他还提出牵着教皇坐骑的缰绳，穿过广场送他到船边；但教皇免去了他这项卑微的马夫差事，认为有此心意便如同已经做了，并再次为他祝福。

以上便是同时代作家和目击者所作的可靠记述。他们并未提及这样的故事：皇帝对教皇说：“我行此敬礼是向彼得，不是向你。”教皇立刻回答：“是向彼得，也是向我。”

教阶主义的想象把这次会晤描绘成第二次卡诺莎事件。在威尼斯的绘画中，教皇坐在宝座上，将脚踏在俯伏的皇帝颈项上，并说出《诗篇》91:13的话：——

“你要踹在狮子和虺蛇的身上；

践踏少壮狮子和大蛇。”28

威尼斯与卡诺莎两地情景的差别，正如巴巴罗萨与亨利四世性格的差别一样大。无论在道德上还是才智上，巴巴罗萨都远胜于他那位萨利安王朝 (Salian) 的前任，即使战败，也赢得了敌人的尊重。他维护了自己的尊严，并光明磊落地信守诺言。

使节和书信被派往基督教世界各地，传报和平的喜讯。皇帝于9月底离开威尼斯，取迂回路线返回德国；教皇则于10月15日启程前往阿纳尼。经过十年的流亡，亚历山大于1178年3月12日凯旋进入罗马。

他按照先前与皇帝达成的协议，召集了一次教会会议，以批准基督教世界的和解，并革除分裂期间滋长的某些弊端。第三次拉特兰会议，即第十一次大公会议，于1179年大斋期在罗马的君士坦丁大教堂 (Constantinian Basilica) 举行。与会者约有三百名主教，此外还有许多修道院院长及其他显贵；29会议彰显了罗马教阶制度的荣耀，尽管后来1215年的第四次拉特兰会议又使它黯然失色。会议议事的详情已不可知，只有在第三次、也是最后一次会议中通过的二十七章条文留存下来。

为防止相互对立的选举，会议规定教皇的选举权专属枢机，须由三分之二多数决定；任何人若胆敢接受不足此数的选票而当选，便要受到绝罚和罢黜。30对立教皇（奥克塔维安 [Octavian]、圭多 [Guido] 和斯特鲁马的约翰 [John of Struma]）所施行的按立，一概宣布无效。凡未满三十岁或非婚生者，不得当选主教。为遏制高级教士巡视途中铺张奢侈的风气，规定大主教在此类行程中只能带四十或五十匹马，枢机二十五匹，主教二十或三十匹，总执事五或七匹。受按立的教士必须遣散姘妇，否则便丧失圣俸。违反自然的淫行，应处以革除圣职并禁闭于修道院的惩罚。会议为向法国南部异端发动十字军铺平了道路，并应许参战者在两年内享有与讨伐穆斯林的十字军同样的全大赦。

会议后不久，亚历山大又被罗马共和国逐入流亡。他于1181年8月30日在奇维塔卡斯泰拉纳 (Cività Castellana) 去世。他的在位时间，比他之前或之后的任何教皇都长，只有西尔维斯特一世 (Sylvester I.)（314–385年）、阿德里安一世 (Adrian I.)（772–795年）、庇护七世 (Pius VII.)（1800–1823年）、庇护九世（1846–1878年）和利奥十三世（1878–1903年）例外。当亚历山大的遗体被运往罗马安葬时，民众向灵柩投掷石块和泥土，侮辱他的身后之名。31亚历山大以非凡的坚贞和对格列高利原则的忠诚，坚持与巴巴罗萨抗争。在托马斯·贝克特 (Thomas à Becket) 与亨利二世那场著名的冲突中，他支持贝克特。1181年，他对苏格兰颁布禁令，因为国王威廉 (William) 拒绝承认约翰依教会法规当选圣安德鲁斯 (St. Andrews) 主教。他将红玫瑰 (Red Rose) 授予法国的路易七世 (Louis VII.)，以酬谢这位君主在他早年流亡时给予的支持。他主持了第三次拉特兰会议，并为讨伐卡特里派 (Cathari) 和阿尔比派 (Albigenses) 的十字军铺平了道路。

他年老体衰的继任者卢修斯三世，于1181年9月1日单由枢机选出。罗马人失去了以往参与选举的权利，便以野蛮的残酷手段对待他。他们在图斯库卢姆俘获了他的二十名或二十六名党羽，除一人外，全部弄瞎双眼，给他们戴上写有枢机姓名的纸制主教冠，让他们骑上驴子，又强迫那名被饶过的司祭，领着他们以这副模样去见“卢修斯，那邪恶的买卖圣职者”。他在流亡中死于维罗纳，曾在那里召开过一次重要的教会会议。

有一个值得注意的事实：一些最伟大的教皇——如格列高利七世、乌尔班二世、英诺森二世、尤金三世、阿德里安四世、亚历山大三世，以及他的三位继任者——竟不能赢得自己臣民的效忠，或在罗马遭到围困，或被迫出逃。阿德里安四世曾对他的同乡兼友人索尔兹伯里的约翰说：“罗马不是众教会的母亲，而是继母。”罗马人始终在对古代共和国的追忆与对帝国的追忆之间摇摆不定：时而拥立执政官、元老或保民官，时而欢迎德国皇帝，视他为真正的奥古斯都·凯撒；时而效忠教皇，时而又将他逐入流亡，并且总是把自己卖给出价最高的人。教廷在原则和目标上十分一贯，但在选择达成目的的手段上，无论身在罗马还是流亡在外，都同样受到贪婪和受贿的指控。就连亚历山大三世最坚定的拥护者托马斯·贝克特，也曾愤然斥责枢机们贪爱黄金。

腓特烈皇帝比他的伟大对手多活了将近十年，1190年在第三次十字军东征途中，溺死于小亚细亚的一条小河中。

巴巴罗萨身材中等，面容明朗，皮肤白皙，头发金黄，胡须微红；待朋友仁厚，对敌人也易于和解；秉公严正，虽然往往过于严厉；施舍慷慨，恪尽宗教本分，第二次婚姻幸福美满。他是中世纪骑士精神最高贵的典范，是十二世纪最伟大的君主，既是历史上的英雄，也是传奇中的英雄。32他一手执德国之剑，一手持《查士丁尼法典》，进入意大利，却未能压服伦巴第诸城的政治独立，也未能在与亚历山大属灵权力的较量中获胜。德国人的想象在歌谣和故事中珍藏着对他的记忆，将他列于罗马诸帝之中、仅次于查理大帝的位置，夸大他的美德，宽宥他那些属于其时代的过失，并盼望他归来，恢复德国的统一与强盛。

在亚历山大三世任教皇期间，教皇的教阶制度对英格兰国王所取得的胜利，比对德国皇帝的胜利来得更早，也更为重大。

托马斯·贝克特 (Thomas Becket，亦作 Thomas à Becket)，又称坎特伯雷的圣托马斯 (St. Thomas of Canterbury)，是十二世纪仅次于亚历山大和巴巴罗萨的最显赫历史人物，在英格兰历史上构成了扣人心弦的一章。他重新展开了安瑟伦与王权的冲突，并因殉道而成为中世纪后期最受欢迎的圣徒。

有关他生平的史料极为丰富，从他在伦敦出生，一直到他在自己的主教座堂中被四名王室骑士杀害，都有记载。除零散记述、传说、“受难记”、国事文书、私人信件以及遍及整个拉丁教会的通信外，我们还有六七部同时代传记。然而，他的一生却笼罩在浪漫传说与神学争论的迷雾之中。在他那个时代，他既有博舍姆的赫伯特那样过分推崇他的仰慕者，也有吉尔伯特·福利奥特 (Gilbert Foliot) 那样激烈的反对者；近代传记作者也仍因各自的信仰以及对政教关系的看法不同，而对其品格作出不同评价：有些人视他为英雄和圣徒，另一些人则视他为伪君子和叛徒。我们必须从十二世纪的立场来评判他。

贝克特于1118年12月21日生于伦敦，时值亨利一世 (Henry I.) 在位。他的父亲吉尔伯特·贝克特 (Gilbert Becket) 是奇普赛德 (Cheapside) 的商人，原籍鲁昂；母亲名叫玛蒂尔达，又名罗丝 (Rose)，是诺曼底卡昂 (Caen) 人。33

在后来的传说中，他的父亲是一位英勇的十字军战士，母亲则是一位撒拉逊 (Saracen) 公主，两人在东方相遇，彼此相爱。玛蒂尔达帮助吉尔伯特逃脱囚禁，随后独自追随他来到英格兰。她只懂“伦敦”和“吉尔伯特”两个英语词，便在城中街头徘徊，最后仿佛奇迹般地在奇普赛德找到了心上人，受了洗，并在圣保罗修道院与他举行了极其隆重的婚礼。她曾在梦中看见自己襁褓中的儿子将来成为伟人，并升任坎特伯雷大主教。

贝克特曾在萨里 (Surrey) 的默顿修道院 (Merton Abbey) 及伦敦的学校受教育。后来，他又进入巴黎、博洛尼亚和欧塞尔 (Auxerre) 的大学就读，主要研习民法和教会法，但在学术上并未取得特别卓越的成就。他不是学者，而是政治家和教会人士。

他凭借个人魅力，在世俗社会和教会中崭露头角。他容貌俊美，身材高大，仪表威严，多才多艺，才华焕发，和蔼可亲，谈吐愉快，辩论时反应敏捷而口才出众；他喜爱狩猎和放鹰，精通中世纪骑士的一切竞技。他能攻下最坚固的城堡，也能把最强悍的骑士打落马下。

坎特伯雷大主教西奥博尔德 (Theobald，1139–1161年在任) 于1142年将他收为属员，派他前往博洛尼亚，当时格拉提安正在那里教授教会法；又委派他承担与教皇宫廷交涉的微妙使命，于1154年任命他为总执事，并赐给他其他收益丰厚的圣俸，如贝弗利 (Beverly) 的教务长职、若干教堂以及几项座堂俸禄。当他任大主教时，有人指责他对将他从“贫困”中擢升起来的国王忘恩负义，他便骄傲地列举这一连串教职，而且丝毫没有试图废除兼领多职 (plurality) 这一昭然的弊端；这种弊端一直延续到宗教改革。许多富裕的教士只把自己的堂区看作收入来源，并让无知而报酬微薄的司铎代为履行职务。

亨利二世国王（1154–1189年在位）在登基第二年，应西奥博尔德的请求，将当时年仅三十七岁的贝克特擢升为英格兰大法官。大法官是最高文职显贵，掌管几乎一切王室赏赐与恩惠，包括出缺的主教职位、修道院院长职位、宫廷牧师职位以及其他教会圣俸。

亨利是骄傲的金雀花王朝 (Plantagenets) 的第一位君主，能干、活跃而精力充沛。他从早到晚总是站着或走动，很少坐下。不幸的司提反在位时，无法无天的暴力与混乱扰乱了国家；亨利则在此后建立起法律与严明司法的统治。34然而，他性情暴烈，睚眦必报，生活放荡。他常常大发雷霆，发作时如同疯子。他是西欧最强大的君主。他在欧洲大陆的领地比法国国王的还要广大，包括曼恩 (Maine)、诺曼底、安茹 (Anjou) 和阿基坦 (Aquitaine)，从佛兰德 (Flanders) 一直延伸到比利牛斯山麓。后来，他又在教皇阿德里安四世和亚历山大三世的授权下，于1171年征服爱尔兰，将其纳入版图。他与阿基坦女王埃莉诺 (Eleanor of Aquitaine) 的婚姻使他的王国更加富庶，却也使他卷入与法国的长期战争以及家庭纠纷。埃莉诺此前曾因不贞而被法国国王路易七世 (Louis VII.) 休弃。她嫉妒自己的情敌，35煽动儿子杰弗里 (Geoffrey) 和理查 (Richard) 反叛父亲，1173年被囚禁；亨利于1189年去世后，他的继承人“狮心王”理查一世 (Richard I., Coeur de Lion) 将她释放，并在启程前往圣地时任命她为摄政。她后来退居丰特夫罗修道院 (Fontevrault)，约于1203年去世。

贝克特担任大法官七年（1155–1162年）。他协助国王恢复秩序与和平，改进司法行政。他办事有力，处事公正，把王室利益置于教士利益之上，却并不敌视教会。他对国王完全忠诚，事奉国王如同先前事奉西奥博尔德、后来事奉教皇一样忠实。在他一生所任的各个职位上，尽忠职守始终是他的特征。

他使这一崇高职位获得了前所未有的显赫地位与辉煌气派。他如同亨利八世手下的沃尔西 (Wolsey) 一样，威仪赫赫，权势无边。他虽无国王之名，却有国王之实；亨利频繁前往欧洲大陆期间，他便代行摄政之职。他衣着极尽时尚，身边簇拥着由一百四十名骑士组成的华丽随从，待客挥霍无度，在家政和公共庆典上耗费巨资，其中一部分取自他兼领的各项教会圣俸收入，而他对此毫无愧意。他在威斯敏斯特大厅 (Westminster Hall) 主持王室宴会，席上陈列金器，备有最精美丰盛的佳肴和最上等年份的美酒。他督导英格兰及外国贵族子弟和年幼的亨利王子的教育。国王、军队、贵族、教士和人民都宠爱他。

大法官亲自出面，为王位继承人（当时是个七岁的男孩）与法国国王的女儿（一位三岁的小淑女）缔结婚约；三年之后，这桩婚姻才正式缔成。他奉差遣出使时，带着二百名骑士，以及司铎和旗手，人人身着新装，喜气洋洋；还带有二十四套替换衣裳、供田野狩猎用的各类猎犬和猎鸟，以及八辆货车，每辆由五匹马牵引，每匹马都由一名身着新外衣的壮健青年照管。大小箱柜中装着大法官的钱财和礼物。走在最前面的一匹马，驮着他礼拜堂中的圣器、圣书和祭坛饰物。法国人看见这支队伍，不禁惊叹：“大法官出行尚且有这般排场，英格兰国王该是何等了不起！”在巴黎，他慷慨散赠自己的金银器皿和替换衣裳——给这个一件长袍，给那个一件镶毛皮的斗篷，给第三个人一件皮衣，给第四个人一匹战马。他既达成了目的，又赢得了普遍的爱戴。

尽管他竭力维持和平，法国与英格兰之间仍然爆发了战争；此时，大法官率领自己出资招募的七百名骑士，成为最勇敢的战士。在围攻路易王被困的图卢兹 (Toulouse) 时，他主动请缨，愿率突击队攻城；但亨利心有顾忌，不许他对法国国王动武。后来，他攻取了三座被认为坚不可摧的城堡，凯旋返回英格兰。他的一位颂扬者爱德华·格里姆为表彰他而记述说：“谁能尽述他率领强大军队所造成的杀戮与荒凉？他攻打城堡，将城镇夷为平地，焚毁房屋和农庄，毫无怜悯之心；凡起而反抗他主人权威的人，他从未施予丝毫慈悲。”正如十字军东征的历史所表明的，这种残酷行径与中世纪对虔诚和仁爱的观念完全相容。

贝克特生来就是适合宫廷与军营的人。然而，他的生活虽全然世俗，却并不淫乱。他与国王一同游乐，却不参与国王的放荡行径。他既已领受执事圣职，便不得结婚，但在一个淫靡的宫廷中，他仍保守了自己的贞洁。这一点被特别提出来作为他的美德，因为贞洁在中世纪是罕见的德行。

总而言之，他担任大法官时的公务生涯光荣而辉煌，足以使他在英格兰杰出的政治家中占有一席之地。然而，更为重要的事业还在等待着他。36

§ 32. 大主教与国王

参见第22–24节（第80页及后）。

西奥博尔德于1161年4月18日去世，一年之后，国王任命贝克特为坎特伯雷大主教。他勉强接受了任命，并微笑着提醒国王，他将失去一个仆人和一位朋友。37学识渊博而精力充沛的赫里福德郡主教吉尔伯特·福利奥特（后来任伦敦主教），也许是因自己的野心落空，讥讽地说：“国王行了一件神迹，把一个俗人变成了大主教，把一个军人变成了圣徒。”

贝克特在五旬节后的星期六被按立为司铎，次日，即1162年6月3日，又在威斯敏斯特修道院极其隆重地祝圣为大主教。他的第一项举措，就是将五旬节后的主日定为英格兰教会庆祝三位一体的节日。他承认亚历山大三世为合法教皇，并通过自己的朋友索尔兹伯里的约翰，从教皇那里领受了肩带。

他是诺曼征服以来第一位登上首席主教之位的本土英格兰人；因为兰弗兰克和安瑟伦都是意大利人，而埃斯屈尔的拉尔夫 (Ralph of Escures)、科尔贝伊的威廉 (William Of Corbeuil) 和贝克的西奥博尔德 (Theobald of Bec)，则是诺曼人或法国人。然而，蒂埃里 (Thierry) 认为贝克特代表撒克逊人对抗诺曼人的事业，这一引人误解的理论毫无根据。他与国王的争斗，并非两个民族之间的争斗，而是教会与国家之间的争斗。在这个问题上，他与那些诺曼前任所持的立场相同，只是更加热切，更加有力。他是一个彻头彻尾的英格兰人。到了那个时候，通过通婚、社会交往和商业往来，两个民族已经相当充分地融合了，至少在他所出身的中等阶层中是如此。38

随着职位的改变，贝克特经历了一场彻底而几乎突如其来的转变。王权权术的首要捍卫者，成了教权权术的首要捍卫者；国王最忠实的朋友，成了他最危险的对手和敌人；辉煌显赫的大法官，成了严苛而邋遢的修士。他脱去华丽的宫廷服饰，换上满是虱虫的粗毛衣，以草根为食，喝令人作呕的水。他每天以骄傲的谦卑和炫耀的仁爱，为十三个肮脏的乞丐洗脚，并给每人四枚银币。他的施舍比西奥博尔德增加了一倍，正如西奥博尔德曾将前任的施舍增加一倍一样。他独自在修道院回廊中徘徊，为过去的罪流下悔改的眼泪，屡次鞭打自己裸露的背脊，并花许多时间祷告和阅读圣经。他努力实现中世纪主教的理想，并颇有成效；这种理想把至高的教会权力要求与个人的谦卑、慷慨的施舍和禁欲的自我苦待结合在一起。他并非伪君子，但从圣经和新教的立场来看，他的圣洁是人为造作而不自然的。

他与国王的关系，就像教皇与皇帝的关系。是的，我们可以说，正如他任大法官时比国王更像国王，他任大主教时也比教皇更像教皇。他指责教皇采取拖延迁就的政策。他挥舞属灵之剑对抗亨利，正如先前为亨利挥舞属世之剑一样勇猛。他继承了安瑟伦对抗威廉·鲁弗斯 (William Rufus)、格列高利七世对抗亨利四世的事业，但有一个重大的区别：他不像希尔德布兰德和安瑟伦那样，热心于教会和教士的道德改革，而只热心于教会的属世权力和教士的权利与豁免。他自己任总执事和大法官时，也曾犯下兼领多职的过错，但他并未试图消除这一丑弊；他也不曾斥责亨利所犯的种种得罪上帝的罪，只斥责他侵犯教阶制度至上权威的罪。

新任大主教应教皇亚历山大三世之召，前往法国图尔参加会议，并受到非同寻常的礼遇（1163年5月）。会议由十七名枢机、一百二十四名主教和四百一十四名修道院院长组成；教皇亲自主持，贝克特坐在他右边，约克的罗杰 (Roger of York) 坐在他左边。诺曼底利雪的阿尔诺夫 (Arnolf of Lisieux) 以教会的合一与自由为题作开幕讲道，这正是当时最迫切的问题。会议一致承认亚历山大的教皇资格，申明教士的权利与特权，并严厉谴责一切侵占教会财产的行为。

正是这一点，在英格兰点燃了王杖与牧杖之间的争端。王权的尊严是国王唯一的目标；教会的尊严则是大主教唯一的目标。双方最初的决裂，发生在世俗征税的问题上。

亨利决意将按惯例每海德 (hide) 土地缴纳的两先令款项转归自己的国库。贝克特反对将这项敕令登记施行，理由是这项税款出于自愿，并非依法应缴。亨利勃然大怒，断言：“凭上帝的眼睛起誓，这道敕令一定要登记！”贝克特回答：“凭你所指着起誓的上帝的眼睛起誓，只要我还活着，就绝不许在我的土地上征收这项税款！”

另一个争端的起因是教会法庭的管辖权。国王要求，凡被控严重罪行的教士，都必须由世俗法庭审判。有一名教士，名叫布鲁瓦的菲利普 (Philip of Broi)，曾在主教法庭受谋杀罪审判而获判无罪。国王深为愤怒，但菲利普拒绝到世俗法庭应诉。大主教接手处理此案，却只判处了轻微的刑罚。

国王在威斯敏斯特 (Westminster) 召集议会，以司法平等的名义，并依据“古老的惯例”（即诺曼诸王的惯例），要求凡被控重罪的教士，都应立即被褫夺圣职，依法处置，不得以其职分为庇护。这与司铎只能在所属主教的法庭受审的权利相抵触；在这种法庭上，可以判处鞭笞、监禁及褫夺圣职，却不能判处死刑。

贝克特与众主教一致认为，国王的要求侵犯了教会法，并援引圣经为自己的立场辩护。约押和祭司亚比亚他都有处死亚多尼雅的罪责。约押受了刑罚，祭司却除被革职以外，未受其他处罚。他们又引《那鸿书》1:9，反对让教士接受两重法庭的审判。按照七十士译本，这段经文宣告，上帝不在同一案件中施行两次审判。

国王匆匆解散议会，剥夺了贝克特掌管王室城堡及教育王子的职责。众主教劝大主教让步；起初他拒绝了，声称即使有天上的使者劝他如此软弱，他也不肯；但最后，他在伍德斯托克 (Woodstock) 向国王作了让步，答应诚心遵守王国的惯例。他是在教皇的赈济官菲利普·德·埃莱奥莫西纳 (Philip de Eleeomosyna) 的劝说下屈服的，而此人已被英格兰的金钱收买。39

国王在距离索尔兹伯里 (Salisbury) 数英里的王宫克拉伦登 (Clarendon) 召集王国大议会，以批准这项让步（1164年1月25日）。出席的有两位大主教、十二位主教和三十九位世俗贵族。会议制定了十六条著名的法令，名为《克拉伦登宪章》(The Clarendon Constitutions)，作为英格兰的法律。条文如下：40——

克拉伦登宪章

I. 关于教堂的圣职荐举权及人选提呈权（de advocatione et presentatione）：如俗人之间、教士与俗人之间，或教士彼此之间发生任何争议，均须在我主国王的法庭受审并裁决。

  1. 属于国王封地的教堂（de feudo domini Regis），未经国王同意和许可，不得永久赠与他人。

  2. 凡被控犯罪的教士，应由国王的司法官传唤至国王法庭，就国王法庭裁定应在该处答辩的事项进行答辩；对于裁定应在教会法庭答辩的事项，则应在教会法庭答辩；但国王的司法官须派人进入神圣教会的法庭，察看案件在那里如何处理；若该教士认罪或被定罪，教会今后不得再庇护他。41

  3. 任何大主教、主教或其他显要人物，未经国王许可，不得离开王国；若他们希望离境，国王有权酌情要求他们提供担保，保证他们无论在出行、旅居或归途中，均不损害国王或王国。

V. 被绝罚者不须提供长期担保（ad remanentiam），也不须起誓，只须提供担保和保证，承诺服从为其解除绝罚的教会所作的裁决。

  1. 对俗人的控告，必须由确实而合法的控告人和证人在主教面前提出，使会吏长不致丧失其权利，或由此而得的任何收益。若被传讯者势力如此之大，以致无人愿意或敢于控告他们，则郡长应主教的要求，须令该村十二名忠诚之人在主教面前宣誓，按自己的良心陈述该案的实情。

  2. 凡直接领受国王封地的人，或国王的任何家仆，均不得被绝罚，其土地亦不得被施以禁令，除非事先已征询国王的意见；若国王不在国内，则须征询其司法官的意见，使其得以对此事作适当处置，并使凡属国王法庭管辖之事均在该处解决；反之，凡属教会法庭管辖之事，亦照此办理。

  3. 如有上诉，必须由会吏长上诉至主教，由主教上诉至大主教；若大主教未能秉公裁断，双方应到我主国王面前，使争讼得以遵照他的谕令，在大主教的法庭终结；未经我主国王同意，不得再向上申诉。

  4. 若教士与俗人之间，或俗人与教士之间，因一处地产发生争议，教士主张该地产属于施舍地，而俗人主张其为世俗封地，则应由国王的首席司法机关办理，由十二名合资格者在国王的司法官面前作出认定，判明该地产究竟是施舍地还是世俗封地。若认定为施舍地，案件应在教会法庭审理；若为世俗封地，则应在国王法庭审理，除非双方都声称该地产是从同一位主教或男爵领受的封地；若双方都声称是从同一位主教或男爵领受该封地，则应在他的法庭审理。不过，上述认定不得剥夺原占有人之占有权，直至诉讼判决将其解除为止。

X. 凡属于城市、城堡、市镇或国王王室庄园的人，若经会吏长或主教传唤，就一项罪行答辩，却不应传，可以对其施以禁令，但不得将其绝罚，直至该地国王的主管官员接获通知，使其能够促令此人应传到庭；若国王的官员未能办理此事，他应听候国王发落，而主教则应立即以教会惩戒迫使被告就范。

  1. 大主教、主教以及王国内所有直接领受国王封地的人，均应以男爵领地的方式持有从国王领受的产业，并就此向国王的司法官和官员负责，遵行国王的一切惯例和正当权利；他们应像其他男爵一样，有义务与男爵们一同出席国王法庭的审判，直至审判进入判处肢解或死刑的阶段。

  2. 凡国王领地内的大主教区、主教区、修道院或小修道院出现空缺，应由国王掌管，国王应如同对自己的领地一样，收取其中的一切收入和收益。到了为该教会委任人选的时候，我主国王应向该教会推荐最合适的人选，选举须在国王的礼拜堂内举行，获得国王同意，并征询他为此召集的国内人士的意见。当选者在祝圣以前，应在那里向我主国王行臣服礼并宣誓效忠，承认他为自己的直属封君，以生命、肢体和世俗尊荣尽忠，但须保全其圣职身份。

  3. 若国王的任何贵族，在涉及自身或其属下任何人的案件上，拒绝向大主教、主教或会吏长秉公行事，我主国王应对他们执行司法。若有人侵夺我主国王的权利，大主教、主教及会吏长应对其施行惩戒，使其向国王作出补偿。

  4. 凡财产应被国王没收者，其动产不得由教堂或墓地扣留，以抗拒国王的司法，因为这些动产无论在教堂之内还是之外发现，都属于国王。

  5. 关于到期债务的诉讼，无论是否附有信誓保证，均属国王法庭管辖。

  6. 农民之子，未经其出生地所属领主的同意，不得受按立。

这些宪章是在国王的授意下，由约克大主教罗杰 (Roger of York)、伦敦主教福利奥特 (Foliot)（此二人是贝克特的主要仇敌）、索尔兹伯里主教乔斯林 (Joceline of Salisbury)、理查德·德·卢西 (Richard de Luci，国王的首席司法官) 和巴利奥尔的乔斯林 (Joceline of Baliol)，按照封建制度的精神和语言拟定的。这些条款限制了教士的豁免权；最后一条则侵犯了人民的权利，不过其依据是教会法规：奴隶未经主人同意，不得加入教士阶层。它们使教士与俗人同样隶属于王权和国法。它们把教会降为“国中之国”（imperium in imperio），而不承认她是一个独立自主的“统治实体”（imperium）。它们以法律条文的形式，规定了某些“古老惯例”（consuetudines）；这些惯例可追溯到征服者威廉 (William the Conqueror) 时代，并曾得到兰弗兰克 (Lanfranc) 的认可；但它们在许多方面侵犯了教会的古老特权，与教阶制度所坚持的教士不受世俗司法管辖的原则不相容。这正是国王与大主教争执的主要焦点。

在今日的文明状况下，教士应与俗人遵守同样的法律，承受同样的刑罚，这是毫无疑问的。但我们不可忽视这一事实：在中世纪，教士豁免权不仅具有维护教阶制度的性质，也具有人道的性质，其中包含着对以肢解人体为手段的野蛮刑罚的抗议，因为人是照着上帝的形象造的。它为减轻刑法开辟了道路，使全体人民，无论俗人还是教士，都能受益。这就解释了为何民众对贝克特的事业怀有如此广泛的同情。

贝克特 (Becket) 作了有保留的同意。他回到坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 后改变了心意，给自己施加严厉的补赎，并请求教皇解除自己的誓言，且得到了允准。但亚历山大 (Alexander) 受到巴巴罗萨 (Barbarossa) 和对立教皇的紧逼，又急于保持亨利 (Henry) 的好感，便试图使双方都满意。他应亨利的请求，授予坎特伯雷首席主教的对手、约克大主教罗杰统辖全英格兰的教皇使节权。后来，他又授权约克大主教在威斯敏斯特修道院 (Abbey of Westminster) 为亨利的长子加冕（1170年6月18日），尽管举行这种加冕礼本是坎特伯雷大主教独享的特权。这加剧了与国王的纠纷，并引发了最后的危机。

与此同时，《克拉伦登宪章》付诸实施。在国王法庭被判有罪的教士，与俗人一样被定罪处罚。

贝克特试图逃往教皇那里，乘船驶向欧洲大陆，却因逆风被水手们送回。此举违反了禁止主教未经王室许可离境的法律。

1164年秋，他被传唤到在北安普敦 (Northampton) 王家城堡举行的主教与贵族大会面前，受到指控，说他在担任大法官和大主教期间处理世俗事务不当。然而，危险越大，他的勇气也越大。他拒绝答辩，并向教皇上诉。会议下令传他到罗马受审，罪名是在克拉伦登背弃誓言，以及命令属下主教无视宪章。主教们劝他辞职，他傲然拒绝。他将要被捕，却威胁贵族们，若他们宣判，便将他们绝罚。他采取了大胆的行动，在午夜乔装成修士，带着大主教的披带和印玺，由两名修士和一名仆人陪同，逃往欧洲大陆。

国王没收了大主教的收入，禁止为他举行公开祈祷，将他逐出王国，下令放逐他所有的亲属和朋友，男女共四百人，并停止向教皇缴纳彼得捐 (Peter’s pence)。

贝克特从1164年10月至1170年12月，在流亡中度过了整整六年。法国国王路易 (Louis) 既是亨利的敌人，又是贝克特的仰慕者，便以隆重的礼遇接待他，并将他推荐给当时同样身在流亡、居于桑斯 (Sens) 的教皇。贝克特会见亚历山大，将《克拉伦登宪章》呈在他面前，并提出辞职。教皇谴责其中十条侵犯教会特权，对另外六条则予以容忍，认为其弊害较轻。他温和地责备贝克特软弱，竟宣誓遵守这些条款，但也安慰他说，他已借着受苦赎了此过。他将大主教戒指交还给他，借此确认他的首席主教地位，答应保护他，并将他托付给蓬蒂尼 (Pontigny) 修道院院长，予以殷勤照料；那是一座距桑斯约十二里格的熙笃会 (Cistercian) 修道院。贝克特在此住到1166年，像一位严守苦修的修士，以豆类和稀粥为食，睡在草铺上，午夜接受随从司铎的鞭笞；不过，他偶尔也享用较好的饮食，生活环境仍保留着几分往日的华贵。他那位持重的朋友、索尔兹伯里的约翰 (John of Salisbury)，曾劝诫他不要如此挥霍。

贝克特进一步采取了最极端的措施：1166年圣灵降临节，他在韦兹莱 (Vezelay) 的教堂内，宣布将《克拉伦登宪章》的一切起草者和维护者绝罚。他没有将当时病势危重的国王列入其中，却压低声音，含泪哽咽地警告他，上帝的报应将临到他，禁令也将临到他的国土。他将这一判决通告教皇和英格兰全体教士，并对后者说：“谁竟敢怀疑，上帝的祭司乃是君王、诸侯和所有信徒的父亲与主人？”

亨利怒不可遏。他关闭英格兰各港口，不许携带绝罚文书的人入境，并威胁要对他们施以羞辱性的肢解、绞刑和火刑。他促使蓬蒂尼修道院逐出贝克特，后者遂退居大主教驻地桑斯附近的一座修道院。他又通过使节，从当时流亡于贝内文托 (Benevento) 的亚历山大那里取得了若干让步。教皇急于保住国王的支持，却又写信安抚贝克特，向他保证这些让步只是暂时的。贝克特愤然作答，谴责教廷的贪赃与贪婪。他写信给枢机们说：“你们的金银，在耶和华发怒的日子，不能救你们。”

国王如今决定利用数年前从教皇那里获得、后来却已撤销的许可，42让约克大主教罗杰 (Roger) 为他的儿子加冕。这种侵犯首席主教权利的羞辱行为，再次激起贝克特的怒火。他重申了绝罚令。他像格列高利七世 (Gregory VII.) 一样，将“禁止刀剑不经血的，必受咒诅”这句话应用于属灵的武器。他甚至命令英格兰的主教们，除非国王在1170年11月2日洁净节之前作出充分补偿，否则就要对整个王国施行禁令，停止一切宗教仪式（洗礼、告解和临终傅油礼除外）。43

这些极端措施并非没有效果。几位主教开始动摇，从支持国王转而支持大主教。国王本人也因禁令的威胁而惊惶。教皇继续奉行拖延权变的政策，劝双方各作让步。

1170年7月22日，国王与大主教在图尔 (Tours) 与沙特尔 (Chartres) 之间的弗雷特维尔城堡 (Fretteville，又作 Freteval) 举行了一次彼此以礼相待的面谈，突然达成和解。亨利对《克拉伦登宪章》只字未提，却提议由贝克特为他的儿媳（法国国王的女儿）加冕，并在同一场合再次为他的儿子举行加冕礼。贝克特把过错归于亨利的谋臣，表现得温和而审慎。国王没有提出行平安之吻，大主教也没有要求。

然而，贝克特虽然愿意赦免国王，却决意对他那些邪恶的谋臣，尤其是约克大主教及伦敦、索尔兹伯里两位主教，行使自己的属灵权柄。这几位高级教士不久前曾主持亨利之子的加冕礼。而导致这场惨剧的，与其说是起初那场更为重大的教士豁免权之争，不如说正是这次加冕。

经过与教廷和国王的长期谈判，贝克特于1170年12月1日回到自己久已疏于照管的羊群中。他没有在众人预期的多佛 (Dover) 登陆，而是在桑威奇 (Sandwich) 上岸，却遭仇敌突然拦截；他们搜查了他的行李，要求他撤销对当时身在多佛的几位主教所下的绝罚令。他拒绝了。在前往坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 的途中，乡间的教士和民众前来迎接，将衣服铺在地上，唱道：“奉主名来的，是应当称颂的！”在浩大的行列簇拥下，他乘马来到大教堂，钟声四起；他以“我们在这里本没有常存的城”为题讲道。

被绝罚的约克、伦敦和索尔兹伯里三位高级教士，前去寻求国王的保护；国王当时住在诺曼底 (Normandy) 贝叶 (Bayeux) 附近的一座城堡里。他说：“若凡主持我儿子加冕礼的人都要被绝罚，那么，凭上帝的眼睛起誓，我也同样有罪。”其中一位高级教士（也许是约克的罗杰）说：“只要托马斯还活着，您就永无宁日。”亨利那与生俱来的暴怒又发作了，脱口说出了致命的话：“一个吃过我的饭的家伙，竟用脚踢我；一个受尽我恩惠的家伙，竟敢侮辱国王；一个骑着瘸马、拿斗篷作鞍子来到宫廷的家伙，如今竟毫无阻拦地坐到了王座上。凭上帝的眼睛起誓，我这些忘恩负义、胆怯懦弱的廷臣之中，难道没有一个人能替我除去这个出身卑贱、滋事作乱的祭司所加的侮辱吗？”说罢，他冲出了房间。

§ 33. 托马斯·贝克特殉难：1170年12月29日

关于贝克特被杀一事，我们有五位目击者的记述：爱德华·格里姆 (Edward Grim，剑桥的一位撒克逊修士)、威廉·菲茨斯蒂芬 (William Fitz-Stephen，贝克特的随从司铎)、索尔兹伯里的约翰（他的忠实朋友）、坎特伯雷的威廉 (William of Canterbury)，以及兰贝斯 (Lambeth) 一部手稿的匿名作者。另外两位传记作者，博舍姆的赫伯特 (Herbert of Bosham) 和蓬蒂尼的罗杰 (Roger of Pontigny)，虽在当时不在英格兰，却都与贝克特交情深厚，并且竭尽心力查明事实，连最细微的详情也不放过。

四名出身高贵、拥有大片地产、担任国王内侍的好战骑士，44——雷金纳德·菲茨厄斯爵士 (Sir Reginald Fitz-Urse，意为“熊之子”，原由贝克特引入宫廷)、威廉·德·特雷西爵士 (Sir William. de Tracy，具有王室血统)、休·德·莫维尔 (Hugh de Moreville，诺森伯兰与坎伯兰的司法官)，以及理查德·勒布雷爵士 (Sir Richard le Bret，又作 Breton，通常称为 Brito45)——急切地抓住国王话中的暗示，决意凭着一腔炽烈的忠诚，自担风险，尽其所能，或以监禁，或以放逐，必要时甚至以谋杀来实现此意。他们似乎除了要施以严厉报复以外，并无预先拟定的计划。他们不等指示，便立刻分道前往英格兰，在索尔特伍德城堡 (Saltwood) 会合。这座城堡属于坎特伯雷主教区，当时却由布罗克的兰道夫 (Randulf of Broc) 占据。他们召集了约十二名武装人员，于12月29日星期二清晨，来到坎特伯雷城墙外的圣奥古斯丁修道院 (St. Augustine’s)。

在那致命一天的清晨，贝克特预感自己将死，便劝教士们趁天未亮逃往桑威奇。他在大教堂参加弥撒，向两位修士告解，并照惯例领受了三次鞭笞。宴席上，他比平日饮酒更多，对斟酒人说：“要流许多血的人，必须多喝些酒。”饭后，他退入自己的房间，坐在床上，与朋友索尔兹伯里的约翰、威廉·菲茨斯蒂芬及爱德华·格里姆谈话。他当时年方五十二岁，仍然精力充沛，仪表依旧庄严，一双大眼睛依然炯炯有神。

当天下午约四点，骑士们前往大主教府邸，将武器留在外面，用寻常的斗篷和长袍遮住锁子甲。他们以国王的名义，要求他解除被绝罚的主教和廷臣的罪罚。他拒绝了，叫他们去求教皇，因为唯有教皇才能赦免他们。他宣告：“凡违犯罗马教规或教会权利的人，我决不宽免。我的属灵权柄得自上帝和教皇；我的世俗权利得自国王。凯撒的物当归给凯撒，上帝的物当归给上帝。”骑士们说：“你说这些话，是在拿性命冒险。”贝克特回答：“你们是来我自己的家中杀害我吗？我甘愿受死的心，绝不亚于你们杀我的心。你们的威胁是徒然的；即使英格兰所有的刀剑都悬在我头上，也不能吓得我不顺服上帝和我的主教皇。我不怕你们，在主的争战中，我要与你们迎面交锋。”争执之中，贝克特未能控制自己暴烈的脾气。他的朋友索尔兹伯里的约翰温和地责备他，认为他的语气过于激人忿怒。骑士们离开房间，召集部下拿起武器。

将近五点时，晚祷的钟声响了。在朋友们催促之下，大主教由人持十字架前导，穿过回廊来到大教堂。礼拜已经开始，修士们正在唱诗席上吟诵诗篇，教堂里挤满了人。这时，两个男孩沿中殿奔来，高喊有武装人员正在闯入回廊，引起一片惊恐。贝克特的随从已经进了教堂，便关上门，催他到唱诗席里避险。他说：“走开，你们这些懦夫！我凭你们服从的本分，命令你们不许关门；教堂不可变成堡垒。”他显然已准备好殉道，并且渴望如此。他亲自重新开门，将被关在门外的修士拉进教堂，喊道：“进来，进来——快些，快些！”修士和司铎们惊恐万状，四散逃入隐蔽处和侧礼拜堂，或奔上屋顶，或躲入地下室。只有三人始终忠心留在原地：默顿的罗伯特法政牧师 (Canon Robert of Merton)、随从司铎威廉·菲茨斯蒂芬，以及教士爱德华·格里姆。46一位修士承认，他当时双手紧握，竭尽双脚之力，沿台阶向上奔逃。

贝克特走向主祭坛和大主教座椅；自久远以来，他和历任前任都是在这座椅上登座就职的。无疑，他希望在那里获得殉道的冠冕。此时约为冬日傍晚五点，夜幕渐渐降临，祭坛上的灯在幽暗的大教堂中只发出微弱的光。随后发生的惨剧，在几分钟内便告结束。

与此同时，骑士们身披锁子甲，面部遮护至双眼，手持出鞘的剑，带着一群手执短斧、形形色色的暴徒，冲入大教堂，喊道：“叛徒在哪里？大主教在哪里？”47贝克特走下祭坛的台阶，面向仇敌，回答：“看，我在这里，不是叛徒，乃是上帝的祭司！”他们再次要求他赦免那几位主教，并接受国王的审判。他说：“我只能照我已经做的去做。”随即转向手持剑斧的菲茨厄斯，说：“雷金纳德，你从我手中得过许多恩惠，如今竟带着武器到我面前，闯进我的教堂！”骑士们试图将他拖出圣所，本不想在那里杀他；他却抵住一根柱子，那柱子立在童贞女祭坛与圣本笃 (St. Benedict) 祭坛之间——前者是他特别敬奉的主保，后者则是他所遵守会规的制定者。他说：“我已准备受死。愿教会借我的血获得和平与自由！我奉全能上帝的名嘱咐你们：除我以外，不可伤害这里任何人。”扭打中，他抓住德·特雷西，将他摔倒在地。菲茨厄斯抓住他长斗篷的领口，他骂他是个卑鄙的恶棍，猛地把斗篷从他手中挣脱，喊道：“滚开，你这拉皮条的！”48那骑士被这句污言激得狂怒，挥剑过顶，砍下第一剑，击落了他的帽子。特雷西从地上起来，朝他的头砍去；站在一旁的爱德华·格里姆伸臂阻挡，手臂几乎被砍断，随即退倒在墙边。贝克特保持祷告的姿态，承受一剑又一剑。他感到血沿面颊流下，便低下颈项，等候致命的一击，双手紧握，低声说道：“我将我的事业和教会的事业交托给上帝，交托给法国的殉道者圣德尼 (St. Denis)、圣阿尔菲奇 (St. Alfege)，以及教会的众圣徒。49奉基督的名，为捍卫他的教会，我甘愿受死。主啊，求你接收我的灵魂。”

这是他最后的话。接下来的一剑使他跪倒，最后一剑使他倒卧在圣本笃祭坛脚下。他的双手仍然合拢，仿佛还在祷告。布列塔尼人理查德 (Richard the Breton) 砍去了他那曾受圣油膏抹的头颅上部。副助祭霍西的休 (Hugh of Horsea) 踩住他的颈项，将剑刺入那骇人的伤口，把鲜血和脑浆撒在地面上。50随后他说：“走吧，走吧；叛徒死了，再也起不来了。”

凶手们从教堂冲出，穿过回廊，进入府邸抢掠；这时，一场猛烈的雷雨笼罩了大教堂。他们抢走了约值两千马克的金银，骑上贝克特的骏马，在浓重的夜色中逃去。

托马斯的遗体葬在地下室。他留下的血与脑浆被当作圣物珍藏起来。敬仰他的修士们惊喜地发现，这位一度身穿紫袍和细麻衣的殉道者，在层层衣服下面，贴身穿着一件最粗糙的毛衣，上面爬满虱虫。按照中世纪的观念，这似乎显明了苦修圣洁的完满境界。51直到如今，从回廊进入大教堂的入口附近，人们仍指给来客看他“殉道”的地点。

§ 34. 贝克特被杀的影响

这场残暴的谋杀使整个基督教世界为之震惊。贝克特死亡的时刻，正是他得胜的时刻。他崇高的地位、个人的美德，以及这亵渎神圣的暴行，都使人们的印象愈加深刻。起初，舆论尚有分歧，因为他有强大的敌人，甚至在坎特伯雷也是如此。一名修士宣称，贝克特因顽固而受到的惩罚是公正的；另一些人说：“他想作王，而且要凌驾于王之上。”约克大主教竟敢在讲道中说，贝克特“像法老一样，因骄傲而灭亡”。

但公众敬仰的洪流很快便使一切反对的声音归于沉寂。他的墓前出现了神迹，印证了他配受圣徒和殉道者的崇敬。他的朋友索尔兹伯里的约翰 (John of Salisbury) 写道：“瞎子看见，聋子听见，哑巴说话，瘸子行走，长大麻风的得洁净，鬼被赶出，甚至死人也复活了。”52无疑，确有一些非同寻常的痊愈之事发生；轻信与欺诈则将这些事夸大，并增添了许多。谋杀发生后不过几年，便有两部记载其神迹的汇编问世，一部出自坎特伯雷修院院长本笃 (Benedict，后来任彼得伯勒修道院院长)，另一部出自坎特伯雷修士威廉 (William)。53据这些记载，神迹从大主教死去的当夜就开始出现。他的血对于饮用的人有神奇的功效。54

贝克特死后两年，即1173年2月21日，亚历山大三世 (Alexander III) 为他举行了隆重的封圣典礼；而在他与国王争斗时，这位教皇对他的支持却颇为冷淡。如此迅速便获承认为圣徒的事例，几乎找不到第二个；但公众的心意早已走在前头。教皇的封圣诏书在威斯敏斯特 (Westminster) 的一次会议上宣读。所有曾反对贝克特的主教都在场，为自己的过犯请求赦免，并接受了教皇的决定。12月29日被定为“坎特伯雷的圣托马斯”的庆日。

亨利二世 (Henry II) 被认为是这桩滔天罪行的主使，因而遭到公众唾弃，仿佛受了绝罚。初闻消息时，他把自己关在房内三天，在麻衣与炉灰中翻滚，执意拒绝进食，也不肯接受安慰。他独处了五个星期，一再喊道：“唉，唉，竟然发生了这样的事！”他下令捉拿凶手，并派使节前往教皇那里，为自己申辩，以免遭绝罚和禁令之灾。经过长久拖延，1172年5月22日，在诺曼底 (Normandy) 的阿夫朗什 (Avranches) 大教堂，他终于在教皇使节、鲁昂 (Rouen) 大主教及许多主教与贵族面前达成和解。55亨利手按神圣的福音书起誓，声明自己既未命令、也未希望贝克特死去；这件事给他带来的悲痛，甚于父亲或母亲的去世；他愿意作出充分补偿。他承诺废除《克拉伦登条例》 (Statutes of Clarendon)，恢复坎特伯雷教会的一切权利和财产；如果教皇要求，他愿参加远征耶路撒冷或西班牙的十字军三年，并负担圣地二百名骑士的费用。作出这些保证之后，他高声说：“使节大人们，看哪，我的身体在你们手中；请相信，无论你们命令什么，无论是去耶路撒冷、罗马，还是圣雅各之地［西班牙的孔波斯特拉 (Compostella)］，我都愿意服从。”主教们领他进入教堂，使他与教会和好。他在场的儿子向枢机阿尔伯特 (Albert) 保证履行父亲的承诺。这次补赎之后，接着便是他在坎特伯雷所受的极大屈辱。

两年后，即1174年7月12日，国王因接连的灾祸以及妻子和儿子们的叛乱而意气消沉，竟亲自前往贝克特墓前朝圣。当坎特伯雷的塔楼映入眼帘时，他便下马，像一个悔罪的朝圣者，身穿羊毛衬衣，赤着流血的双脚，步行穿过街道。他跪在大教堂的门廊，亲吻大主教倒下之处的圣石，随后在地下室的坟墓前俯伏，流泪呻吟，向主教们承认，自己对那些导致谋杀的轻率言语深感悔恨。伦敦主教吉尔伯特·福利奥特 (Gilbert Foliot) 曾是贝克特的对手与仇敌，此时向修士及旁观者宣布，国王已经悔罪，打算恢复教会的权利和财产，并每年赐给修道院四十马克，使殉道者墓前的灯长明不熄。国王把头和肩膀伏在墓上，接受屈辱的鞭笞；每位主教和修道院院长各打他五鞭，八十名修士每人各打三鞭。在得到完全赦免之后，他整夜伏在地下室光秃的地面上，流泪祈祷，恳求那位他曾在地上逼迫、如今已在天上被封为圣徒的人饶恕他。

历史上没有记载过国王在教士面前遭受比这更深的屈辱。狄奥多西 (Theodosius) 向安布罗斯 (Ambrose) 的屈服、埃德加 (Edgar) 向邓斯坦 (Dunstan) 的屈服、巴巴罗萨 (Barbarossa) 向亚历山大的屈服，甚至卡诺莎 (Canossa) 的那一幕，与之相比都黯然失色。

殉道五十年之后，贝克特的遗骸以异常隆重的仪式，从地下室的坟墓迁到重建的坎特伯雷大教堂内那座昂贵的贝克特圣龛中；圣龛金光灿烂，珠宝辉煌（1220年）。从此，那绵延不绝的朝圣活动开始达到极大的规模；在三百多年间，坎特伯雷成为西方基督教世界仅次于耶路撒冷和罗马的最大朝圣胜地。前来的人数超过了意大利的洛雷托 (Loreto) 和瑞士的艾因西德伦 (Einsiedeln)。1420年，在坎特伯雷登记的朝圣者竟不下十万人。男女信众从英格兰、苏格兰、威尔士、爱尔兰各地，从法国和遥远的北方，涌向这座圣龛：有教士、修士、王公、骑士、学者、律师、商人、工匠和农民。从亨利二世到亨利八世，英国国王几乎无一不曾出于虔诚或政治考量，向这位圣徒致敬。最后一批显赫的访客中，有圣保罗座堂主任牧师约翰·科利特 (John Colet) 和伊拉斯谟 (Erasmus)，他们于1511至1513年间一同参拜圣龛；还有亨利八世与皇帝查理五世 (Charles V)，他们参加了1520年最后一次禧年庆典。朝圣者获赐全大赦。有些人在他殉道的12月前来，更多的人则选择迁移遗骸的7月。每隔五十年，便举行一次为期十五天的禧年庆典来纪念他。这样的禧年共举行过六次，即1270、1320、1370、1420、1470和1520年。献给圣托马斯的供物，超过了献给其他任何圣徒、甚至圣母的供物。

英国诗歌之父杰弗里·乔叟 (Geoffrey Chaucer) 生活在贝克特殉道两个世纪之后，他在《坎特伯雷故事集》 (Canterbury Tales) 中使这些朝圣之旅流芳千古，并为我们留下了对当时英国社会最出色的描绘。

朝圣活动既促进了虔敬与社会交往，也助长了迷信、游惰、轻浮和败德，并激起许多严肃而注重属灵生活之人的道德义愤。

对圣托马斯的这种迷信性偶像崇拜，一直延续到宗教改革时期，才被粗暴却永久地摧毁。亨利八世传唤贝克特出庭，答辩叛国与叛乱的指控。案件在威斯敏斯特正式审理。他的罪行被判定成立；1538年6月10日，圣托马斯被定罪为“背叛其君主的叛逆和卖国者”。坎特伯雷那座富丽的圣龛遭到洗劫，黄金和珠宝装入两个坚固的大箱中运走，其余财宝则装满了二十六辆车。珠宝落入亨利八世手中，其中最贵重的一颗钻石“法兰西王宝” (Regale of France)，被他镶在拇指所戴的戒指上；后来，它又在他那信仰偏执的女儿玛丽女王 (Queen Mary) 的金“项圈”上闪耀。一道王室公告解释了贝克特死亡的原因和经过，以及褫夺其尊位的理由。一切奉他之名举行的节庆、礼仪和祈祷都被禁止。他的圣龛原址直到今日仍然空着。

宗教改革开辟了道路，使人能够以更属灵的方式敬拜上帝，也能比托马斯·贝克特 (Thomas Becket) 生前和去世的时代更公正地评价他的美德与过失。他是教皇圣统制的英雄和殉道者，却不是《新约》所记载之纯正基督教的英雄和殉道者。对于他的大多数同胞，以及一般说英语的民众而言，他的名字始终是教士骄傲与妄自尊大的同义语，代表着对世俗政权权利的傲慢侵犯。对于英国高教会派 (High Churchmen) 中的某一类人来说，他却像后世的劳德 (Laud) 一样，仍是为祭司特权殉身的人，是无私捍卫教会受赋权利的斗士。他被杀的残暴，无人愿意否认。但这位高级教长的高傲自恃，也为激烈的愤慨和严厉的处置提供了充分的口实。教士的袍服或可暂时掩盖骄傲，甚至保护它免遭暴力，但骄傲迟早会得到应有的报应。贝克特最爱说的“须保全我所属圣职等级的尊荣”，其中所含的教长优越感，实在不能指望一位生性不甘屈从的国王忍受。

Calixtus II. was followed by Honorius II., whose rule of six years, 1124–1130, was an uneventful one. After his death a dangerous schism broke out between Innocent II., 1130–1143, and Anacletus II., 1130–1138, who represented two powerful Roman families, the Frangipani, or Breadmakers,(1) and the Pierleoni.

Innocent, formerly cardinal-legate of Urban II. and mediator of the Concordat of Worms, enjoyed the reputation of superior learning and piety, which even his opponents could not dispute. He had also the advantage of a prior election, but of doubtful legal validity, since it was effected only by a minority of cardinals, who met in great hurry in an unknown place to anticipate the rival candidate.(2)

Anacletus was a son of Pierleone, Petrus Leonis, and a grandson of Leo, a baptized Jewish banker, who had acquired great financial, social, and political influence under the Hildebrandian popes. A Jewish community with a few hundred members were tolerated in Trastevere and around the island of the Tiber as a monumental proof of the truth of Christianity, and furnished some of the best physicians and richest bankers, who helped the nobility and the popes in their financial troubles. Anacletus betrayed his Semitic origin in his physiognomy, and was inferior to Innocent in moral character; but he secured an election by a majority of cardinals and the support of the principal noble families and the Roman community. With the help of the Normans, he took possession of Rome, banished his opponent, deposed the hostile cardinals, and filled the college with his friends.

Innocent was obliged to flee to France, and received there the powerful support of Peter of Cluny and Bernard of Clairvaux, the greatest monks and oracles of their age. He was acknowledged as the legitimate pope by all the monastic orders and by the kings of France and England.

Lothaire II. (III.) of Saxony, 1125–1137, to whom both parties appealed, decided for Innocent, led him and St. Bernard to Rome by armed force, and received in turn from the pope the imperial crown, June 4, 1133.

But after Lothaire’s departure, Anacletus regained possession of Rome, with the help of the Norman duke, Roger, and the party of the rival emperor, Conrad III. He made Roger II. king of Sicily, and thus helped to found a kingdom which lasted seven hundred and thirty years, till it was absorbed in the kingdom of Italy, 1860. Innocent retired to Pisa (1135). Lothaire made a second expedition to Italy and defeated Roger II. Bernard again appeared at Rome and succeeded in strengthening Innocent’s position. At this juncture Anacletus died, 1138. The healing of the schism was solemnly announced at the Second Lateran Council, 1139. War soon after broke out between Innocent and Roger, and Innocent was taken prisoner. On his release he confirmed Roger as king of Sicily. Lothaire had returned to Germany to die, 1137. Innocent had granted to him the territories of Matilda for an annual payment. On this transaction later popes based the claim that the emperor was a papal vassal.

After the short pontificates of Coelestin II., 1143–1144, and Lucius II., 1144–1145, Eugene III., a pupil and friend of St. Bernard, was elected, Feb. 15, 1145, and ruled till July 8, 1153. He wore the rough shirt of the monks of Citeaux under the purple. He had to flee from Rome, owing to the disturbances of Arnold of Brescia, and spent most of his time in exile. During his pontificate, Edessa was lost and the second crusade undertaken. Eugene has his chief interest from his connection with St. Bernard, his wise and loyal counsellor, who addressed to him his famous treatise on the papacy, the de consideratione.(3)

During the pontificates of Innocent II., Eugene III., and Adrian IV. occurred the interesting episode of Arnold of Brescia, an unsuccessful ecclesiastical and political agitator, who protested against the secularization of the Church, and tried to restore it to apostolic poverty and apostolic purity. These two ideas were closely connected in his mind. He proclaimed the principle that the Church and the clergy, as well as the monks, should be without any temporal possessions, like Christ and the Apostles, and live from the tithes and the voluntary offerings of the people. Their calling is purely spiritual. All the things of this earth belong to the laity and the civil government.

He practised what he taught, and begged his daily bread from house to house. He was a monk of severe ascetic piety, enthusiastic temper, popular eloquence, well versed in the Scriptures, restless, radical, and fearless.(4) He agreed with the Catholic orthodoxy, except on the doctrines of the eucharist and infant baptism; but his views on these sacraments are not known.(5)

With this ecclesiastical scheme he combined a political one. He identified himself with the movement of the Romans to emancipate themselves from the papal authority, and to restore the ancient republic. By giving all earthly power to the laity, he secured the favor of the laity, but lost the influence of the clergy. It was the political complication which caused his ruin.

Arnold was a native of Brescia in Lombardy, and an ordained reader in the Church. He was a pupil of Abaelard, and called armor-bearer to this Goliath.(6) He sympathized with his spirit of independence and hostility to Church authority, and may have been influenced also (as Neander assumes) by the ethical principles of that magnetic teacher. He certainly, at a later period, sided with him against St. Bernard, who became his bitter enemy. But with the exception of the common opposition to the hierarchy, they differed very widely. Abaelard was a philosopher, Arnold, a politician; Abaelard, a speculative thinker, Arnold, a practical preacher; Abaelard, a rationalist, Arnold, an enthusiast. The former undermined the traditional orthodoxy, the latter attacked the morals of the clergy and the temporal power of the Church. Arnold was far below Abaelard in intellectual endowment, but far more dangerous in the practical drift of his teaching, which tended to pauperize the Church and to revolutionize society. Baronius calls him "the father of political heresies."

In his ascetic zeal for the moral reform of the clergy, Arnold was in sympathy with the Hildebrandian party, but in his views of the temporal power of the pope, he went to the opposite extreme. Hildebrand aimed at the theocratic supremacy of the Church over the State; Arnold sought the welfare of the Church in her complete separation from the State and of the clerical office from secular entanglements. Pascal II., we may say, had prepared the way for this theory when he was willing to sacrifice the investiture to the emperor. The Hildebrandian reform had nearly passed away, and the old corruptions reappeared. The temporal power of the Church promoted the worldliness of the clergy. The author of the Historia Pontificalis says that Arnold’s doctrine agreed with the Gospel, but stood in crying contrast with the actual condition of things. St. Bernard, his opponent, was as much opposed as he to the splendor and luxury of bishops, the secular cares of the popes, and expressed a wish that he might see the day when "the Church, as in olden times, should cast her net for souls, and not for money."(7) All the monastic orders protested against the worldliness of the Church, and realized the principle of apostolic poverty within the wall of convents. But Arnold extended it to the secular clergy as well, and even went so far as to make poverty a condition of salvation for priests and monks.(8)

Arnold’s sermons gained great popular applause in Lombardy, and caused bitter disputes between the people and the bishop of Brescia. He was charged before the Lateran Synod of 1139 with inciting the laity against the clergy, was deposed as a schismatic (not as a heretic), commanded to be silent, and was expelled from Italy.

He went again to France and was entangled in the controversy of Abaelard with Bernard. Pope Innocent condemned both Abaelard and Arnold to silence and seclusion in a convent, 1140. Abaelard, weary of strife and life, submitted and retired to the convent of Cluny, where two years later he died in peace.(9) But Arnold began in Paris a course of public lectures against the worldliness and immorality of the clergy. He exposed especially the avarice of the bishops. He also charged St. Bernard with unholy ambition and envy against scholars. Bernard called him a man whose speech was honey, whose doctrine was poison. At his request the king expelled Arnold from France.

Arnold fled to Zürich and was kindly received and protected by the papal legate, Cardinal Guido, his former fellow-student in Paris.(10) But Bernard pursued him even there and denounced him to the bishop of Constance.

After a few years of unknown exile, Arnold appeared in Rome as the leader of a political movement. Innocent II. had allowed him to return to Italy; Eugene III. had pardoned him on condition of his doing penance in the holy places of Rome. But after the flight of this pope to France, Arnold preached again the doctrine of apostolic poverty, called the popes and cardinals Pharisees and scribes, and their church a house of merchandise and den of robbers. He was protected by the Roman senate, and idolized by the people. The Romans had renounced the papal authority, expelled the pope, substituted a purely secular government after the ancient model, and invited Conrad III. to assume the rôle of Constantine I. or Justinian. They lost themselves in dreams of government. The tradition of the old Roman rule controlled the Middle Ages in various forms: it lived as a universal monarchy in the German Empire, as a universal theocracy in the papacy; as a short-lived republic in the Roman people. The modern Italians who oppose the temporal power of the pope are more sensible: they simply claim the natural right of the Italian people to govern themselves, and they confine the dominion of Rome to Italy.

Arnold stepped out of the ecclesiastical into the political sphere, and surrounded the new republic with the halo of religion. He preached in his monastic gown, on the ruins of the Capitol, to the patres conscripti, and advised them to rebuild the Capitol, and to restore the old order of senators and knights. His emaciated face gave him a ghost-like appearance and deepened the effect of his eloquence.

But the republican experiment failed. The people were at last forced into submission by the interdict of Pope Adrian IV. Arnold was banished from Rome, 1154, and soon afterwards hanged by order of Emperor Frederick I., who hated democracy and republicanism. His body was burnt and his ashes were thrown into the Tiber, 1155, lest his admirers should worship his bones.(11)

Arnold’s was a voice of protest against the secular aims of the papacy and the worldliness of the clergy which still has its hearers. "So obstinate is the ban of the Middle Ages under which Rome is still held," says Gregorovius, "that the soul of a heretic of the twelfth century has not yet found rest, but must still haunt Rome." The Catholic Bishop Hefele refused to class him among "real heretics."(12) In 1883 Brescia raised a monument to its distinguished son.

The Arnoldists continued for some time to defend the doctrines of their master, and were declared heretics by a council of Verona, 1184, after which they disappeared.

But the idea of apostolic poverty and the opposition to the temporal power of the papacy reappeared among the Spirituals of the Franciscan order. Arnold’s political scheme of restoring the Roman republic was revived two hundred years later by Cola di Rienzi (1347), but with no better success; for Rienzi was murdered, his body burnt, and the ashes were scattered to the winds (1354).

With Conrad III. the powerful family of the Hohenstaufen ascended the imperial throne and occupied it from 1138 till 1254. They derive the name from the family castle Hohenstaufen, on a hill in the Rough Alp near Göppingen in Swabia.(13) They were descended from a knight, Friedrich von Büren, in the eleventh century, and his son Friedrich von Staufen, a faithful adherent of Emperor Henry IV., who made him duke of Swabia (1079), and gave him his daughter Agnes in marriage. They were thus connected by blood with the antagonist of Pope Hildebrand, and identified with the cause of the Ghibellines against the Guelphs in their bloody feuds in Germany and Italy. Henry VI., 1190–1197, acquired by marriage the kingdom of Naples and Sicily. His son, Frederick II., raised his house to the top of its prosperity, but was in his culture and taste more an Italian than German prince, and spent most of his time in Italy.

The Hohenstaufen or Swabian emperors maintained the principle of imperialism, that is, the dignity and independence of the monarchy, as a divine institution, against papal sacerdotalism on the one hand, and against popular liberty on the other.

They made common cause with the popes, and served their purposes in the crusades: three of them, Conrad III., Frederick I., and Frederick II., undertook crusades against the Saracens; Conrad III. engaged in the second, which was a failure; Frederick I. perished in Syria; Frederick II. captured Jerusalem. The Hohenstaufen made also common cause with the popes against political and doctrinal dissent: Barbarossa sacrificed and punished by death Arnold of Brescia as a dangerous demagogue; and Frederick II., though probably himself an unbeliever, persecuted heretics.

But on the question of supremacy of power, the Hohenstaufen were always in secret or open war with the popes, and in the end were defeated. The conflict broke out under Frederick Barbarossa, who after long years of contention died at peace with the Church. It was continued by his grandson Frederick II. who died excommunicated and deposed from his throne by the papacy. The dynasty went out in tragic weakness in Conradin, the last male representative, who was beheaded on the charge of high treason, 1268. This conflict of the imperial house of the Hohenstaufen was more imposing than the conflict waged by Henry IV. with Gregory and his successors because of the higher plane on which it was fought and the greater ability of the secular antagonists engaged. Lasting more than one hundred years, it forms one of the most august spectacles of the Middle Ages, and furnishes some of the most dramatic scenes in which kings have ever figured. The historian Gregorovius has felt justified in saying that "this Titanic war of the Middle Ages filled and connected the centuries and formed the greatest spectacle of all ages."

After the fall of the Hohenstaufen, the German Empire maintained, till its death in 1806, a nominal connection with the papacy, but ceased to be the central political power of Europe, except in the period of the Reformation under Charles V., 1519–1558, when it was connected with the crowns of Austria, the Low Countries, and Spain, and the newly discovered lands of America, and when that mighty monarch, true to his Austrian and Spanish descent, retarded the Protestant movement for national independence and religious freedom. The new German Empire, founded on the ruins of the old and the defeat of France (1870), is ruled by a hereditary Protestant emperor.

§ 28. Adrian IV. and Frederick Barbarossa.

Lives of Hadrian in Muratori, Script. Rer. Ital. I. III.—Migne, vol. 188.—Otto of Freising.—William of Newburgh, 2 vols. London, 1856.—R. Raby: Pope Hadrian IV. Eugene III. was followed by Anastasius IV., whose rule lasted only sixteen months.

His successor was Nicolas Breakspear, the first and the only Englishman that has (thus far) worn the tiara. He was the son of a poor priest of St. Albans. He went to France in pursuit of bread and learning, became a monk, prior, and abbot of the convent of St. Rufus, between Arles and Avignon. He studied theology and canon law. Eugene III. made him cardinal-bishop of Albano, and sent him as legate to Norway and Sweden, where he organized the Church and brought it into closer contact with Rome.

He occupied the papal chair as Adrian IV., from 1154 to 1159, with great ability and energy. A beggar raised to the highest dignity in Christendom! The extremes of fortune met in this Englishman. Yet he felt happier in his poverty than in his power. He declared soon after his consecration that "the papal chair was full of thorns and the papal mantle full of holes and so heavy as to load down the strongest man." And after some experience in that high office, he said: "Is there a man in the world so miserable as a pope? I have found so much trouble in St. Peter’s chair that all the bitterness of my former life appears sweet in comparison."(14)

The Romans, under the lead of Arnold, requested him to resign all claim to temporal rule; but he refused, and after a bloody attack made by an Arnoldist upon one of the cardinals in the open street, he laid—for the first time in history—the interdict on the city. By this unbloody, yet awful and most effective, weapon, he enforced the submission of the people. He abolished the republican government, expelled Arnold and his adherents, and took possession of the Lateran.

At this time, Frederick I., called Barbarossa (Redbeard) by the Italians from the color of his beard, one of the bravest, strongest, and most despotic of German emperors,—the sleeper in Kyffhäuser,(15)— made, with a powerful army, his first expedition to Italy to receive the iron crown of royalty from the Lombards and the golden crown of empire from the pope (1154).

The pope demanded, as the first condition of his coronation, the surrender of Arnold. With this Barbarossa willingly complied and ordered the execution of the popular agitator. In his first interview with Adrian, he kissed the pope’s toe, but neglected the ceremony of holding the stirrup on descending from his palfrey. Adrian felt indignant and refused to give him the kiss of peace. When informed that this was an old custom, Barbarossa on the following day complied with it, but in an ambiguous way by holding the left stirrup instead of the right. He took forcible possession of Trastevere, and was solemnly invested, anointed, and crowned, according to the prescribed ritual, in St. Peter’s, amid the acclamations of the curia, the clergy, and the army (June 13, 1155). An insurrection of the Roman people was speedily suppressed, the emperor leading the charge into the rebel ranks. But on the next morning he retired with the pope to the Tiburtine hills. He was reluctantly compelled by the want of supplies and by rumors of rebellion in Lombardy to return with his army. The pope, shut out from Rome, without foreign or domestic ally, retired to Benevento, was besieged there by King William of Sicily (son and successor of Roger II.) and forced by desertion and famine to submit to the terms of the conqueror by investing him with the kingdom of Sicily, the duchy of Apulia, and the principality of Capua. This involved him in a controversy with the emperor, who regarded Apulia and Capua as parts of the empire. He protested against the divorce from his first, and the marriage to his second, wife, 1156.

To these occasions of offence Adrian added another which Frederick would not bear. It was evoked by the ill-treatment done by robbers to the archbishop of Lund on his way from Rome through Germany to his Scandinavian diocese.(16) Adrian spoke of Frederick’s empire as a benefice, beneficium, a word which meant either a fief or a gift. In either case the implication was offensive to the Germans, and they chose to interpret it as a claim that the emperor held his empire as a fief of the apostolic see. Two legates, rent by Adrian, attempted to soften down the meaning of the imprudent expression.

The pope was too much of a hierarch and Frederick too much of an emperor to live in peace. In 1158 Frederick led his army across the Alps to reduce Milan and other refractory Lombard cities to submission. Having accomplished this, he assembled a diet on the plain of Roncaglia, near Piacenza, which is memorable for the decision rendered by Bologna jurists, that the emperor held his empire by independent divine right and not by the will of the pope. This was the most decisive triumph the empire had won since the opening of the conflict with Henry IV. But the decision of professors of law did not change the policy of the papacy.

Adrian again gave offence by denying the emperor’s right to levy a tax for military purposes, fodrum, on estates claimed by the papacy and demanded that he should recognize the papal claim of feudal rights over the Matilda grant, Sardinia, Corsica, Ferrara, and the duchy of Spoleto. Frederick proudly retorted that instead of owing fealty to the pope, the popes owed fealty to the emperor, inasmuch as it was by the gift of the emperor Constantine that Pope Sylvester secured possession of Rome. A war of letters followed. Adrian was intending to punish his imperial foe with excommunication when he was struck down by death at Anagni. He was buried in St. Peter’s in an antique sarcophagus of red granite which is still shown. So ended the career of a man who by his moral character and personal attractions had lifted himself up from the condition of a child of a poor cleric to the supreme dignity of Christendom, and ventured to face the proudest monarch as his superior and to call the imperial crown a papal beneficium.(17)

This English pope, who laid the city of Rome under the interdict, which no Italian or German pope had dared to do, presented Ireland to the crown of England, on the ground that all the islands of the Christian world belong to the pope by virtue of Constantine’s donation. The curious bull Laudabiliter, encouraging Henry II. to invade and subjugate the land and giving it to him and to his heirs for a possession, may not be genuine, but the authorization was certainly made by Adrian as John of Salisbury, writing about 1159, attests, and it was renewed by Alexander III. and carried out, 1171.(18) The loyal sons of Ireland will hardly want to have a second trial of an English pope.

With Alexander III. (1159–1181) the conflict between Caesarism and sacerdotalism, which had begun under Adrian, assumed a more serious character. It was not a war for destruction, but for supremacy on the one hand and submission on the other. "Who shall be the greater?" that was the question. It was the old contention between Church and State under a new phase. Caesar and pope were alike Catholic Christians as far as they had any religion at all. They were indispensable to each other. The emperor or king needed a pope, as a kind of chief chaplain and father confessor for the control of the consciences of his subjects; the pope needed the secular arm of an emperor for the protection of the property and rights of the Church and the prosecution of heretics. The emperors elected anti-popes, and the popes supported rival emperors. It was the ambition of the Hohenstaufen to keep Germany and Italy united; it was the interest of the popes to keep them separated, and to foment division in Germany and in Italy, according to the maxim. "Divide et impera."

On the 7th of September, 1159, Cardinal Roland, the chancellor of the Roman curia and a distinguished canonist, ascended the papal chair as Alexander III. He had previously been professor at Bologna, and written the first work on the Decretum Gratiani. He had been created cardinal by Eugene III. He had once offended Barbarossa by the question: "From whom does the emperor receive his dignity if not from the pope?" He had also advised Adrian to excommunicate the emperor. He was a scholar, a statesman, and a vigorous champion of the Hildebrandian theocracy. He had an unusually long pontificate of twenty-one years, and is the most conspicuous pope between Gregory VII. and Innocent III. He had a checkered career of fortune and misfortune in a conflict with the emperor and four anti-popes; but he consistently adhered to his principles, and at last triumphed over his enemies by moral force and the material aid of the Normans in the south and the Lombards in the north.

The election of Roland by fourteen cardinals was immediately followed by the election of Cardinal Octavian of St. Cecilia, the imperial anti-pope, who called himself Victor IV., and at once took possession of the Vatican. Roland was consecrated at Ninfa, Octavian in the convent of Farfa. They were quartered in the Campagna, a few miles distant from each other, and published contradictory reports with charges of disgraceful violence at the election.(19)

The emperor, who was then besieging the city of Cremona, being appealed to by both parties (though with different feelings), and using a right exercised by Constantine, Theodosius, Justinian, Charlemagne, and Otto, summoned a council at Pavia to investigate and decide the case, 1160.(20) The rival popes were invited by messengers to appear in person. Octavian, who was always an imperialist, accepted the invitation. Roland distrusted the emperor, and protested against his right to call a council without his permission. He said that he honored him as a special defender of the Church above all other princes, but that God had placed the pope above kings.

The partisan council, which consisted chiefly of bishops from Germany and North Italy, after a grave debate, unanimously decided in favor of Octavian, and excommunicated Roland, Feb. 11, 1160. The emperor paid the customary honors to Victor IV., held his stirrup and kissed his toe. Alexander issued from Anagni a counter-excommunication against the anti-pope and the emperor, March 24, 1160. He thereby encouraged revolt in Lombardy and division in Germany. Another schism rent the Church.

The rival popes dispatched legates to all the courts of Europe. France, Spain, and England sided with Alexander. He took refuge in France for three years (1162–1165), and was received with enthusiasm. The kings of France and England, Louis VII. and Henry II., walked on either side of his horse, holding the bridle, and conducting him into the town of Courcy on the Loire. Germany, Hungary, Bohemia, Norway, and Sweden supported Victor. Italy was divided: Rome and Tuscany were under the power of the emperor; Sicily favored the Gregorian pope; the flourishing commercial and manufacturing cities of Lombardy were discontented with the despotic rule of Barbarossa, who was called the destroyer of cities. He put down the revolt with an iron hand; he razed Milan to the ground after a long and atrocious siege, scattered the population, and sent the venerated relics of the Magi to the cathedral of Cologne, March, 1162.

Victor IV. died in April, 1164. Pascal III. was elected his successor without regard to the canonical rules. At the request of the emperor, he canonized Charles the Great (1165).

Alexander III. put himself at the head of the Lombard league against the emperor; city after city declared itself for him. In September, 1165, he returned to Italy with the help of Sicily, and French and English gold, and took possession of Rome.

In November, 1166, Frederick crossed the Alps a fourth time, with a strong army, marched to Rome, captured the Leonine city, put Pascal III. in possession of St. Peter’s, and was crowned again, with Beatrice, Aug. 1, 1167. Alexander defended the city on the other side of the Tiber, but soon withdrew to Benevento. The emperor, victorious over armies, found a more formidable enemy in the Roman fever, which made fearful ravages among his bishops, noblemen, and soldiers. He lost in a few weeks his bravest knights and two thousand men by the plague. He broke up his camp in great haste, and marched to Pavia (September, 1167).(21) He found all Lombardy in league against him, and recrossed the Alps for safety, alone and almost a fugitive, but with unbroken spirit and a determination to return.

The second anti-pope died, Sept. 20, 1168, and with him the power of the schism collapsed. Calixtus III. was elected his successor, but he was a mere shadow, 1168–1178.(22)

Barbarossa undertook a fifth campaign to Italy in 1174. He destroyed Susa, and, descending through Piedmont, besieged the new city of Alessandria, which was named in honor of Alexander III., and strongly fortified. Here he found determined resistance. His forces were weakened by a severe winter. He was forsaken by his strongest ally, the Saxon duke, Henry the Lion. He fought a pitched battle against the Lombards, near Legnano, May 29, 1176. He rushed, as usual, into the thickest of the fight, but was defeated after terrible slaughter, and lost his shield, banner, cross, lance, and coffers of silver and gold. He retired with the remnant of his army to Pavia. He was left without a single ally, and threatened in Germany by the dangerous rivalry of Henry the Lion. He now took serious steps towards a reconciliation with Alexander, the spiritual head of his enemies.

The emperor sent Archbishop Christian of Mainz (his chancellor, ablest general, and diplomat), Archbishop Wichmann of Magdeburg, Bishop Conrad of Worms, and Protonotary Wortwin to Anagni, with full powers to treat with the pope (October, 1176). Alexander received the commissioners with marked respect, and in private conferences, lasting over a fortnight, he arranged with them the preliminary terms of peace, which were to be ratified at Venice during a personal interview between him and the emperor.

The pope, provided with a safe-conduct by the emperor, left Anagni on Christmas, 1176, in company with his cardinals and the two commissioners of the kingdom of Sicily, Archbishop Romuald of Salerno and Count Roger of Andria, and arrived at Venice, March 24, 1177. The emperor tarried at Chioggia, near Venice, till July 23. The peace negotiations between the pope and the imperial commissioners began in May and lasted till July. They were conducted on the basis of the previous negotiations in Anagni.

§ 30. The Peace of Venice. 1177.

The negotiations resulted in the Peace of Venice, which was embodied in twenty-eight articles.(23) Alexander was acknowledged as legitimate pope. Calixtus, the anti-pope, was remanded to an abbey, while his cardinals were reduced to the positions they had occupied before their appointment to the curia. Beatrice was acknowledged as Frederick’s legal wife, and his son Henry as king of the Romans. Rome and the patrimonium were restored to the pope, and Spoleto, the Romagna, and Ancona were recognized as a part of the empire.

The peace was ratified by one of the most solemn congresses of the Middle Ages. Absolved from the ban, and after eighteen years of conflict, the emperor met the pope in front of St. Mark’s, July 24, 1177. A vast multitude filled the public square. The pope in his pontifical dress sitting upon a throne in front of the portal of the cathedral must have had mingled with his feelings of satisfaction reminiscences of his painful fortunes since the time he was elected to the tiara. Cardinals, archbishops, bishops, and other dignitaries occupied lower seats according to their rank.

The emperor, on arriving in the magnificent gondola of the doge, with a train of prelates and nobles, was received by a procession of priests with banners and crosses, and the shouts of the people. He slowly proceeded to the cathedral. Overcome with feelings of reverence for the venerable pope, he cast off his mantle, bowed, and fell at his feet.(24) Alexander, in tears, raised him up,(25) and gave him the kiss of peace and his benediction. Thousands of voices responded by singing the Te Deum.(26)

Then the emperor, taking the hand of the pope, walked with him and the doge into the church, made rich offerings at the altar, bent his knees, and received again the apostolic benediction.

On the next day (the 25th), being the feast of St. James, the pope, at the emperor’s request, celebrated high mass, and preached a sermon which he ordered the patriarch of Aquileia to translate at once into German. The emperor accompanied him from the altar to the door, and paid him the customary homage of holding the stirrup.(27) He offered to conduct his palfrey by the bridle across the piazza to the bark; but the pope dispensed with this menial service of a groom, taking the will for the deed, and gave him again his benediction.

This is the authentic account of contemporary writers and eye-witnesses. They make no mention of the story that the emperor said to the pope, "I do this homage to Peter, not to thee," and that the pope quickly replied, "To Peter and to me."

The hierarchical imagination has represented this interview as a second Canossa. In Venetian pictures the pope is seen seated on a throne, and planting his foot on the neck of the prostrate emperor, with the words of Ps. 91:13: —

"Thou shalt tread upon the lion and the adder:

The young lion and the serpent shalt thou trample under feet."(28)

There is as much difference between the scenes of Venice and Canossa as there is between the characters of Barbarossa and Henry IV. Barbarossa was far superior, morally as well as intellectually, to his Salian predecessor, and commanded the respect of his enemies, even in his defeat. He maintained his dignity and honorably kept his word.

Delegates and letters were sent to all parts of Christendom with the glad tidings of peace. The emperor left Venice toward the end of September for Germany by a roundabout way, and the pope for Anagni on the 15th of October. After an exile of ten years, Alexander made a triumphal entry into Rome, March 12, 1178.

He convened, according to previous agreement with the emperor, a synod to ratify the pacification of Christendom, and to remove certain evils which had multiplied during the schism. The Third Lateran or the Eleventh Oecumenical Council was held in the Constantinian Basilica at Rome during Lent, 1179. It numbered about three hundred bishops, besides many abbots and other dignitaries,(29) and exhibited the Roman hierarchy in its glory, though it was eclipsed afterwards by the Fourth Lateran Council of 1215. The details of the transactions are unknown, except twenty-seven chapters which were adopted in the third and last session.

The council, in order to prevent rival elections, placed the election of popes exclusively in the hands of cardinals, to be decided by a majority of two-thirds, and threatened with excommunication and deposition any one who should dare to accept an election by a smaller number of votes.(30) The ordinations of the anti-popes (Octavian, Guido, and John of Struma) were declared invalid. No one was to be elected bishop who was not at least thirty years of age and of legitimate birth. To check the extravagance of prelates on their visitation journeys, the archbishops were limited to forty or fifty horses on those occasions, the cardinals to twenty-five, the bishops to twenty or thirty, the archdeacons to five or seven. Ordained clergymen must dismiss their concubines, or forfeit their benefices. Unnatural licentiousness was to be punished by expulsion from the priesthood and confinement in a convent. The council prepared the way for a crusade against the heretics in the South of France, and promised to those who should engage in it the same plenary indulgence for two years as had been granted to the crusaders against the Moslems.

Soon after the synod, Alexander was again driven into exile by the Roman republic. He died at Cività Castellana, Aug. 30, 1181, having reigned longer than any pope before or after him, except Sylvester I., 314–385, Adrian I., 772–795, Pius VII., 1800–1823, Pius IX., 1846–1878, and Leo XIII., 1878–1903. When Alexander’s remains were being carried to Rome for burial, the populace insulted his memory by pelting the coffin with stones and mud.(31) Alexander had with signal constancy and devotion to the Gregorian principles maintained the conflict with Barbarossa. He supported Thomas à Becket in his memorable conflict with Henry II. In 1181 he laid the interdict upon Scotland because of the refusal of its king, William, to acknowledge the canonical election of John to the see of St. Andrews. Upon Louis VII. of France he conferred the Red Rose for the support he had received from that sovereign in the days of his early exile. He presided over the Third Lateran Council and prepared the way for the crusade against the Cathari and Albigenses.

His aged and feeble successor, Lucius III., was elected, Sept. 1, 1181, by the cardinals alone. The Romans, deprived of their former share in the election, treated him with barbarous cruelty; they captured twenty or twenty-six of his partisans at Tusculum, blinded them, except one, crowned them with paper mitres inscribed with the names of cardinals, mounted them on asses, and forced the priest whom they had spared to lead them in this condition to "Lucius, the wicked simoniac." He died in exile at Verona where he held an important synod.

It is a remarkable fact that some of the greatest popes—as Gregory VII., Urban II., Innocent II., Eugene III., Adrian IV., Alexander III., and three of his successors—could not secure the loyalty of their own subjects, and were besieged in Rome or compelled to flee. Adrian IV. said to his countryman and friend, John of Salisbury, "Rome is not the mother, but the stepmother of the Churches." The Romans were always fluctuating between memories of the old republic and memories of the empire; now setting up a consul, a senator, a tribune; now welcoming the German emperor as the true Augustus Caesar; now loyal to the pope, now driving him into exile, and ever selling themselves to the highest bidder. The papal court was very consistent in its principles and aims, but as to the choice of means for its end it was subject to the same charge of avarice and venality, whether at Rome or in exile. Even Thomas Becket, the staunchest adherent of Alexander III., indignantly rebuked the cardinals for their love of gold.

Emperor Frederick survived his great rival nearly ten years, and died by drowning in a little river of Asia Minor, 1190, while marching on the third crusade.

Barbarossa was a man of middle size, bright countenance, fair complexion, yellow hair and reddish beard, a kind friend and placable enemy, strictly just, though often too severe, liberal in almsgiving, attentive to his religious duties, happy in his second marriage, of the noblest type of mediaeval chivalry, the greatest sovereign of the twelfth century, a hero in fact and a hero in romance.(32) He came into Italy with the sword of Germany in one hand and the Justinian code in the other, but failed in subduing the political independence of the Lombard cities, and in his contest with the spiritual power of Alexander. The German imagination has cherished his memory in song and story, placing him next in rank to Charles the Great among the Roman emperors, exaggerating his virtues, condoning his faults, which were those of his age, and hoping for his return to restore the unity and power of Germany.

During the pontificate of Alexander III., the papal hierarchy achieved an earlier and greater triumph over the king of England than over the emperor of Germany.

Thomas Becket, or Thomas à Becket, or St. Thomas of Canterbury, is, next to Alexander and Barbarossa, the most prominent historical figure in the twelfth century, and fills a chapter of thrilling interest in the history of England. He resumed the conflict of Anselm with the crown, and by his martyrdom became the most popular saint of the later Middle Ages.

The materials for his history, from his birth in London to his murder in his own cathedral by four knights of the royal household, are abundant. We have six or seven contemporary biographies, besides fragments, legends, and "Passions," state papers, private letters, and a correspondence extending over the whole Latin Church. But his life is surrounded by a mist of romantic legends and theological controversies. He had extravagant admirers, like Herbert of Bosham, and fierce opponents, like Gilbert Foliot, in his own day; and modern biographers still differ in the estimate of his character, according to their creed and their views on the question of Church and State, some regarding him as a hero and a saint, others as a hypocrite and a traitor. We must judge him from the standpoint of the twelfth century.

Becket was born in London, Dec. 21, 1118, during the reign of Henry I. He was the son of Gilbert Becket, a merchant in Cheapside, originally from Rouen, and of Matilda or Rose, a native of Caen in Normandy.(33)

In the later legend his father appears as a gallant crusader and his mother as a Saracen princess, who met in the East and fell in love with each other. Matilda helped Gilbert to escape from captivity, and then followed him alone to England. Knowing only two English words, "London" and "Gilbert," she wandered through the streets of the city, till at last she found her beloved in Cheapside as by a miracle, was baptized and married to him in St. Paul’s with great splendor. She had dreams of the future greatness and elevation of her infant son to the see of Canterbury.

Becket was educated at Merton Abbey in Surrey and in the schools of London. At a later period he attended the universities of Paris, Bologna, and Auxerre, and studied there chiefly civil and canon law, without attaining to special eminence in learning. He was not a scholar, but a statesman and an ecclesiastic.

He made his mark in the world and the Church by the magnetism of his personality. He was very handsome, of tall, commanding presence, accomplished, brilliant, affable, cheerful in discourse, ready and eloquent in debate, fond of hunting and hawking, and a proficient in all the sports of a mediaeval cavalier. He could storm the strongest castle and unhorse the stoutest knight.

Archbishop Theobald of Canterbury, 1139–1161, took him into his service, 1142; sent him to Bologna, where Gratian then taught canon law; employed him in delicate missions with the papal court; made him archdeacon (1154), and bestowed upon him other profitable benefices, as the provostship of Beverly, a number of churches, and several prebends. When charged, as archbishop, with ingratitude to the king, who had raised him from "poverty," he proudly referred to this accumulation of preferments, and made no attempt to abolish the crying evil of plurality, which continued till the Reformation. Many a prosperous ecclesiastic regarded his parishes simply as sources of income, and discharged the duties by proxy through ignorant and ill-paid priests.

King Henry II., 1154–1189, in the second year of his reign, raised Becket, then only thirty-seven years of age, at Theobald’s instance, to the chancellorship of England. The chancellor was the highest civil dignitary, and held the custody of nearly all the royal grants and favors, including vacant bishoprics, abbacies, chaplaincies, and other ecclesiastical benefices.

Henry, the first of the proud Plantagenets, was an able, stirring, and energetic monarch. He kept on his feet from morning till evening, and rarely sat down. He introduced a reign of law and severe justice after the lawless violence and anarchy which had disturbed the reign of the unfortunate Stephen.(34) But he was passionate, vindictive, and licentious. He had frequent fits of rage, during which he behaved like a madman. He was the most powerful sovereign in Western Europe. His continental dominions were more extensive than those of the king of France, and embraced Maine and Normandy, Anjou and Aquitaine, reaching from Flanders to the foot of the Pyrenees. He afterwards (1171) added Ireland by conquest, with the authority of Popes Adrian IV. and Alexander III. His marriage to Queen Eleanor of Aquitaine, who had been divorced for infidelity from King Louis VII. of France, enriched his realm, but involved him in protracted wars with France and in domestic troubles. Eleanor was jealous of her rivals,(35) incited her sons, Geoffrey and Richard, to rebel against their father, was imprisoned in 1173, and released after Henry’s death in 1189 by his successor, Richard I., Coeur de Lion, who made her regent on his departure for the Holy Land. She afterwards retired to the abbey of Fontevrault, and died about 1203.

Becket occupied the chancellorship for seven years (1155–1162). He aided the king in the restoration of order and peace. He improved the administration of justice. He was vigorous and impartial, and preferred the interests of the crown to those of the clergy, yet without being hostile to the Church. He was thoroughly loyal to the king, and served him as faithfully as he had served Theobald, and as he afterwards served the pope. Thorough devotion to official duty characterized him in all the stations of his career.

He gave to his high office a prominence and splendor which it never had before. He was as magnificent and omnipotent as Wolsey under Henry VIII. He was king in fact, though not in name, and acted as regent during Henry’s frequent absences on the Continent. He dressed after the best fashion, surrounded himself with a brilliant retinue of a hundred and forty knights, exercised a prodigal hospitality, and spent enormous sums upon his household and public festivities, using in part the income of his various ecclesiastical benefices, which he retained without a scruple. He presided at royal banquets in Westminster Hall. His tables were adorned with vessels of gold, with the most delicate and sumptuous food, and with wine of the choicest vintage. He superintended the training of English and foreign nobles, and of the young Prince Henry. He was the favorite of the king, the army, the nobility, the clergy, and the people.

The chancellor negotiated in person a matrimonial alliance (three years before it was consummated) between the heir of the crown (then a boy of seven years) and a daughter of the king of France (a little lady of three). He took with him on that mission two hundred knights, priests, standard-bearers, all festively arrayed in new attire, twenty-four changes of raiment, all kinds of dogs and birds for field sports, eight wagons, each drawn by five horses, each horse in charge of a stout young man dressed in a new tunic. Coffers and chests contained the chancellor’s money and presents. One horse, which preceded all the rest, carried the holy vessels of his chapel, the holy books, and the ornaments of the altar. The Frenchmen, seeing this train, exclaimed, "How wonderful must be the king of England, whose chancellor travels in such state!" In Paris he freely distributed his gold and silver plate and changes of raiment,—to one a robe, to another a furred cloak, to a third a pelisse, to a fourth a war-horse. He gained his object and universal popularity.

When, notwithstanding his efforts to maintain peace, war broke out between France and England, the chancellor was the bravest warrior at the head of seven hundred knights, whom he had enlisted at his own expense, and he offered to lead the storming party at the siege of Toulouse, where King Louis was shut up; but the scruples of Henry prevented him from offering violence to the king of France. He afterwards took three castles which were deemed impregnable, and returned triumphant to England. One of his eulogists, Edward Grim, reports to his credit: "Who can recount the carnage, the desolation, which he made at the head of a strong body of soldiers? He attacked castles, razed towns and cities to the ground, burned down houses and farms without a touch of pity, and never showed the slightest mercy to any one who rose in insurrection against his master’s authority." Such cruelty was quite compatible with mediaeval conceptions of piety and charity, as the history of the crusades shows.

Becket was made for the court and the camp. Yet, though his life was purely secular, it was not immoral. He joined the king in his diversions, but not in his debaucheries. Being in deacon’s orders, he was debarred from marriage, but preserved his chastity at a profligate court. This point is especially mentioned to his credit; for chastity was a rare virtue in the Middle Ages.

All together, his public life as chancellor was honorable and brilliant, and secures him a place among the distinguished statesmen of England. But a still more important career awaited him.(36)

§ 32. The Archbishop and the King.

Compare §§ 22–24 (pp. 80 sqq.).

A year after the death of Theobald, April 18, 1161, Becket was appointed by the king archbishop of Canterbury. He accepted reluctantly, and warned the king, with a smile, that he would lose a servant and a friend.(37) The learned and energetic Bishop Gilbert Foliot of Hereford (afterwards of London) remarked sarcastically, perhaps from disappointed ambition, that "the king had wrought a miracle in turning a layman into an archbishop, and a soldier into a saint."

Becket was ordained priest on the Saturday after Pentecost, and consecrated archbishop on the following day with great magnificence in Westminster Abbey, June 3, 1162. His first act was to appoint the Sunday after Whitsunday as a festival of the Holy Trinity in the Church of England. He acknowledged Alexander III. as the rightful pope, and received from him the pallium through his friend, John of Salisbury.

He was the first native Englishman who occupied the seat of the primate since the Norman Conquest; for Lanfranc and Anselm were Italians; Ralph of Escures, William Of Corbeuil, and Theobald of Bec were Normans or Frenchmen. There is, however, no ground for the misleading theory of Thierry that Becket asserted the cause of the Saxon against the Norman. His contest with the king was not a contest between two nationalities, but between Church and State. He took the same position on this question as his Norman predecessors, only with more zeal and energy. He was a thorough Englishman. The two nations had at that time, by intermarriage, social and commercial intercourse, pretty well coalesced, at least among the middle classes, to which he belonged.(38)

With the change of office, Becket underwent a radical and almost sudden transformation. The foremost champion of kingcraft became the foremost champion of priestcraft; the most devoted friend of the king, his most dangerous rival and enemy; the brilliant chancellor, an austere and squalid monk. He exchanged the showy court dress for haircloth infested with vermin, fed on roots, and drank nauseous water. He daily washed, with proud humility and ostentatious charity, the feet of thirteen dirty beggars, and gave each of them four pieces of silver. He doubled the charities of Theobald, as Theobald had doubled the charities of his predecessor. He wandered alone in his cloister, shedding tears of repentance for past sins, frequently inflicted stripes on his naked back, and spent much time in prayer and reading of the Scriptures. He successfully strove to realize the ideal of a mediaeval bishop, which combines the loftiest ecclesiastical pretensions with personal humility, profuse charity, and ascetic self-mortification. He was no hypocrite, but his sanctity, viewed from the biblical and Protestant standpoint, was artificial and unnatural.

His relation to the king was that of the pope to the emperor. Yea, we may say, as he had outkinged the king as chancellor, so he outpoped the pope as archbishop. He censured the pope for his temporizing policy. He wielded the spiritual sword against Henry with the same gallantry with which he had wielded the temporal sword for him. He took up the cause of Anselm against William Rufus, and of Gregory VII. against Henry IV., but with this great difference, that he was not zealous for a moral reformation of the Church and the clergy, like Hildebrand and Anselm, but only for the temporal power of the Church and the rights and immunities of the clergy. He made no attempt to remove the scandal of pluralities of which he had himself been guilty as archdeacon and chancellor, and did not rebuke Henry for his many sins against God, but only for his sins against the supremacy of the hierarchy.

The new archbishop was summoned by Pope Alexander III. to a council at Tours in France, and was received with unusual distinction (May, 1163). The council consisted of seventeen cardinals, a hundred and twenty-four bishops, four hundred and fourteen abbots; the pope presided in person; Becket sat at his right, Roger of York at his left. Arnolf of Lisieux in Normandy preached the opening sermon on the unity and freedom of the Church, which were the burning questions of the day. The council unanimously acknowledged the claims of Alexander, asserted the rights and privileges of the clergy, and severely condemned all encroachments on the property of the Church.

This was the point which kindled the controversy between the sceptre and the crozier in England. The dignity of the crown was the sole aim of the king; the dignity of the Church was the sole aim of the archbishop. The first rupture occurred over the question of secular taxation.

Henry determined to transfer the customary payment of two shillings on every hide of land to his own exchequer. Becket opposed the enrolment of the decree on the ground that the tax was voluntary, not of right. Henry protested, in a fit of passion, "By the eyes of God, it shall be enrolled!" Becket replied, "By the eyes of God, by which you swear, it shall never be levied on my lands while I live!"

Another cause of dispute was the jurisdiction of the ecclesiastical courts. The king demanded that all clerics accused of gross misdemeanors be tried by the civil court. A certain clerk, Philip of Broi, had been acquitted of murder in the bishop’s court. The king was indignant, but Philip refused to plead in the civil court. The matter was taken up by the archbishop, but a light sentence imposed.

The king summoned a Parliament at Westminster, and demanded in the name of equal justice, and in accordance with "ancient customs" (of the Norman kings), that all clerks accused of heinous crimes should be immediately degraded, and be dealt with according to law, instead of being shielded by their office. This was contrary to the right of the priest to be tried only in the court of his bishop, where flagellation, imprisonment, and degradation might be awarded, but not capital punishment.

Becket and the bishops agreed that the king’s demand was an infringement of the canon law and argued the case from Scripture. Joab, and Abiathar the priest, were guilty of putting Adonijah to death. Joab was punished, but the priest suffered no other punishment than deposition from office. Nahum 1:9 was quoted as against a double tribunal for clerks. According to the Septuagint version, this passage declares that God does not give two judgments in the same case.

The king hastily broke up the Parliament, deprived Becket of the custody of the royal castles, and of the education of his son. The bishops advised the archbishop to yield; at first he refused, though an angel from heaven should counsel such weakness; but at last he made a concession to the king at Woodstock, and promised to obey in good faith the customs of the realm. He yielded at the persuasion of the pope’s almoner, Philip de Eleeomosyna, who was bribed by English gold.(39)

The king summoned a great council of the realm to Clarendon, a royal palace a few miles from Salisbury, for the ratification of the concession (Jan. 25, 1164). The two archbishops, twelve bishops, and thirty-nine lay-barons were present. Sixteen famous statutes were enacted, under the name of The Clarendon Constitutions, as laws of England. They are as follows:(40)—

THE CONSTITUTIONS OF CLARENDON.

I. Of the advowson and presentation (de advocatione et presentatione) to churches: if any dispute shall arise between laics, or between clerks and laics, or between clerks, let it be tried and decided in the court of our lord the king.

  1. Churches in the king’s fee (de feudo domini Regis) shall not be given in perpetuity without his consent and license.

  2. Clerks accused of any crime shall be summoned by the king’s justiciaries into the king’s court to answer there for whatever the king’s court shall determine they ought to answer there; and in the ecclesiastical court, for whatever it shall be determined that they ought to answer there; yet so that the king’s justiciaries shall send into the court of holy Church to see in what way the matter shall there be handled; and if the clerk shall confess or be convicted, the Church for the future shall not protect him.(41)

  3. No archbishop, bishop, or other exalted person shall leave the kingdom without the king’s license; and if they wish to leave it, the king shall be empowered, if he pleases, to take security from them, that they will do no harm to the king or kingdom, either in going or remaining, or in returning.

V. Persons excommunicated are not to give bail, ad remanentiam, nor to make oath, but only to give bail and pledge that they will stand by the judgment of the Church where they are absolved.

  1. Laics shall not be accused, save by certain and legal accusers and witnesses in presence of the bishop, so that the archdeacon may not lose his rights, or anything which accrues to him therefrom. And if those who are arraigned are such that no one is willing or dares to accuse them, the sheriff, on demand from the bishop, shall cause twelve loyal men of the village to swear before the bishop that they will declare the truth in that matter according to their conscience.

  2. No one who holds of the king in chief, nor any of his domestic servants, shall be excommunicated, nor his lands be put under an interdict, until the king shall be consulted, if he is in the kingdom; or, if he is abroad, his justiciary, that he may do what is right in that matter, and so that whatever belongs to the king’s court may therein be settled, and the same on the other hand of the ecclesiastical court.

  3. Appeals, if they arise, must be made from the archdeacon to the bishop, and from the bishop to the archbishop; and if the archbishop shall fail in administering justice, the parties shall come before our lord the king, that by his precept the controversy may be terminated in the archbishop’s court, so that it may not proceed further without the consent of our lord the king.

  4. If a dispute shall arise between a clerk and a laic, or between a laic and a clerk, about a tenement, which the clerk wishes to claim as eleemosynary, but the laic claims as lay fee, it shall be settled by the declaration of twelve qualified men, through the agency of the king’s capital judiciary, whether the tenement is eleemosynary or lay fee, in presence of the king’s judiciaries. And if it shall be declared that it is eleemosynary, it shall be pleaded in the ecclesiastical court; but, if a lay fee, unless both shall claim the tenement of the same bishop or baron, it shall be pleaded in the king’s court; but if both shall claim of that fee from the same bishop or baron, it shall be pleaded in his court, yet so that the same declaration above-named shall not deprive of seizing him who before was seized, until he shall be divested by the pleadings.

X. If any man belonging to a city, castle, borough, or king’s royal manor shall be summoned by the archdeacon or bishop to answer for a crime, and shall not comply with the summons, it shall be lawful to place him under an interdict, but not to excommunicate him, until the king’s principal officer of that place be informed thereof, that he may justify his appearing to the summons; and if the king’s officer shall fail in that matter, he shall be at the king’s mercy, and the bishop shall forthwith coerce the party accused with ecclesiastical discipline.

  1. The archbishops, bishops, and all other persons of the kingdom, who hold of the king in chief, shall hold their possessions of the king as barony, and answer for the same to the king’s justiciaries and officers, and follow and observe all the king’s customs and rectitudes; and be bound to be present, in the judgment of the king’s court with the barons, like other barons, until the judgment proceeds to mutilation or death.

  2. When an archbishopric, bishopric, abbacy, or priory on the king’s domain shall be vacant, it shall be in his hand, and he shall receive from it all the revenues and proceeds, as of his domains. And when the time shall come for providing for that church, our lord the king shall recommend the best persons to that church, and the election shall be made in the king’s chapel, with the king’s consent, and the advice of the persons of the kingdom whom he shall have summoned for that purpose. And the person elected shall there do homage and fealty to our lord the king, as to his liege lord, of life and limb, and of his earthly honors saving his orders, before he is consecrated.

  3. If any of the king’s nobles shall have refused to render justice to an archbishop or bishop or archdeacon, for himself or any of his men, our lord the king shall justice them. And if by chance any one shall have deforced our lord the king of his rights, the archbishops, bishops, and archdeacons shall justice him that he may render satisfaction to the king.

  4. The chattels of those who are in forfeiture to the king shall not be detained by the Church or the cemetery, in opposition to the king’s justice, for they belong to the king, whether they are found in the Church or without.

  5. Pleas for debts which are due, whether with the interposition of a pledge of faith or not, belong to the king’s court.

  6. The sons of rustics shall not be ordained without the consent of the lord, in whose land they are known to have been born.

These Constitutions were drawn up in the spirit and language of feudalism, under the inspiration of the king, by Archbishop Roger of York, Bishop Foliot of London (the chief enemies of Becket), Bishop Joceline of Salisbury, Richard de Luci (the king’s chief judiciary), and Joceline of Baliol. They are restrictions on the immunities of the clergy; the last is an invasion of the rights of the people, but is based on the canonical exclusion of slaves from the clerical order without the consent of their masters. They subject the clergy equally with the laity to the crown and the laws of the land. They reduce the Church to an imperium in imperio, instead of recognizing her as a distinct and independent imperium. They formulate in the shape of legal enactments certain "ancient customs" (consuetudines) which date from the time of William the Conqueror, and were conceded by Lanfranc; but they infringe at many points on the ancient privileges of the Church, and are inconsistent with the hierarchical principle of the exemption of the clergy from temporal jurisdiction. And this was the chief point of the quarrel between the king and the archbishop.

In the present state of civilization there can be no doubt that the clergy should obey the same laws and be subject to the same penalties as the laity. But we must not overlook the fact that in the Middle Ages the clerical exemption had a humanitarian as well as a hierarchical feature, and involved a protest against barbarous punishments by mutilation of the human body, man being made in the image of God. It prepared the way for a mitigation of the criminal code for the benefit of the whole people, the laity as well as the clergy. This explains the large amount of popular sympathy with the cause of Becket.

Becket gave a qualified assent. On his return to Canterbury he changed his mind and imposed upon himself severe penances, and sought and obtained the pope’s absolution from his oath. But Alexander, hard pressed by Barbarossa and the anti-pope, and anxious to keep the good will of Henry, tried to please both parties. He granted, at the request of Henry, legatine commission over all England to Archbishop Roger of York, the rival of the primate of Canterbury. He also afterwards authorized the coronation of Henry’s eldest son by the archbishop of York in the Abbey of Westminster (June 18, 1170), although such coronation was the exclusive privilege of the archbishop of Canterbury. This aggravated the difficulty with the king, and brought on the final crisis.

In the meantime the Clarendon Constitutions were carried out. Clergymen convicted of crime in the king’s court were condemned and punished like laymen.

Becket attempted to flee to the pope, and sailed for the Continent, but was brought back by the sailors on account of adverse winds. This was a violation of the law which forbade bishops to leave the country without royal permission.

He was summoned before a great council of bishops and nobles at the royal castle of Northampton in the autumn of 1164, and charged with misconduct in secular affairs while chancellor and archbishop. But his courage rose with the danger. He refused to answer, and appealed to the pope. The council ordered him cited to Rome on the charges of perjury at Clarendon and of commanding his suffragans to disregard the Constitutions. The bishops he met with a haughty refusal when they advised him to resign. He was to be arrested, but he threatened the peers with excommunication if they pronounced the sentence. He took the bold course of making his escape to the Continent in the disguise of a monk, at midnight, accompanied by two monks and a servant, and provided with his episcopal pall and seal.

The king seized the revenues of the archbishop, forbade public prayers for him, and banished him from the kingdom, ordered the banishment of all his kinsmen and friends, including four hundred persons of both sexes, and suspended the payment of Peter’s pence to the pope.

Becket spent fully six years in exile, from October, 1164, to December, 1170. King Louis of France, an enemy of Henry and admirer of Becket, received him with distinction and recommended him to the pope, who, himself in exile, resided at Sens. Becket met Alexander, laid before him the Constitutions of Clarendon, and tendered his resignation. The pope condemned ten as a violation of ecclesiastical privileges, and tolerated six as less evil than the rest. He tenderly rebuked Becket for his weakness in swearing to them, but consoled him with the assurance that he had atoned for it by his sufferings. He restored to him the archiepiscopal ring, thus ratifying his primacy, promised him his protection, and committed him to the hospitable care of the abbot of Pontigny, a Cistercian monastery about twelve leagues distant from Sens. Here Becket lived till 1166, like a stern monk, on pulse and gruel, slept on a bed of straw, and submitted at midnight to the flagellation of his chaplain, but occasionally indulged in better diet, and retained some of his former magnificence in his surroundings. His sober friend, John of Salisbury, remonstrated against the profuse expenditure.

Becket proceeded to the last extremity of pronouncing, in the church of Vezelay, on Whitsuntide, 1166, the sentence of excommunication on all the authors and defenders of the Constitutions of Clarendon. He spared the king, who then was dangerously ill, but in a lower tone, half choked with tears, he threatened him with the vengeance of God, and his realm with the interdict. He announced the sentence to the pope and all the clergy of England, saying to the latter, "Who presumes to doubt that the priests of God are the fathers and masters of kings, princes, and all the faithful?"

The wrath of Henry knew no bounds. He closed the ports of England against the bearers of the instrument of excommunication, threatening them with shameful mutilation, hanging, and burning. He procured the expulsion of Becket from Pontigny, who withdrew to a monastery near the archiepiscopal city of Sens. He secured through his ambassadors several concessions from Alexander, who was then in exile at Benevento. The pope was anxious to retain the support of the king, and yet he wrote soothing letters to Becket, assuring him that the concessions were to be only temporary. Becket answered with indignation, and denounced the papal court for its venality and rapacity. "Your gold and silver," he wrote to the cardinals, "will not deliver you in the day of the wrath of the Lord."

The king now determined to use the permission received from the pope several years before, but afterwards revoked,(42) and have his son crowned by Roger, archbishop of York. This humiliating infringement upon the rights of the primate stirred Becket’s blood afresh. He repeated his excommunication. Like Gregory VII., he applied the words, "Cursed is he that refraineth his sword from blood," to the spiritual weapon. He even commanded the bishops of England to lay the whole kingdom under interdict and to suspend the offices of religion (except baptism, penance, and extreme unction), unless the king should give full satisfaction before the feast of purification, Nov. 2, 1170.(43)

These extreme measures were not without effect. Several bishops began to waver and change from the king’s cause to that of the archbishop. The king himself was alarmed at the menace of the interdict. The pope pursued his temporizing policy, and counselled concessions by both parties.

The king and the archbishop suddenly made peace in a respectful personal interview at Fretteville (Freteval), a castle between Tours and Chartres, July 22, 1170. Henry said nothing about the Clarendon Constitutions, but made the offer that Becket should crown his daughter-in-law (the daughter of the king of France), and should on that occasion repeat the coronation of his son. Becket laid the blame on the shoulders of Henry’s counsellors, and showed moderation and prudence. The king did not offer the kiss of peace, nor did the archbishop demand it.

But while Becket was willing to pardon the king, he meant to exercise his spiritual authority over his evil counsellors, and especially over the archbishop of York and the bishops of London and Salisbury. These prelates had recently officiated at the coronation of Henry’s son. And it was this coronation, even more than the original and more important dispute about the immunity of the clergy, that led to the catastrophe.

After prolonged negotiations with the papal court and the king, Becket returned to his long-neglected flock, Dec. 1, 1170. On landing at Sandwich (instead of Dover, where he was expected), he was surprised by enemies, who searched his baggage, and demanded that he should withdraw his excommunication of the bishops who were then at Dover. He refused. On his way to Canterbury the country clergy and people met him, cast down their garments, chanting, "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord." He rode to the cathedral with a vast procession, amid the ringing of the bells, and preached on the text, "Here we have no abiding city."

The excommunicated prelates of York, London, and Salisbury sought the protection of the king, who was then at a castle near Bayeux in Normandy. He said: "If all are to be excommunicated who officiated at my son’s coronation, by the eyes of God, I am equally guilty." One of the prelates (perhaps Roger of York) remarked, "As long as Thomas lives, you will never be at peace." Henry broke out into one of his constitutional fits of passion, and dropped the fatal words: "A fellow that has eaten my bread, has lifted up his heel against me; a fellow that I loaded with benefits, dares insult the king; a fellow that came to court on a lame horse, with a cloak for a saddle, sits without hindrance on the throne itself. By the eyes of God, is there none of my thankless and cowardly courtiers who will deliver me from the insults of this low-born and turbulent priest?" With these words he rushed out of the room.

§ 33. The Martyrdom of Thomas Becket. Dec. 29, 1170.

On the murder of Becket we have the reports of five eye-witnesses, Edward Grim (a Saxon monk of Cambridge), William Fitz-Stephen (Becket’s chaplain), John of Salisbury (his faithful friend), William of Canterbury, and the anonymous author of a Lambeth MS. Two other biographers, Herbert of Bosham and Roger of Pontigny, though absent from England at that time, were on intimate terms with Becket, and took great pains to ascertain the facts to the minutest details.

Four warlike knights of high birth and large estate, chamberlains to the king,(44)— Sir Reginald Fitz-Urse ("Son of the Bear," whom Becket had originally introduced to the court), Sir William. de Tracy (of royal blood), Hugh de Moreville (judiciary of Northumberland and Cumberland), and Sir Richard le Bret or Breton (commonly known as Brito(45)), eagerly caught at the king’s suggestion, and resolved to carry it out in the spirit of passionate loyalty, at their own risk, as best they could, by imprisonment, or exile, or, if necessary, by murder. They seem to have had no premeditated plan except that of signal vengeance. Without waiting for instructions, they at once departed on separate routes for England, and met at the castle of Saltwood, which belonged to the see of Canterbury, but was then occupied by Randulf of Broc. They collected a band of about a dozen armed men, and reached St. Augustine’s abbey outside of the walls of Canterbury, early on the 29th of December, which was a Tuesday.

On the morning of that fatal day, Becket had forebodings of his death, and advised the clergy to escape to Sandwich before daylight. He attended mass in the cathedral, confessed to two monks, and received three scourgings, as was his custom. At the banquet he drank more freely than usual, and said to the cupbearer, "He who has much blood to shed, must drink much." After dinner he retired to his private room and sat on his bed, talking to his friends, John of Salisbury, William Fitz-Stephen, and Edward Grim. He was then still in full vigor, being in the fifty-third year of his age, retaining his dignified aspect and the lustre of his large eyes.

At about four that afternoon, the knights went to the archbishop’s palace, leaving their weapons behind, and concealing their coats of mail by the ordinary cloak and gown. They demanded from him, in the name of the king, the absolution of the excommunicated bishops and courtiers. He refused, and referred them to the pope, who alone could absolve them. He declared: "I will never spare a man who violates the canons of Rome or the rights of the Church. My spirituals I hold from God and the pope; my temporals, from the king. Render unto Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and unto God the things that are God’s." The knights said, "You speak in peril of your life." Becket replied: "Come ye to murder me in my own house? You cannot be more ready to kill me than I am to die. You threaten me in vain; were all the swords in England hanging over my head, you could not terrify me from my obedience to God and my lord the pope. I defy you, and will meet you foot to foot in the battle of the Lord." During the altercation, Becket lost command over his fiery temper. His friend, John of Salisbury, gently censured him for his exasperating tone. The knights quitted the room and called their men to arms.

A few minutes before five the bell tolled for vespers. Urged by his friends, the archbishop, with his cross carried before him, went through the cloisters to the cathedral. The service had begun, the monks were chanting the psalms in the choir, the church was filled with people, when two boys rushed up the nave and created a panic by announcing that armed men were breaking into the cloister. The attendants of Becket, who had entered the church, shut the door and urged him to move into the choir for safety. "Away, you cowards!" he said, "by virtue of your obedience, I command you not to shut the door; the church must not be turned into a fortress." He was evidently prepared and eager for martyrdom. He himself reopened the door, and dragged the excluded monks into the building, exclaiming, "Come in, come in—faster, faster!" The monks and priests were terror-stricken and fled in every direction, to the recesses and side-chapels, to the roof above, and the crypt below. Three only remained faithful,—Canon Robert of Merton, Chaplain William Fitz-Stephen, and the clerk Edward Grim.(46) One of the monks confesses that he ran with clasped hands up the steps as fast as his feet would carry him.

Becket proceeded to the high altar and archiepiscopal chair, in which he and all his predecessors from time immemorial had been enthroned. There, no doubt, he wished to gain the crown of martyrdom. It was now about five in the winter evening; the shades of night were gathering, and the lamps on the altars shed only a dim light in the dark cathedral. The tragedy which followed was finished in a few minutes.

In the meantime the knights, clad in mail which covered their faces up to their eyes, and with drawn swords, followed by a motley group of ruffians, provided with hatchets, rushed into the cathedral and shouted: "Where is the traitor? Where is the archbishop?"(47) Becket replied, descending the steps of the altar and facing his enemies, "Behold me, no traitor, but a priest of God!" They again demanded the absolution of the bishops and his surrender to the king’s justice. "I cannot do otherwise than I have done," he said, and turning to Fitz-Urse, who was armed with a sword and an axe, he added; "Reginald, you have received many favors at my hands: come you to me and into my church armed!" The knights tried to drag him out of the sanctuary, not intending to kill him there; but he braced himself against the pillar between the altars of the Virgin, his special patroness, and St. Benedict, whose rule he followed, and said: "I am ready to die. May the Church through my blood obtain peace and liberty! I charge you in the name of God Almighty that you hurt no one here but me." In the struggle, he grappled with De Tracy and threw him to the pavement. He called Fitz-Urse (who had seized him by the collar of his long cloak) a miserable wretch, and wrenched the cloak from his grasp, saying, "Off, thou pander, thou!"(48) The soldier, maddened by the foul epithet, waving the sword over his head, struck the first blow, and dashed off his cap. Tracy, rising from the pavement, aimed at his head; but Edward Grim, standing by, interposed his arm, which was almost severed, and then he sank back against the wall. Becket received blow after blow in an attitude of prayer. As he felt the blood trickling down his face, he bowed his neck for the death-blow, clasped his hands, and said in a low voice: "I commend my cause and the cause of the Church to God, to St. Denis, the martyr of France, to St. Alfege, and to the saints of the Church.(49) In the name of Christ and for the defence of his Church, I am ready to die. Lord, receive my spirit."

These were his last words. The next blow felled him to his knees, the last laid him on the floor at the foot of the altar of St. Benedict. His hands were still joined as if in prayer. Richard the Breton cut off the upper part of his skull, which had received the sacred oil. Hugh of Horsea, the subdeacon, trampled upon his neck, thrust his sword into the ghastly wound, and scattered the blood and the brains over the pavement.(50) Then he said, "Let us go, let us go; the traitor is dead; he will rise no more."

The murderers rushed from the church through the cloisters into the palace for plunder; while a violent thunder-storm broke over the cathedral. They stole about two thousand marks in gold and silver, and rode off on Becket’s fine horses in the thick darkness of the night.

The body of Thomas was buried in the crypt. The remains of his blood and brains were sacredly kept. His monkish admirers discovered, to their amazement and delight, that the martyr, who had once been arrayed in purple and fine linen, wore on his skin under his many garments the coarsest haircloth abounding with vermin. This seemed to betray the perfection of ascetic sanctity according to mediaeval notions.(51) The spot of his "martyrdom" is still shown close to the entrance of the cathedral from the cloister.

§ 34. The Effects of Becket’s Murder.

The atrocious murder sent a thrill of horror throughout the Christian world. The moment of Becket’s death was his triumph. His exalted station, his personal virtues, the sacrilege,—all contributed to deepen the impression. At first opinion was divided, as he had strong enemies, even at Canterbury. A monk declared that Becket paid a just penalty for his obstinacy others said, "He wished to be king and more than king; the archbishop of York dared to preach that Becket "perished, like Pharaoh, in his pride."

But the torrent of public admiration soon silenced all opposition. Miracles took place at his tomb, and sealed his claim to the worship of a saint and martyr. "The blind see, the deaf hear, the dumb speak, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the devils are cast out, even the dead are raised to life." Thus wrote John of Salisbury, his friend.(52) Remarkable cures, no doubt, took place; credulity and fraud exaggerated and multiplied them. Within a few years after the murder, two collections of his miracles were published, one by Benedict, prior of Canterbury (afterwards abbot of Peterborough), and one by William, monk of Canterbury.(53) According to these reports, the miracles began to occur the very night of the archbishop’s death. His blood had miraculous efficacy for those who drank it.(54)

Two years after his death, Feb. 21, 1173, Becket was solemnly canonized by Alexander III., who had given him only a lukewarm support in his contest with the king. There is scarcely another example of such an early recognition of saintship; but public sentiment had anticipated it. At a council in Westminster the papal letters of canonization were read. All the bishops who had opposed Becket were present, begged pardon for their offence, and acquiesced in the pope’s decision. The 29th of December was set apart as the feast of "St. Thomas of Canterbury."

King Henry II., as the supposed author of the monstrous crime, was branded with a popular excommunication. On the first news, he shut himself up for three days in his chamber, rolled himself in sackcloth and ashes, and obstinately refused food and comfort. He lived secluded for five weeks, exclaiming again and again, "Alas, alas that it ever happened!" He issued orders for the apprehension of the murderers, and despatched envoys to the pope to exculpate, himself and to avert the calamity of excommunication and, an interdict. After long delay a reconciliation took place in the cathedral of Avranches in Normandy, before the papal legates, the archbishop of Rouen, and many bishops and noblemen, May 22, 1172.(55) Henry swore on the holy Gospels that he had neither commanded nor desired the death of Becket, that it caused him more grief than the death of his father or his mother, and that he was ready to make full satisfaction. He pledged himself to abrogate the Statutes of Clarendon; to restore the church of Canterbury to all its rights and possessions; to undertake, if the pope should require it, a three years’ crusade to Jerusalem or Spain, and to support two hundred knights in the Holy Land. After these pledges he said aloud: "Behold, my lord legates, my body is in your hands; be assured that whatever you order, whether to go to Jerusalem or to Rome or to St. James [at Compostella in Spain], I am ready to obey." He was led by the bishops into the church and reconciled. His son, who was present, promised Cardinal Albert to make good his father’s pledges. This penance was followed by a deepest humiliation at Canterbury.

Two years later, July 12, 1174, the king, depressed by disasters and the rebellion of his wife and his sons, even made a pilgrimage to the tomb of Becket. He dismounted from his horse as he came in sight of the towers of Canterbury, walked as a penitent pilgrim in a woollen shirt, with bare and bleeding feet, through the streets, knelt in the porch of the cathedral, kissed the sacred stone on which the archbishop had fallen, threw himself prostrate before the tomb in the crypt, and confessed to the bishops with groans and tears his deep remorse for the hasty words which had led to the murder. Gilbert Foliot, bishop of London, once Becket’s rival and enemy, announced to the monks and bystanders the king’s penitence and intention to restore the rights and property of the Church, and to bestow forty marks yearly on the monastery to keep lamps burning at the martyr’s tomb. The king, placing his head and shoulders on the tomb, submitted to the degrading punishment of scourging, and received five stripes from each bishop and abbot, and three stripes from each of the eighty monks. Fully absolved, he spent the whole night on the bare ground of the crypt in tears and prayers, imploring the forgiveness of the canonized saint in heaven whom he had persecuted on earth.

No deeper humiliation of king before priest is recorded in history. It throws into the shade the submission of Theodosius to Ambrose, of Edgar to Dunstan, of Barbarossa to Alexander, and even the scene at Canossa.

Fifty years after the martyrdom, Becket’s relics were translated with extraordinary solemnity from the tomb in the crypt to the costly shrine of Becket, which blazed with gold and jewels, in the reconstructed Canterbury cathedral (1220). And now began on the largest scale that long succession of pilgrimages, which for more than three hundred years made Canterbury the greatest sacred resort of Western Christendom, next to Jerusalem and Rome. It was more frequented than Loreto in Italy and Einsiedeln in Switzerland. No less than a hundred thousand pilgrims were registered at Canterbury in 1420. From all parts of England, Scotland, Wales, and Ireland, from France and the far north, men and women flocked to the shrine: priests, monks, princes, knights, scholars, lawyers, merchants, mechanics, peasants. There was scarcely an English king, from Henry II. to Henry VIII., who did not from motives of piety or policy pay homage to the memory of the saint. Among the last distinguished visitors were John Colet, dean of St. Paul’s, and Erasmus, who visited the shrine together between the years 1511 and 1513, and King Henry VIII. and Emperor Charles V., who attended the last jubilee in 1520. Plenary indulgences were granted to the pilgrims. Some went in December, the month of his martyrdom; a larger number in July, the month of the translation of his relics. Every fiftieth year a jubilee lasting fifteen days was celebrated in his honor. Six such jubilees were celebrated,—1270, 1320, 1370, 1420, 1470, 1520. The offerings to St. Thomas exceeded those given to any other saint, even to the holy Virgin.

Geoffrey Chaucer, the father of English poetry, who lived two centuries after Becket’ martyrdom, has immortalized these pilgrimages in his Canterbury Tales, and given us the best description of English society at that time.

The pilgrimages promoted piety, social intercourse, superstition, idleness, levity, and immorality, and aroused moral indignation among many serious and spiritually minded men.

The superstitious idolatry of St. Thomas was continued down to the time of the Reformation, when it was rudely but forever crushed out. Henry VIII. cited Becket to appear in court to answer to the charges of treason and rebellion. The case was formally argued at Westminster. His guilt was proved, and on the 10th of June, 1538, St. Thomas was condemned as a "rebel and a traitor to his prince." The rich shrine at Canterbury was pillaged; the gold and jewels were carried off in two strong coffers, and the rest of the treasure in twenty-six carts. The jewels went into the hands of Henry VIII., who wore the most precious of them, a diamond, the "Regale of France," in the ring on his thumb; afterwards it glittered in the golden, "collar" of his daughter, the bigoted Queen Mary. A royal proclamation explained the cause and mode of Becket’s death, and the reasons for his degradation. All festivals, offices, and prayers in his name were forbidden. The site of his shrine has remained vacant to this day.

The Reformation prepared the way for a more spiritual worship of God and a more just appreciation of the virtues and faults of Thomas Becket than was possible in the age in which he lived and died,—a hero and a martyr of the papal hierarchy, but not of pure Christianity, as recorded in the New Testament. To the most of his countrymen, as to the English-speaking people at large, his name has remained the synonym for priestly pride and pretension, for an arrogant invasion of the rights of the civil estate. To a certain class of English High Churchmen he remains, like Laud of a later age, the martyr of sacerdotal privilege, the unselfish champion of the dowered rights of the Church. The atrocity of his taking-off no one will choose to deny. But the haughty assumption of the high prelate had afforded pretext enough for vehement indignation and severe treatment. Priestly robes may for a time conceal and even protect pride from violence, but sooner or later it meets its just reward. The prelate’s superiority involved in Becket’s favorite expression, "saving the honor of my order," was more than a king of free blood could be expected to bear.

This dramatic chapter of English history may be fitly closed with a scene from Lord Tennyson’s tragedy which presents the personal quality that brought about Thomas à Becket’s fall.(56)

John of Salisbury.

Thomas, I would thou hadst returned to England

Like some wise prince of this world from his wars,

With more of olive-branch and amnesty

For foes at home—thou hast raised the world against thee.

Becket.

Why, John, my kingdom is not of this world.

John of Salisbury.

If it were more of this world it might be

More of the next. A policy of wise pardon

Wins here as well as there. To bless thine enemies —

Becket.

Ay, mine, not Heaven’s.

John of Salisbury.

And may there not be something

Of this world’s leaven in thee too, when crying

On Holy Church to thunder out her rights

And thine own wrong so piteously. Ah, Thomas,

The lightnings that we think are only Heaven’s

Flash sometimes out of earth against the heavens.

The soldier, when he lets his whole self go

Lost in the common good, the common wrong,

Strikes truest ev’n for his own self. I crave

Thy pardon—I have still thy leave to speak.

Thou hast waged God’s war against the King; and yet

We are self-uncertain creatures, and we may,

Yea, even when we know not, mix our spites

And private hates with our defence of Heaven.
