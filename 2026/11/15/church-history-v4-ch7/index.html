§ 82. 中世纪修道院的作用

修士是教会的属灵贵族，他们与世界完全隔绝，献身于上帝的国，代表着一种更高的德行典范。教父时代的敬虔理想传入了中世纪；这既不是圣经的理想，也不是现代的理想，而是在与先前及周围异教的败坏形成鲜明对照的过程中形成的理想。修士式的圣洁，与其说是胜过世界，不如说是逃离世界；是禁绝婚姻，而不是使婚姻成圣；是在自然秩序之外而非其中持守贞洁；是彻底压抑感官欲望，而不是将其洁净和约束。然而，这种圣洁对蛮族产生了强大的影响，是促成归信和文明开化的主要力量之一。东方修士沉溺于无所事事的默想和过度的苦修，而西方的气候使这些做法无法实行；总的说来，西方修士更为持重、务实，也更有益处。爱尔兰 (Irish) 和苏格兰 (Scotch) 的修道院以传教热忱著称，为各地提供了教会的创立者和民众的主保圣徒。

随着基督教的进展，传教士在欧洲各蛮族中纷纷建立修道院。修道院从君主、贵族、教皇和主教那里获得了特别的特权和捐赠。那些厌倦人生纷扰，或在家产、名誉上遭遇覆舟之灾，除了拯救自己的灵魂以外别无所求的男女，都能在其中找到安静的退隐之所。修道院款待异乡人和旅客，在旅行艰难而危险的时代，实为极大的福惠。1它们是操练苦修德行的学校，是培育圣徒的园地。它们保存了古代文明的遗存，以供后世使用。每一所大修道院都有图书馆和学校，并雇用抄写员，抄录古代经典、圣经和教父著作的手稿。古代几乎全部神圣和世俗学问的保存与流传，都有赖于这些安静从事文献工作的修士。即使他们没有做过别的事，也足以赢得教会和世界长久的感激。

在第九、十世纪剧烈的动荡与混乱中，修道纪律日渐衰败。修道院的财富，本是勤劳和德行的报偿，却往往反而成为网罗和祸根。贪婪的平信徒（Abba-comites，伯爵院长）夺取了修道院的控制权，并使之世代留在自己的家族之中。甚至公主也领受女修道院院长的头衔和俸禄。

§ 83. 圣本笃、圣尼卢斯、圣罗穆阿尔德

然而，即使在这黑暗的时期，仍有几盏明灯。

阿尼亚讷的圣本笃 (St. Benedict of Aniane，750—821年) 出身于法国南部的显赫家族，曾在查理大帝 (Charlemagne) 的宫廷任职，后来厌弃尘世，进入修道院，又依照努尔西亚的圣本笃 (St. Benedict of Nursia) 的严格会规，在阿尼亚讷建立了一所新修道院。他搜集藏书，施行慈善，尤其在一次饥荒期间如此，并致力于改革修道制度。虔诚者路易 (Louis the Pious) 委托他督管西法兰克的全部修道院；他使这些修道院遵行同一会规，将它们组成一个“会众”。他出席了817年的亚琛 (Aix-la-Chapelle) 宗教会议。他去世（821年2月12日）后不久，劳苦所得的成果便遭毁坏，混乱比从前更加严重。2

小圣尼卢斯 (St. Nilus the younger)3具有希腊血统，生于卡拉布里亚 (Calabria) 的罗萨诺 (Rossano)4，故称 Nilus Rossanensis（罗萨诺的尼卢斯）；他照亮了第十世纪的黑暗。约在940年，妻子去世后，他便仿效圣安东尼 (St. Anthony) 和圣希拉里翁 (St. Hilarion)，献身于独修生活，并在意大利南部建立了数所修道院。显贵们常向他求教，他像圣安东尼一样，不徇情面地作答。他大胆斥责教皇格列高利五世 (Gregory V.) 和皇帝奥托三世 (Otho III.) 虐待一位大主教。后来皇帝表示，他无论求什么恩惠都可以得到，尼卢斯回答说：“我向你别无所求，只求你拯救自己的灵魂；因为你必像其他每一个人一样死去，并要为自己所行的一切善恶向上帝交账。”皇帝摘下头上的冠冕，恳求这位年老修士的祝福。一位生活放荡的贵族拿所罗门的例子安慰自己，问尼卢斯，那位智慧的君王岂不是得救了吗？修士回答：“所罗门的结局与我们无关；但对我们说的是：‘凡看见妇女就动淫念的，这人心里已经与她犯奸淫了。’我们没有读到所罗门曾像玛拿西那样悔改。”对于出于无聊好奇的问题，他或不作答，或照愚昧人的愚妄话回答他。因此，有人想知道亚当和夏娃吃了什么样的苹果，以致败亡，他便说是野酸苹果。晚年，他被入侵者逐出卡拉布里亚，在西塞罗 (Cicero) 著名的图斯库卢姆 (Tusculum) 附近建立了一所小修道院，名为克里普塔费拉塔 (Crypta Ferrata)。1005年，他在那里安然去世，享年约九十六岁。5

圣罗穆阿尔德 (St. Romuald) 是卡马尔多利修会 (order of Camaldoli) 的创立者，第十世纪初生于拉文纳 (Ravenna) 一个富有的贵族家庭。他二十岁时进入邻近的克拉西斯 (Classis) 本笃会修道院，为要以四十天严厉的补赎，抵偿父亲在财产争执中杀死一位亲属的罪。他几乎不住地祈祷哭泣。他在这所修道院住了三年，此后过着四处漂泊的隐修生活。他以种种克己苦行加诸己身，为要战胜魔鬼的试探。他的敬虔操练之一，是每天凭记忆背诵《诗篇》全卷；威尼斯 (Venice) 附近一位朴实的隐修士马里努斯 (Marinus)，曾借助责打教他这种机械式的功课及其他苦修操练。无论他走到哪里，都有敬仰他的门徒追随。人们相信他领受了预言和行神迹的恩赐，然而他仍不免遭人诽谤。1000年，皇帝奥托三世曾在拉文纳附近的一座岛上拜访他。罗穆阿尔德差派传教士前往异教之地，自己也带领一些门徒来到匈牙利 (Hungary) 边境；但一场重病警告他，他并非命定要过传教生活，于是便返回。他于1027年在瓦莱德卡斯特罗 (Valle de Castro) 修道院去世。6

达米安尼 (Damiani) 在罗穆阿尔德死后十五年为他作传；据他所说，罗穆阿尔德活了一百二十岁，其中二十年生活在尘世，三年住在修道院，九十七年作隐修士。7

罗穆阿尔德的修道隐居之所，最著名的是坎波马尔多利 (Campo Maldoli)，又称卡马尔多利 (Camaldoli)，位于托斯卡纳 (Tuscany) 阿雷佐 (Arezzo) 附近的亚平宁山脉 (Appennines)，由他于约1009年建立。达米安尼既是他的颂扬者，又是希尔德布兰德 (Hildebrand) 的朋友；在达米安尼的影响下，这里成为一个修会的核心。这个修会将共住生活与隐修生活结合起来，以极其严厉著称。教皇格列高利十六世 (Gregory XVI.) 就属于这个修会。

§ 84. 克吕尼修道院

马里耶 (Marrier) 与迪谢纳 (Duchesne)：《克吕尼文献集》(Bibliotheca Cluniacensis)。巴黎，1614年，对开本。霍尔斯滕 (Holsten.)：《修道会规汇编》(Cod. Regul. Mon.)，II. 176。洛兰 (Lorain)：《克吕尼修道院史论》(Essay historique sur l’ abbaye de Cluny)。第戎，1839年。尼安德 (Neander)，III. 417及后，444及下页。弗里德里希·胡尔特 (Friedr. Hurter，曾任沙夫豪森的新教牧师，后改宗罗马天主教)：《教皇英诺森三世史》(Gesch. Papst Innocenz des Dritten)，第二版，汉堡，1844年，第IV卷，第22—55页。

第九、十世纪修道纪律衰败之后，一场改革从勃艮第 (Burgundy) 的克吕尼 (Cluny) 修道院兴起，影响了整个教会。8

该院由虔诚的阿基坦公爵威廉 (Duke William of Aquitania) 于910年建立，以圣本笃会规为基础，为尊崇圣彼得和圣保罗而设。

布鲁诺伯爵 (Count Bruno，卒于927年) 是第一任院长，推行了严厉的纪律。他的继任者奥多 (Odo，927—941年在任)，起初是一名军人，后来成为一位博学、睿智、品格圣洁的教士，改革了数所本笃会修道院。尼安德赞赏他对基督徒生活的开明见解，也赞赏他认为基督教的道德力量高于其神迹力量的卓识。失明后辞职的艾马尔杜斯 (Aymardus，又名 Aymard，941—948年在任)、谢绝教皇冠冕的马约卢斯 (Majolus，又名 Maieul，任至994年)、绰号“良善者”的奥迪洛 (Odilo，任至1048年)，以及雨果 (Hugo，任至1109年)，都秉承同样的精神。后两人对皇帝和教皇施加了重大影响，并推动了教皇制度和教会的改革。正是在克吕尼，希尔德布兰德劝告由亨利三世 (Henry III.) 选为教皇的图尔主教布鲁诺 (Bruno of Toul，即利奥九世〔Leo IX.〕)，先寻求罗马教士的正式选举；这也预示了他自己日后与皇权的冲突。奥迪洛创行了“上帝的休战”(Treuga Dei) 和万灵节。雨果是希尔德布兰德的朋友，治理修道院六十年，使其声望达到顶峰。

克吕尼是改革后的本笃会修道院的中心（archimonasterium，总修道院），其首领是总院长（archiabbas）。它为教会造就了许多杰出的主教和三位教皇（格列高利七世〔Gregory VII.〕、乌尔班二世〔Urban II.〕及帕斯卡二世〔Pascal II.〕）。在最兴盛的时候，它统辖着两千所修道机构。日常生活的一切细节都有规章；一天的大部分时间必须保持缄默，在这段时间里，修士只能以手势交流；院内实行严格的顺服；对穷人和异乡人则慷慨接待，广施仁惠，而他们的人数通常超过修士。在一次严重的饥荒中，奥迪洛用尽了修道院的储粮，甚至熔化圣器，变卖教堂的装饰品以及亨利二世 (Henry II.) 从德国送给他的一顶冠冕。修道院直属教皇管辖，备受优待，获得许多捐赠和特权。9其附属教堂是法国最大、最富丽的教堂（或许也是全欧洲之最），以二十五座祭坛、钟以及昂贵的艺术品为人称羡。教堂由雨果奠基，七十年后，在可敬者彼得 (Peter the Venerable) 治理期间，由教皇英诺森二世 (Innocent II.) 祝圣（1131年）。

克吕尼的榜样促成了其他修会的兴起，例如圣约翰·瓜尔贝特 (St. John Gualbert) 于1038年在距佛罗伦萨 (Florence) 十八英里的地方创立的瓦隆布罗萨会 (Congregation of the Vallombrosa，Vallis umbrosa，意为“荫蔽之谷”)，以及1069年在符腾堡 (Württemberg) 创立的希尔绍会 (Congregation of Hirsau)。

然而，克吕尼的声名与繁荣本身却成了试探和衰落的原因。一位不称职的院长庞蒂乌斯 (Pontius) 挥霍院款，最后被教皇视为劫掠教会者，革职并逐出教会。可敬者彼得是圣伯尔纳 (St. Bernard) 的朋友，又是不幸的阿伯拉尔 (Abelard) 的仁厚庇护者；他长期而明智的治理（1122—1156年），使克吕尼重获生机，再度达到繁荣的顶峰。他将修士人数从200增至460，并使314所修道院归属于母院。1245年，教皇英诺森四世 (Innocent IV.)、十二位枢机及其全体教士、两位宗主教、三位大主教、十一位主教、法国国王、君士坦丁堡皇帝，以及许多公爵、伯爵和骑士及其随从，都在克吕尼院舍中受到款待。10这就是其繁荣的终点。随后又一次衰落来临，克吕尼此后再未完全恢复。末期的院长不过是装点门面的，在法国宫廷中耗费了修道院三分之二的收入。1789年的法国大革命将这个机构一扫而空，使一度闻名的建筑化为废墟；不过，此后已有所修复。11

圣邓斯坦 (St. Dunstan，925年—988年5月19日) 曾在英格兰尝试推行类似的修道制度与教士改革，并取得了部分成效。他起初担任格拉斯顿伯里 (Glastonbury) 修道院院长，继而任温彻斯特 (Winchester) 和伦敦主教，最后成为坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 大主教（961年），并成为王国实际上的统治者。他是一位极其严苛的修士，也是一位具有钢铁意志的教会领袖；他强制实行本笃会规章，将主要主教职位和收入丰厚的圣职交给本笃会士，大力讨伐教士婚姻（当时教士结婚是常态，而非例外），希望借着使司祭脱离尘世来纠正他们的不道德行为；在埃德威 (Edwy) 和埃德加 (Edgar) 两位国王统治期间，他又主张教会以神权统辖世俗政权。然而，他的过激举措却在英格兰引起了修士与世俗教士之间的激烈争斗。他是希尔德布兰德 (Hildebrand) 和托马斯·贝克特 (Thomas à Becket) 的先驱。12

CHAPTER VII. MONASTICISM.

See the Lit. on Monasticism in vol. II. 387, and III. 147 sq.

§ 82. Use of Convents in the Middle Ages.

The monks were the spiritual nobility of the church, and represented a higher type of virtue in entire separation from the world and consecration to the kingdom of God. The patristic, ideal of piety passed over into the middle ages; it is not the scriptural nor the modern ideal, but one formed in striking contrast with preceding and surrounding heathen corruption. The monkish sanctity is a flight from the world rather than a victory over the world, an abstinence from marriage instead of a sanctification of marriage, chastity, outside rather than inside the order of nature, a complete suppression of the sensual passion in the place of its purification and control. But it had a powerful influence over the barbaric races, and was one of the chief converting and civilizing agencies. The Eastern monks lost themselves in idle contemplation and ascetic extravagances, which the Western climate made impossible; the Western monks were, upon the whole, more sober, practical, and useful. The Irish and Scotch convents became famous for their missionary zeal, and furnished founders of churches and patron saints of the people.

Convents were planted by the missionaries among all the barbarous nations of Europe, as fast as Christianity progressed. They received special privileges and endowments from princes, nobles, popes, and bishops. They offered a quiet retreat to men and women who were weary of the turmoil of life, or had suffered shipwreck of fortune or character, and cared for nothing but to save their souls. They exercised hospitality to strangers and travelers, and were a great blessing in times when traveling was difficult and dangerous.(1) They were training schools of ascetic virtue, and the nurseries of saints. They saved the remnants of ancient civilization for future use. Every large convent had a library and a school. Scribes were employed in copying manuscripts of the ancient classics, of the Bible, and the writings of the fathers. To these quiet literary monks we are indebted for the preservation and transmission of nearly all the learning, sacred and secular, of ancient times. If they had done nothing else, they would be entitled to the lasting gratitude of the church and the world.

During the wild commotion and confusion of the ninth and tenth centuries, monastic discipline went into decay. Often the very richs of convents, which were the reward of industry and virtue, became a snare and a root of evil. Avaricious laymen (Abba-comites) seized the control and perpetuated it in their families. Even princesses received the titles and emoluments of abbesses.

§ 83. St. Benedict. St. Nilus. St. Romuald.

Yet even in this dark period there were a few shining lights.

St. Benedict of Aniane (750–821), of a distinguished family in the south of France, after serving at the court of Charlemagne, became disgusted with the world, entered a convent, founded a new one at Aniane after the strict rule of St. Benedict of Nursia, collected a library, exercised charity, especially during a famine, labored for the reform of monasticism, was entrusted by Louis the Pious with the superintendence of all the convents in Western France, and formed them into a "congregation," by bringing them under one rule. He attended the Synod of Aix-la-Chapelle in 817. Soon after his death (Feb. 12, 821) the fruits of his labors were destroyed, and the disorder became worse than before.(2)

St. Nilus the younger,(3) of Greek descent, born at Rossano in Calabria(4) (hence Nilus Rossanensis), enlightened the darkness of the tenth century. He devoted himself, after the death of his wife, about 940, to a solitary life, following the model of St. Anthony and St. Hilarion, and founded several convents in Southern Italy. He was often consulted by dignitaries, and answered, like St. Anthony, without respect of person. He boldly rebuked Pope Gregory V. and Emperor Otho III. for bad treatment of an archbishop. When the emperor afterwards offered him any favor he might ask, Nilus replied: "I ask nothing from you but that you would save your soul; for you must die like every other man, and render an account to God for all your good and evil deeds." The emperor took the crown from his head, and begged the blessing of the aged monk. When a dissolute nobleman, who comforted himself with the example of Solomon, asked Nilus, whether that wise king was not saved, the monk replied: "We have nothing to do with Solomon’s fate; but to us it is said, ’Every one that looketh on a woman to lust after her hath committed adultery with her already in his heart.’ We do not read of Solomon that he ever repented like Manasseh." To questions of idle curiosity he returned no answer, or he answered the fool according to his folly. So when one wished to know what kind of an apple Adam and Eve ate, to their ruin, he said that it was a crab-apple. In his old age he was driven from Calabria by invaders, and founded a little convent, Crypta Ferrata, near the famous Tusculum of Cicero. There he died peacefully when about ninety-six years old, in 1005.(5)

St. Romuald, the founder of the order of Camaldoli, was born early in the tenth century at Ravenna, of a rich and noble family, and entered the neighboring Benedictine convent of Classis, in his twentieth year, in order to atone, by a severe penance of forty days, for a murder which his father had committed against a relative in a dispute about property. He prayed and wept almost without ceasing. He spent three years in this convent, and afterwards led the life of a roaming hermit. He imposed upon himself all manner of self-mortification, to defeat the temptations of the devil. Among his devotions was the daily repetition of the Psalter from memory; a plain hermit, Marinus, near Venice, had taught him this mechanical performance and other ascetic exercises with the aid of blows. Wherever he went, he was followed by admiring disciples. He was believed to be endowed with the gift of prophecy and miracles, yet did not escape calumny. Emperor Otho III. paid him a visit in the year 1000 on an island near Ravenna. Romuald sent missionaries to heathen lands, and went himself to the border of Hungary with a number of pupils, but returned when he was admonished by a severe sickness that he was not destined for missionary life. He died in the convent Valle de Castro in 1027.(6)

According to Damiani, who wrote his life fifteen years after his death, Romuald lived one hundred and twenty years, twenty in the world, three in a convent, ninety-seven as a hermit.(7)

The most famous of Romuald’s monastic retreats is Campo Maldoli, or Camaldoli in the Appennines, near Arezzo in Tuscany, which he founded about 1009. It became, through the influence of Damiani, his eulogist and Hildebrand’s friend, the nucleus of a monastic order, which combined the cenobitic and eremitic life, and was distinguished by great severity. Pope Gregory XVI. belonged to this order.

§ 84. The Convent of Cluny.

Marrier and Duchesne: Bibliotheca Cluniacensis. Paris 1614 fol. Holsten.: Cod. Regul. Mon. II. 176. Lorain: Essay historique sur l’ abbaye de Cluny. Dijon 1839. Neander III. 417 sqq. 444 sq. Friedr. Hurter (Prot, minister in Schaffhausen, afterwards R. Cath.): Gesch. Papst Innocenz des Dritten (second ed. Hamb. 1844), vol. IV. pp. 22–55.

After the decay of monastic discipline during the ninth and tenth centuries, a reformation proceeded from the convent of Cluny in Burgundy, and affected the whole church.(8)

It was founded by the pious Duke William of Aquitania in 910, to the honor of St. Peter and St. Paul, on the basis of the rule of St. Benedict.

Count Bruno (d. 927) was the first abbot, and introduced severe discipline. His successor Odo (927–941), first a soldier, then a clergyman of learning, wisdom, and saintly character, became a reformer of several Benedictine convents. Neander praises his enlightened views on Christian life, and his superior estimate of the moral, as compared with the miraculous, power of Christianity. Aymardus (Aymard, 941–948), who resigned when he became blind, Majolus (Maieul to 994), who declined the papal crown, Odilo, surnamed "the Good" (to 1048), and Hugo (to 1109), continued in the same spirit. The last two exerted great influence upon emperors and popes, and inspired the reformation of the papacy and the church. It was at Cluny that Hildebrand advised Bishop Bruno of Toul (Leo IX.), who had been elected pope by Henry III., to seek first a regular election by the clergy in Rome; and thus foreshadowed his own future conflict with the imperial power. Odilo introduced the Treuga Dei and the festival of All Souls. Hugo, Hildebrand’s friend, ruled sixty years, and raised the convent to the summit of its fame.

Cluny was the centre (archimonasterium) of the reformed Benedictine convents, and its head was the chief abbot (archiabbas). It gave to the church many eminent bishops and three popes (Gregory VII., Urban II., and Pascal II.). In the time of its highest prosperity it ruled over two thousand monastic establishments. The daily life was regulated in all its details; silence was imposed for the greater part of the day, during which the monks communicated only by signs; strict obedience ruled within; hospitality and benevolence were freely exercised to the poor and to strangers, who usually exceeded the number of the monks. During a severe famine Odilo exhausted the magazines of the convent, and even melted the sacred vessels, and sold the ornaments of the church and a crown which Henry II. had sent him from Germany. The convent stood directly under the pope’s jurisdiction, and was highly favored with donations and privileges.(9) The church connected with it was the largest and richest in France (perhaps in all Europe), and admired for its twenty-five altars, its bells, and its costly works of art. It was founded by Hugo, and consecrated seventy years afterwards by Pope Innocent II. under the administration of Peter the Venerable (1131).

The example of Cluny gave rise to other monastic orders, as the Congregation of the Vallombrosa (Vallis umbrosa), eighteen miles from Florence, founded by St. John Gualbert in 1038, and the Congregation of Hirsau in Württemberg, in 1069.

But the very fame and prosperity of Cluny proved a temptation and cause of decline. An unworthy abbot, Pontius, wasted the funds, and was at last deposed and excommunicated by the pope as a robber of the church. Peter the Venerable, the friend of St. Bernard and kind patron of the unfortunate Abelard, raised Cluny by his wise and long administration (1122–1156) to new life and the height of prosperity. He increased the number of monks from 200 to 460, and connected 314 convents with the parent institution. In 1245 Pope Innocent IV., with twelve cardinals and all their clergy, two patriarchs, three archbishops, eleven bishops, the king of France, the emperor of Constantinople, and many dukes, counts and knights with their dependents were entertained in the buildings of Cluny.(10) This was the end of its prosperity. Another decline followed, from which Cluny never entirely recovered. The last abbots were merely ornamental, and wasted two-thirds of the income at the court of France. The French Revolution of 1789 swept the institution out of existence, and reduced the once famous buildings to ruins; but restorations have since been made.(11)

A similar reformation of monasticism and of the clergy was attempted and partially carried out in England by St. Dunstan (925-May 19, 988), first as abbot of Glastonbury, then as bishop of Winchester and London, and last as archbishop of Canterbury (961) and virtual ruler of the kingdom. A monk of the severest type and a churchman of iron will, he enforced the Benedictine rule, filled the leading sees and richer livings with Benedictines, made a crusade against clerical marriage (then the rule rather than the exception), hoping to correct the immorality of the priests by abstracting them from the world, and asserted the theocratic rule of the church over the civil power under Kings Edwy and Edgar; but his excesses called forth violent contentions between the monks and the seculars in England. He was a forerunner of Hildebrand and Thomas à Becket.(12)
