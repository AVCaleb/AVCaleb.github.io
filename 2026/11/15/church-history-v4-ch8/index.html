§85. 悔罪书

I. 各次会议的议决录、查理大帝 (Charlemagne) 及其继承者的敕令集，以及各部悔罪书，尤其是坎特伯雷的狄奥多尔 (Theodore of Canterbury) 的悔罪书和罗马悔罪书。见米涅 (Migne)《教父集》(Patrol.)，第99卷，第901–983页。

大公教会的纪律以授予使徒及其继承者的钥匙权为基础，包括将犯罪的成员逐出教会及使其复归。它起初纯属属灵的管辖权；但在基督教确立为国教以后，也开始影响受惩罚者在世俗社会中的处境。中世纪普遍相信，以罗马教皇制为中心的有形教会，乃是上帝所设立、施予永恒救恩的机构；被逐出她的团契，若不继之以悔改和复归，就意味着永远沉沦。这种信念使教会纪律牢牢支配了公众的心灵。从来没有任何异端或宗派声称拥有这种权力。

纪律为蛮族各族那种狂野而独立的精神所深恶痛绝。主教通过宗教会议法庭执行纪律；在查理大帝的领土内，这种法庭每年开庭，以促进良好的道德。查理大帝命令主教每年巡视所属各堂区一次，查究乱伦、弑父、杀害兄弟、奸淫，以及其他违反上帝律法的恶行。1西班牙和英格兰的宗教会议也颁布了类似的指示。较大的主教区划分为若干会吏长辖区。会吏长代表主教，由于这种密切关系，他们拥有高于司祭的权力和管辖权。会众中有七名成员受托负责监督，必须向审讯法庭报告宗教和道德的状况。无论违反教会法规还是民事法律的行为，都立即受到罚金、禁食、朝圣、鞭打或监禁的惩罚。世俗当局协助主教执行纪律。公开的过犯须受公开的补赎；私下的过犯则向司祭告解，司祭随即在一定条件下给予赦免。

中世纪拉丁教会的纪律载于所谓“悔罪书”中。2这些书规定悔罪的程序，并为某些罪行，如醉酒、淫乱、贪婪、伪誓、杀人、异端、拜偶像，订立具体的惩罚。其材料大多取自教父著作，以及安居拉 (Ancyra，314年)、新凯撒利亚 (Neocaesarea，314年)、尼西亚 (Nicaea，325年)、冈格拉 (Gangra，362年) 各次会议的教规，并取自直至七世纪的北非、法兰克和西班牙各次会议的教规。这些悔罪书的共同宗旨，是使人履行实际的义务，根除异教的凶暴和淫荡情欲。它们描绘了一幅极其阴暗的肉体罪恶图景。它们使人始终意识到上帝的道德治理：凡违背他律法的行为，他都要惩罚；但它们又助长了一种有害的观念，以为罪可以借机械的操练和缴纳一笔钱款来赎除，因而削弱了人的罪责感。

这类书为数甚多，有不列颠的、爱尔兰的、法兰克的、西班牙的和罗马的。其中最著名的是七、八世纪盎格鲁-撒克逊人的悔罪书，尤以坎特伯雷大主教狄奥多尔（669–690年）的为著。他生于基利家 (Cilicia) 的大数 (Tarsus)，是希腊人，将东西方的纪律规则整理成体系。以他的名字命名的那部书，并非由他亲自执笔，而是在他的指导下编成，在他生前经他授权出版；其中收录了他答复一位名叫埃奥达 (Eoda) 的司祭和其他人，就补赎及整个教会纪律领域所提各种问题而作的裁决。近来，德国和英国学者共同努力，已从早期手稿中发现了这部书的真本文本。3其序言和正文均以粗陋的拉丁文写成。从书中对圣巴西尔 (St. Basil) 及希腊习俗的援引，可以看出狄奥多尔所受希腊教育的痕迹。继狄奥多尔的汇编之后，还有以尊者比德 (Bede，卒于735年) 和约克大主教埃格伯特 (Egbert，卒于767年) 之名流传的悔罪书。4

最早的法兰克悔罪书出自爱尔兰传教士哥伦巴努 (Columban，卒于615年) 之手。他严厉执行修道院纪律，在惩罚过犯时特别重视体罚。《库米安悔罪书》(Poenit. Cummeani) 源于苏格兰—爱尔兰传统，或被归于爱奥那的哥伦巴 (Columba of Iona，约597年)，或归于他的门徒之一库明 (Cumin)，或归于死在博比奥 (Bobbio) 哥伦巴努修道院的库米安 (Cummean，711年以后)。康布雷主教哈尔蒂加尔 (Haltigar, bishop of Cambray) 于九世纪（约829年）出版了一部《罗马悔罪书》，声称取材于罗马档案，实际上却有很大部分来自哥伦巴努及法兰克的资料。另有一部较早的著作，也称为《罗马悔罪书》(Poenitentiale Romanum)，成于八世纪前期，性质较为一般，但其确切来源尚不清楚。“罗马”一词，是用来标明某一类悔罪书的性质，表示其权威超出了地方范围。5拉巴努斯·毛鲁斯 (Rabanus Maurus，卒于855年) 应美因茨大主教奥特加尔 (Otgar of Mayence) 之请，于841年编成一部《悔罪书》(Liber Poenitentitae)。几乎每个主教区都有自己的一部此类书籍，但其精神和材料大致相同。

作为这些悔罪书的范例，我们摘录《狄奥多尔悔罪书》(Poenitentiale Theodori，作者为坎特伯雷大主教) 第一卷的头两章，依据哈丹与斯塔布斯《大不列颠与爱尔兰的会议及教会文献》(Councils and Eccles. Doc. relating to Great Britain and Ireland)，第III卷，第177页及后所刊文本。我们也采用了瓦瑟施莱本所用其他手稿中的几处较佳异读。

I. 论饕餮与醉酒 (De Crapula et Ebrietate)

  1. Si quis Episco pus aut aliquis ordinatus in consuetudine vitium habuerit ebrietatis, aut desinat aut deponatur.

若有主教或任何受按立者染有惯常醉酒的恶习，或须戒除，或须革职。

  1. Si monachus pro ebrietate vomitum facit, XXX. dies peniteat.

若修士因醉酒而呕吐，须补赎三十日。

  1. Si presbiter aut diaconus pro ebrietate, XL. dies peniteat.

若司祭或执事因醉酒而如此，须补赎四十日。

  1. Si vero pro infirmitate aut quia longo tempore se abstinuerit, et in consuetudine non erit ei multum bibere vel manducare, aut pro gaudio in Natale Domini aut in Pascha aut pro alicujus Sanctorum commemoratione faciebat, et tunc plus non accipit quam decretum est a senioribus, nihil nocet. Si Episcopus juberit, non nocet illi, nisi ipse similiterfaciat.

但若是由于身体软弱，或由于长久节制，而素无多饮多食的习惯；或是在主降生节、复活节，或纪念某位圣徒时因欢喜而如此，且所进饮食并未超过长老规定的分量，则不算过犯。若是主教吩咐的，对那人不算过犯，除非他自己也照样行。

  1. Si laicus fidelis pro ebrietate vomitum facit, XV. dies peniteat.

若平信徒因醉酒而呕吐，须补赎十五日。

  1. *Qui vero inebriatur contra Domini interdictum, si votum sanctitatis habuerit VII. dies in pane et aqua, LXX. sine pinguedine peniteat; laici sine cervisa [cervisia*].

凡违背主的禁令而醉酒者，若曾立圣洁生活之愿，须以七日仅食面包、饮水，七十日不食油脂作为补赎；平信徒则须戒饮啤酒。

  1. Qui per nequitiam inebriat alium, XL. dies peniteat.

凡存恶意使他人醉酒者，须补赎四十日。

  1. Qui pro satietate vomitum facit, III. diebus [dies] peniteat.

凡因饱食而呕吐者，须补赎三日。

  1. Si cum sacrificio communionis, VII. dies peniteat; si infirmitatis causa, sine culpa.

若连同所领圣餐祭物一并吐出，须补赎七日；若因疾病所致，则无罪。

II. 论淫乱 (De Fornicatione)

  1. Si quis fornicaverit cum virgine, I. anno peniteat. Si cum marita, IIII. annos, II. integros, II alios in XL. mis. III. bus., et III dies in ebdomada peniteat.

若有人与处女行淫，须补赎一年。若与有夫之妇行淫，须补赎四年：两整年，另两年则在每年三个四十日禁食期及每周三日行补赎。

  1. Qui sepe cum masculo aut cum pecude fornicat, X. annos ut peniteret judicavit.

对于屡次与男子或牲畜行淫者，他裁定须补赎十年。

  1. Rem aliud. Qui cum pecoribus coierit, XV. annos peniteat.

另有一项规定：凡与牲畜交合者，须补赎十五年。

  1. Qui coierit cum masculo post XX. annum, XV. annos peniteat.

凡二十岁以后与男子交合者，须补赎十五年。

  1. Si masculus cum masculo fornicaverit, X. annos peniteat.

若男子与男子行淫，须补赎十年。

  1. Sodomitae VII. annos peniteat [peniteant]; molles [et mollis] sicut adultera.

犯所多玛罪者须补赎七年；其中居被动地位者，按淫妇处置。

  1. Item hoc; virile scelus semel faciens IIII annos peniteat; si in consuetudine fuerit, ut Basilius dicit, XV. Si sine, sustinens unum annum ut mulier. Si puer sit, primo II. bus annis; si iterat IIII.

又有此条：犯男子间的恶行一次者，须补赎四年；若已成习惯，则如巴西尔所说，须十五年。若非惯犯，居被动地位者须补赎一年，与妇人相同。若是男孩，初犯须两年；再犯须四年。

  1. Si in femoribus, annum I. vel. III. XL. mas.

若在两股之间行此事，须补赎一年，或三个四十日禁食期。

  1. *Si se ipsum coinguinat, XL. dies [peniteat*.]

若玷污自己，须补赎四十日。

  1. Qui concupiscit fornicari [fornicare] sed non potest, XL. dies vel XX. peniteat. Si frequentaverit, si puer sit, XX. dies, vel vapuletur.

凡欲行淫而未能行者，须补赎四十日或二十日。若屡次如此，且是男孩，须补赎二十日，或受鞭打。

  1. Pueri qui fornicantur inter se ipsos judicavit ut vapulentur.

对于彼此行淫的男孩，他裁定须受鞭打。

  1. Mulier cum muliere fornicando [si ... fornicaverit], III. annos peniteat.

若妇人与妇人行淫，须补赎三年。

  1. Si sola cum se ipsa coitum habet, sic peniteat.

若她独自与自身交合，也须照此补赎。

14. Una penitentia est viduae et puellae. Majorem meruit quae virum habet, si fornicaverit.

寡妇与少女所受补赎相同。有夫之妇若行淫，应受更重的补赎。

  1. Qui semen in os miserit, VII annos peniteat: hoc pessimum malum. Alias ab eo judicatum est ut ambo usque in finem vitae peniteant; vel XXII. annos, vel ut superius VII.

凡将精液射入口中者，须补赎七年：这是极恶之事。他在别处裁定，双方均须终身补赎；或二十二年，或如上述七年。

  1. Si cum matre quis fornicaverit, XV. annos peniteat, et nunquam, mutat [mutet] nisi Dominicis diebus: et hoc tam profanum incertum [incestum] ab eo similiter alio modo dicitur ut cum peregrinatione perenni VII. annos peniteat.

若有人与母亲行淫，须补赎十五年，除主日外，绝不可变更补赎的规制。对于这等亵渎的乱伦，他也曾另作规定：须终身离乡漂泊，并补赎七年。

  1. Qui cum sorore fornicatur, XV. annos peniteat, eo modo quo superius de matre dicitur, sed et istud XV. alias in canone confirmavit; unde non absorde XV. anni ad matrem transeunt qui scribuntur.

凡与姊妹行淫者，须补赎十五年，方式如上文论母亲时所述。他在别处的教规中也确认了这十五年；因此，将所记载的十五年同样应用于与母亲行淫的情形，并非没有道理。

  1. Qui sepe fornicaverit, primus canon judicavit X. annos penitere; secundus canon VII.; sed pro infirmitate hominis, per consilium dixerunt III. annos penitere.

对于屡次行淫者，第一条教规裁定须补赎十年，第二条教规规定七年；但顾念人的软弱，经过商议，他们说须补赎三年。

  1. Si frater cum fratre naturali fornicaverit per commixtionem carnis, XV. annos ab omni carne abstineat.

若兄弟与亲兄弟发生肉体交合而行淫，须十五年禁食一切肉类。

  1. Si mater cum filio suo parvulo fornicationem imitatur, III. annos se abstineat a carne, et diem unum jejunet in ebdomada, id est, usque ad vesperum.

若母亲与自己的幼子模拟行淫，须禁食肉类三年，并每周禁食一日，即直到傍晚不进食。

  1. Qui inludetur fornicaria cogitatione, peniteat usque dum cogitatio superetur.

凡受淫念戏弄者，须行补赎，直到胜过这念头。

  1. Qui diligit feminam mente, veniam petat ab eo [a Deo] id est, de amore et amicitia si dixerit si non est susceptus ab ea, VII. dies peniteat."

凡心中爱恋妇人者，当向他［向上帝］求赦免；也就是说，若他表露爱情和亲昵之意，却未蒙她接纳，须补赎七日。

§86. 教会的惩罚：逐出教会、咒诅、禁令

弗里德里希·科伯 (Friedrich Kober，罗马天主教徒)：《依教会法原则阐述的教会绝罚》(Der Kirchenbann nach den Grundsätzen des canonischen Rechts dargestellt)。图宾根，1857年（560页）。同一作者：《教会教职人员的停职》(Die Suspension der Kirchendiener)。图宾根 (Tüb.)，1862年。

亨利·C. 利 (Henry C. Lea)：《逐出教会》(Excommunication)，载其《教会史研究》(Studies in Church History)（费城，1869年），第223–475页。

教会最严厉的惩罚是逐出教会、咒诅和禁令。在中世纪，人们相信教会掌管救恩，世俗权力又以法律的强力执行教会的谕令，这些便成了教阶制度手中可怕的武器。受罚者悔改，惩罚即告终止，随后便予以赦免。赦免的判决必须由宣布逐出教会的主教作出；但在临终之际（articulo mortis），任何司铎都可以施行赦免，条件是受赦者若康复，便须服从。

  1. 逐出教会，就是禁止领受圣礼，尤其是圣餐。在查理曼 (Charlemagne) 的领土内，这种惩罚还伴随着公民权利的剥夺，如不得诉诸世俗法庭，甚至遭受监禁和财产没收。主教可以将任何拒绝按教会法规服从的人逐出教会。但主教只能由教皇逐出教会，而地上没有任何权力能够将教皇逐出教会。6 这种判决往往伴随着对犯过者身体与灵魂的可怕咒诅。教皇既高居普通主教之上，在咒诅的技巧上也胜过他们，并且以骇人的亵渎施展这种技巧。例如，曾为皇帝亨利二世 (Henry II) 加冕（公元1014年）的本笃八世 (Benedict VIII)，将普罗旺斯伯爵威廉二世 (William II, Count of Provence) 手下几个肆无忌惮的封臣逐出教会；这些人企图以不敬的手侵占圣吉尔修道院 (monastery of St. Giles) 的财产。7 他以可怕的诅咒将他们交给撒但，虽然他大概认为，自己不过是在效法圣彼得定亚拿尼亚、撒非喇和行邪术的西门之罪的榜样。8

“顽梗的罪人，”利 (Lea) 说，“也许会藐视这样的诅咒，但其对信徒所起的作用必然难以言喻：在华丽而庄严的礼拜仪式中，主教由十二位手持燃烛的司铎环绕，郑重诵读可怕的言辞，以如此骇人的繁复咒诅，反复宣告作恶者及其所有后代都要受永远的痛苦；当这灭亡的判词达到高潮时，侍立的司铎同时将蜡烛掷在地上踏灭，象征一个人的灵魂在地狱永恒的黑夜中熄灭。此外，人们还怀着一种几乎确信无疑的期待，认为上天不会等待事态自然发展才证实这样宣布的判决，咒诅在今世也会与在来世一样有效。因此，即便有人不为属灵的恐怖所屈服，也会被那些可怕的故事吓倒；这些故事讲述审判如何临到胆敢藐视可畏咒诅的顽梗罪人。”

  1. 咒诅 (Anathema) 通常与逐出教会同义，即断绝教会的团契，剥夺教会的权利。但在较狭义的用法中，它指“大”绝罚，9 即禁止一切基督徒之间的交往，使犯过者成为不受法律保护的人；而“小”绝罚则只禁止领受圣礼。格拉提安 (Gratian) 和英诺森三世 (Innocent III) 作过这样的区分。咒诅的宣告伴随着更为庄严的仪式。335年的尼西亚会议 (Council of Nicaea) 咒诅了阿里乌派 (Arians)，1563年的特伦托会议 (Council of Trent) 则以对所有异端者的三次咒诅闭幕。

  2. 禁令 (Interdict)10 的范围遍及整个城镇、教区、地区或国家，使无辜者与有罪者一同受罚。它停止一切公开的宗教活动，甚至包括婚礼和葬礼；只有洗礼和临终傅油礼仍可施行，而且也只能闭门举行。它使整个国家笼罩在葬礼般的阴郁中，使人们战栗，等候最后的审判。这种非常的惩罚在第五世纪开始小规模实行。奥古斯丁曾公正地责备同任主教的奥克西利乌斯 (Auxilius)，因为他滥用权柄，因一家之主的过犯而将全家逐出教会；教皇大利奥 (Leo the Great) 也禁止对任何未参与犯罪的人施行这一惩罚。11 然而，十一至十三世纪的中世纪主教和教皇却不作此想，屡次诉诸这种迫使人服从的极端手段。蛮族习惯使家族或部落为个别成员所犯的罪负责，这为他们的做法提供了某种根据。

施行禁令最初的显著实例出现在法国。鲁昂主教普雷泰克斯塔图斯 (Pretextatus, Bishop of Rouen) 在祭坛前被残暴的王后弗蕾德贡达 (Fredegunda) 雇用的凶手杀害之后，巴约主教勒乌多瓦尔德 (Leudovald of Bayeux) 与其他主教商议，于586年关闭了鲁昂所有的教堂，剥夺民众从宗教所得的安慰，直到凶手被查明为止。12 拉昂的欣克马尔 (Hincmar of Laon) 曾对自己的教区施行禁令（869年），但兰斯的欣克马尔 (Hincmar of Rheims) 不赞同此举，并将禁令撤销。1031年利摩日（利穆赞）会议 (synod of Limoges [Limoisin]) 以禁令推行“上帝的和平” (Peace of God)，下列宣告曾在教堂中宣读：“我们将利摩日主教区内一切不听从主教劝告、不守和平的贵族（milites）逐出教会。愿他们和帮助他们的人都受咒诅，愿他们的武器和马匹也受咒诅！愿他们与该隐、大坍、亚比兰同得一分！正如现在灯火熄灭，他们在天使面前的喜乐也必毁灭，除非他们在死前悔改并作出补赎。”会议下令停止公开礼拜，撤去祭坛上的遮盖，移走十字架和饰物，禁止婚礼；只有教士、乞丐、异乡人及两岁以下的儿童可以获准安葬，只有临终者可以领圣餐；在贵族屈服以前，任何教士或平信徒都不得剃须。每日第三时辰，教堂应发出信号，召唤众人屈膝祈祷。人人都应穿丧服。整个禁令期间，都应作为连续禁食和自卑的时期来遵守。13

教皇用这种可怕的武器对付不服从的国王，为了自己的教阶野心，牺牲整个民族的属灵安慰。格列高利七世 (Gregory VII) 对格涅兹诺省 (Gnesen) 施行禁令，因为国王博莱斯瓦夫二世 (Bolislaw II) 亲手杀害了克拉科夫主教斯坦尼斯劳斯 (Stanislaus of Cracow)。亚历山大二世 (Alexander II) 对苏格兰施行禁令（1180年），因为国王拒绝接受教皇所派的主教，并将他逐出国境。英诺森三世于1200年将禁令悬在法国头上，因为国王腓力·奥古斯都 (Philip Augustus) 抛弃了合法妻子，与一个妾同居。14 同一位教皇又将此惩罚加于英国（1208年3月23日），希望迫使无地王约翰 (John [Lackland]) 就范。英国的禁令持续了六年多，其间除洗礼、告解和临终圣餐外，一切宗教仪式都被禁止。

禁令只有在教会拥有无限权力的中世纪才有可能实行。它们被频繁使用，又无法完全执行，威力便随之减弱；直到欧洲各国不再受制于祭司权术的管教，觉醒而意识到自己已经成人，禁令也就遭人蔑视，被一扫而空。

“悔改”或“悔罪”一词，不足以译出相应的希腊文 metanoia；后者意指心意的根本改变，或从罪恶生活转向敬虔生活的归信。其中，消极的一面是在依着上帝意思的忧愁中离弃罪（狭义的悔改），积极的一面则是凭信心转向基督，立志跟从他。15 施洗约翰和耶稣基督的传道，都是从呼召人作这种意义上的悔改开始的。16

在拉丁教会中，悔改的观念被外在化，等同于某些为赎罪而自卑或自罚的外在行为。除极其严重的过犯外，在教会面前所作的公开补赎，在第七或第八世纪逐渐废止，由私下的补赎和告解取代。17 1215年，教皇英诺森三世主持的拉特兰会议 (Lateran Council) 规定，每个天主教基督徒都必须每年至少一次向本堂司铎告解。18

补赎，包括附耳告解和司铎赦罪，被提升为处理受洗后所犯罪行的圣礼。其理论基础由教父（德尔图良和居普良）奠定，由经院学者完成，并获罗马教会认可。这一理论认为，洗礼使过去的罪完全得赦，却不赦免此后的罪；它使人免于永远的定罪，却不免除暂时的刑罚，而这种刑罚最终体现于死亡或炼狱。补赎被描述为一种“艰苦的洗礼”；特伦托会议宣告，对于受洗后跌倒的人，补赎是得救所必需的，正如对于尚未重生的人，洗礼是必需的一样。19

补赎圣礼与司铎赦罪包含三个要素：心中的痛悔、口中的告白、以善行作出补赎。20 在这些条件下，司铎赐予赦免，这不只是宣告性的行为，更是司法性的行为。所要求的善行尤其是禁食和施舍。前往耶路撒冷、罗马、图尔 (Tours)、孔波斯特拉 (Compostella) 及其他圣地朝圣，也是人们喜爱的补赎方式。彼得·达米安 (Peter Damiani) 提倡自愿鞭笞自己，以为平息上帝忿怒的手段。在民众心目中，这些虔敬操练涵盖了补赎的全部意义。衡量敬虔的标准，是善行的数量，而非品格的优劣。

这里还须提到另一种与补赎密切相关的中世纪制度。西方教会热心防止暴力和流血，因而赞成蛮族以金钱赔偿代替对过犯施加惩罚的习惯，这原是对的；但将这一习惯应用于宗教领域，却是错的。于是，金钱可以代替禁食和其他补赎行为，并被赋予赎罪的效力。这一习惯似乎源于英国教会，不久便传遍欧洲大陆。21 它蜕变成一种常规交易，成为增加教会和修道院财产的丰厚来源。

这就是所谓赎罪券 (indulgences) 的起源，即以缴纳金钱，并以痛悔和祈祷为条件，获得小罪的赦免。经院学说为这种做法辩护，认为圣徒超出本分的善功构成了一座额外功德与额外赏报的宝库，由教皇掌管。因此，赎罪券便具有一种特殊含义：教皇从圣徒满溢的功德宝库中施予特赦或赦罪；这种权力甚至被扩展到使炼狱中的亡者受益。22

主教和大主教可以在各自的教区内颁发赎罪券，教皇则可以向所有天主教徒颁发。前者从事零售，后者从事批发。教皇颁发赎罪券的最早事例出现于第九世纪，帕斯卡尔一世 (Paschalis I.) 和约翰八世 (John VIII.) 将其赐给那些为保卫教会而战死的人。1046年，格列高利六世 (Gregory VI.) 应许将其赐给所有捐款修缮罗马各教堂的人。乌尔班二世 (Urban II.) 在克莱蒙会议 (Clermont，1095年) 上，“凭使徒之首彼得和保罗的权柄”，向十字军战士提供全大赦，作为前往圣地的赏报。此后，每一次讨伐穆罕默德教徒和异端的十字军远征，都重申了同样的应许。教皇们发现，这是扩张权势和充实府库的便利手段。于是，颁发赎罪券成为一种定期实行的制度。在利奥十世 (Leo X.) 任内，台彻尔 (Tetzel) 为圣彼得教堂筹款所进行的亵渎而可耻的交易，使其流弊登峰造极；然而，上帝的护理却使这一切转而促成宗教改革，使人重新回到圣经所教导的悔改观念。

The discipline of the Catholic church is based on the power of the keys intrusted to the apostles and their successors, and includes the excommunication and restoration of delinquent members. It was originally a purely spiritual jurisdiction, but after the establishment of Christianity as the national religion, it began to affect also the civil and temporal condition of the subjects of punishment. It obtained a powerful hold upon the public mind from the universal belief of the middle ages that the visible church, centering in the Roman papacy, was by divine appointment the dispenser of eternal salvation, and that expulsion from her communion, unless followed by repentance and restoration, meant eternal damnation. No heresy or sect ever claimed this power.

Discipline was very obnoxious to the wild and independent spirit of the barbaric races. It was exercised by the bishop through synodical courts, which were held annually in the dominions of Charlemagne for the promotion of good morals. Charlemagne ordered the bishops to visit their parishes once a year, and to inquire into cases of incest, patricide, fratricide, adultery, and other vices contrary to the laws of God.(1) Similar directions were given by Synods in Spain and England. The more extensive dioceses were divided into several archdeaconries. The archdeacons represented the bishops, and, owing to this close connection, they possessed a power and jurisdiction superior to that of the priests. Seven members of the congregation were entrusted with a supervision, and had to report to the inquisitorial court on the state of religion and morals. Offences both ecclesiastical and civil were punished at once with fines, fasting, pilgrimages, scourging, imprisonment. The civil authorities aided the bishops in the exercise of discipline. Public offences were visited with public penance; private offences were confessed to the priest, who immediately granted absolution on certain conditions.

The discipline of the Latin church in the middle ages is laid down in the so-called "Penitential Books."(2) They regulate the order of penitence, and prescribe specific punishments for certain sins, as drunkenness, fornication, avarice, perjury, homicide, heresy, idolatry. The material is mostly derived from the writings of the fathers, and from the synodical canons of Ancyra (314), Neocaesarea (314), Nicaea (325), Gangra (362), and of the North African, Frankish, and Spanish councils down to the seventh century. The common object of these Penitentials is to enforce practical duties and to extirpate the ferocious and licentious passions of heathenism. They present a very dark picture of the sins of the flesh. They kept alive the sense of a moral government of God, who punishes every violation of his law, but they lowered the sense of guilt by fostering the pernicious notion that sin may be expiated by mechanical exercises and by the payment of a sum of money.

There were many such books, British, Irish, Frankish, Spanish, and Roman. The best known are the Anglo-Saxon penitentials of the seventh and eighth centuries, especially that of Theodore, archbishop of Canterbury (669–690). He was a Greek by birth, of Tarsus in Cilicia, and reduced the disciplinary rules of the East and West to a system. He was not the direct author of the book which bears his name, but it was drawn up under his direction, published during his life-time and by his authority, and contains his decisions in answer to various questions of a priest named Eoda and other persons on the subject of penance and the whole range of ecclesiastical discipline. The genuine text has recently been brought to light from early MSS. by the combined labors of German and English scholarship.(3) The introduction and the book itself are written in barbarous Latin. Traces of the Greek training of Theodore may be seen in the references to St. Basil and to Greek practices. Next to Theodore’s collection there are Penitentials under the name of the venerable Bede (d. 735), and of Egbert, archbishop of York (d. 767).(4)

The earliest Frankish penitential is the work of Columban, the Irish missionary (d. 615). He was a severe monastic disciplinarian and gave prominence to corporal punishment among the penalties for offences. The Cummean Penitential (Poenit. Cummeani) is of Scotch-Irish origin, and variously assigned to Columba of Iona (about 597), to Cumin, one of his disciples, or to Cummean, who died in Columban’s monastery at Bobbio (after 711). Haltigar, bishop of Cambray, in the ninth century (about 829) published a "Roman Penitential," professedly derived from Roman archives, but in great part from Columban, and Frankish sources. An earlier work which bears the name "Poenitentiale Romanum," from the first part of the eighth century, has a more general character, but its precise origin is uncertain. The term "Roman" was used to designate the quality of a class of Penitentials which enjoyed a more than local authority.(5) Rabanus Maurus (d. 855) prepared a "Liber Poenitentitae" at the request of the archbishop Otgar of Mayence (841). Almost every diocese had its own book of the kind, but the spirit and the material were substantially the same.

§ 86. Ecclesiastical Punishments. Excommunication, Anathema, Interdict.

Friedrich Kober (R.C.): Der Kirchenbann nach den Grundsätzen des canonischen Rechts dargestellt. Tübingen 1857 (560 pages). By the same author: Die Suspension der Kirchendiener. Tüb. 1862.

Henry C. Lea: Excommunication, in his Studies in Church History (Philadelphia 1869), p. 223–475.

The severest penalties of the church were excommunication, anathema, and interdict. They were fearful weapons in the hands of the hierarchy during the middle ages, when the church was believed to control salvation, and when the civil power enforced her decrees by the strong arm of the law. The punishment ceases with repentance, which is followed by absolution. The sentence of absolution must proceed from the bishop who pronounced the sentence of excommunication; but in articulo mortis every priest can absolve on condition of obedience in case of recovery.

  1. Excommunication was the exclusion from the sacraments, especially the communion. In the dominions of Charlemagne it was accompanied with civil disabilities, as exclusion from secular tribunals, and even with imprisonment and seizure of property. A bishop could excommunicate any one who refused canonical obedience. But a bishop could only be excommunicated by the pope, and the pope by no power on earth.(6) The sentence was often accompanied with awful curses upon the bodies and souls of the offender. The popes, as they towered above ordinary bishops, surpassed them also in the art of cursing, and exercised it with shocking profanity. Thus Benedict VIII., who crowned Emperor Henry II. (a.d. 1014), excommunicated some reckless vassals of William II., Count of Provence, who sought to lay unhallowed hands upon the property of the monastery of St. Giles,(7) and consigned them to Satan with terrible imprecations, although be probably thought he was only following St. Peter’s example in condemning Ananias and Sapphira, and Simon Magus.(8)

"Hardened sinners" (says Lea) "might despise such imprecations, but their effect on believers was necessarily unutterable, when, amid the gorgeous and impressive ceremonial of worship, the bishop, surrounded by twelve priests bearing flaming candles, solemnly recited the awful words which consigned the evil-doer and all his generation to eternal torment with such fearful amplitude and reduplication of malediction, and as the sentence of perdition came to its climax, the attending priests simultaneously cast their candles to the ground and trod them out, as a symbol of the quenching of a human soul in the eternal night of hell. To this was added the expectation, amounting almost to a certainty, that Heaven would not wait for the natural course of events to confirm the judgment thus pronounced, but that the maledictions would be as effective in this world as in the next. Those whom spiritual terrors could not subdue thus were daunted by the fearful stories of the judgment overtaking the hardened sinner who dared to despise the dread anathema."

  1. The Anathema is generally used in the same sense as excommunication or separation from church communion and church privileges. But in a narrower sense, it means the "greater" excommunication,(9) which excludes from all Christian intercourse and makes the offender an outlaw; while the "minor" excommunication excludes only from the sacrament. Such a distinction was made by Gratian and Innocent III. The anathema was pronounced with more solemn ceremonies. The Council of Nicaea, 335, anathematized the Arians, and the Council of Trent, 1563, closed with three anathemas on all heretics.

  2. The Interdict(10) extended over a whole town or diocese or district or country, and involved the innocent with the guilty. It was a suspension of religion in public exercise, including even the rites of marriage and burial; only baptism and extreme unction could be performed, and they only with closed doors. It cast the gloom of a funeral over a country, and made people tremble in expectation of the last judgment. This exceptional punishment began in a small way in the fifth century. St. Augustin justly reproved Auxilius, a brother bishop, who abused his power by excommunicating a whole family for the offence of the head, and Pope Leo the Great forbade to enforce the penalty on any who was not a partner in the crime.(11) But the bishops and popes of the middle ages, from the eleventh to the thirteenth century, thought otherwise, and resorted repeatedly to this extreme remedy of enforcing obedience. They had some basis for it in the custom of the barbarians to hold the family or tribe responsible for crimes committed by individual members.

The first conspicuous examples of inflicting the Interdict occurred in France. Bishop Leudovald of Bayeux, after consulting with his brother bishops, closed in 586 all the churches of Rouen and deprived the people of the consolations of religion until the murderer of Pretextatus, Bishop of Rouen, who was slain at the altar by a hireling of the savage queen Fredegunda, should be discovered.(12) Hincmar of Laon inflicted the interdict on his diocese (869), but Hincmar of Rheims disapproved of it and removed it. The synod of Limoges (Limoisin), in 1031, enforced the Peace of God by the interdict in these words which were read in the church: "We excommunicate all those noblemen (milites) in the bishopric of Limoges who disobey the exhortations of their bishop to hold the Peace. Let them and their helpers be accursed, and let their weapons and horses be accursed! Let their lot be with Cain, Dathan, and Abiram! And as now the lights are extinguished, so their joy in the presence of angels shall be destroyed, unless they repent and make satisfaction before dying." The Synod ordered that public worship be closed, the altars laid bare, crosses and ornaments removed, marriages forbidden; only clergymen, beggars, strangers and children under two years could be buried, and only the dying receive the communion; no clergyman or layman should be shaved till the nobles submit. A signal in the church on the third hour of the day should call all to fall on their knees to pray. All should be dressed in mourning. The whole period of the interdict should be observed as a continued fast and humiliation.(13)

The popes employed this fearful weapon against disobedient kings, and sacrificed the spiritual comforts of whole nations to their hierarchical ambition. Gregory VII. laid the province of Gnesen under the interdict, because King Bolislaw II. had murdered bishop Stanislaus of Cracow with his own hand. Alexander II. applied it to Scotland (1180), because the king refused a papal bishop and expelled him from the country. Innocent III. suspended it over France (1200), because king Philip Augustus had cast off his lawful wife and lived with a concubine.(14) The same pope inflicted this punishment upon England (March 23, 1208), hoping to bring King John (Lackland) to terms. The English interdict lasted over six years during which all religious rites were forbidden except baptism, confession, and the viaticum.

Interdicts were only possible in the middle ages when the church had unlimited power. Their frequency and the impossibility of full execution diminished their power until they fell into contempt and were swept out of existence as the nations of Europe outgrew the discipline of priestcraft and awoke to a sense of manhood.

The word repentance or penitence is an insufficient rendering for the corresponding Greek metanoia, which means a radical change of mind or conversion from a sinful to a godly life, and includes, negatively, a turning away from sin in godly sorrow (repentance in the narrower sense) and, positively, a turning to Christ by faith with a determination to follow him.(15) The call to repent in this sense was the beginning of the preaching both of John the Baptist, and of Jesus Christ.(16)

In the Latin church the idea of repentance was externalized and identified with certain outward acts of self-abasement or self-punishment for the expiation of sin. The public penance before the church went out of use during the seventh or eighth century, except for very gross offences, and was replaced by private penance and confession.(17) The Lateran Council of 1215 under Pope Innocent III. made it obligatory upon every Catholic Christian to confess to his parish priest at least once a year.(18)

Penance, including auricular confession and priestly absolution, was raised to the dignity of a sacrament for sins committed after baptism. The theory on which it rests was prepared by the fathers (Tertullian and Cyprian), completed by the schoolmen, and sanctioned by the Roman church. It is supposed that baptism secures perfect remission of past sins, but not of subsequent sins, and frees from eternal damnation, but not from temporal punishment, which culminates in death or in purgatory. Penance is described as a "laborious kind of baptism," and is declared by the Council of Trent to be necessary to salvation for those who have fallen after baptism, as baptism is necessary for those who have not yet been regenerated.(19)

The sacrament of penance and priestly, absolution includes three elements: contrition of the heart, confession by the mouth, satisfaction by good works.(20) On these conditions the priest grants absolution, not simply by a declaratory but by a judicial act. The good works required are especially fasting and almsgiving. Pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome, Tours, Compostella, and other sacred places were likewise favorite satisfactions. Peter Damiani recommended voluntary self-flagellation as a means to propitiate God. These pious exercises covered in the popular mind the whole idea of penance. Piety was measured by the quantity of good works rather than by quality of character.

Another mediaeval institution must here be mentioned which is closely connected with penance. The church in the West, in her zeal to prevent violence and bloodshed, rightly favored the custom of the barbarians to substitute pecuniary compensation for punishment of an offence, but wrongly applied this custom to the sphere of religion. Thus money, might be substituted for fasting and other satisfactions, and was clothed with an atoning efficacy. This custom seems to have proceeded from the church of England, and soon spread over the continent.(21) It degenerated into a regular traffic, and became a rich source for the increase of ecclesiastical and monastic property.

Here is the origin of the indulgences so called, that is the remission of venial sins by the payment of money and on condition of contrition and prayer. The practice was justified by the scholastic theory that the works of supererogation of the saints constitute a treasury of extra-merit and extra-reward which is under the control of the pope. Hence indulgence assumed the special meaning of papal dispensation or remission of sin from the treasury of the overflowing merits of saints, and this power was extended even to the benefit of the dead in purgatory.(22)

Indulgences may be granted by bishops and archbishops in their dioceses, and by the pope to all Catholics. The former dealt with it in retail, the latter in wholesale. The first instances of papal indulgence occur in the ninth century under Paschalis I. and John VIII. who granted it to those who had fallen in war for the defence of the church. Gregory VI. in 1046 promised it to all who sent contributions for the repair of the churches in Rome. Urban II., at the council of Clermont (1095), offered to the crusaders "by the authority of the princes of the Apostles, Peter and Paul," plenary indulgence as a reward for a journey to the Holy Land. The same offer was repeated in every crusade against the Mohammedans and heretics. The popes found it a convenient means for promoting their power and filling their treasury. Thus the granting of indulgences became a periodical institution. Its abuses culminated in the profane and shameful traffic of Tetzel under Leo X. for the benefit of St. Peter’s church, but were overruled in the Providence of God for the Reformation and a return to the biblical idea of repentance.
