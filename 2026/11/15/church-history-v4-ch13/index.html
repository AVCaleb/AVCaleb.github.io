§ 135. 中世纪早期的学术特征

这一时期圣学的主要特征，是忠实恪守传统；它保存了古代古典文学与基督教文献的遗存，并将其移植到新的土壤上。从西罗马帝国 (West Roman Empire) 覆亡（476年）至希尔德布兰德 (Hildebrand) 时代（1049–1085年）之间的六个世纪，是从衰败的异教文明向新的基督教文明、从教父神学向经院神学过渡的时期。这是一个黑暗的时期，却已有黎明将近的迹象。教父已经逝去，经院学者尚未诞生。所能做的最好之事，就是保存过去的遗产，以造福未来。创造力已经耗尽，代之而起的是模仿与编纂。勤勉的文献工作取代了独立研究。

希腊教会 (Greek church) 保持着与古典学术及教父学术的联系，紧守尼西亚教父 (Nicene fathers) 和七次大公会议 (oecumenical councils) 的教导。拉丁教会 (Latin church) 则俯首于圣奥古斯丁 (St. Augustin) 和圣哲罗姆 (St. Jerome) 的权威之下。东方较富于学识；西方则较富于实践的活力，主要表现在宣教领域。希腊教会回首过去，直至今日仍坚守第八世纪的教义立场；拉丁教会展望未来，虽曾经历蒙昧的漫漫长夜，却从新鲜血液中汲取了新的力量。希腊教会所呈现的，是静止中的古代基督教；中世纪的拉丁教会所呈现的，则是朝向近代前进的基督教。

§ 136. 东方教会的学术

东方教会在希腊语言知识上优于西方教会，因此得以直接研读希腊文新约、希腊古典著作及希腊教父著作；但另一方面，她又不得不承受穆罕默德教徒的入侵，以及专制宫廷的阴谋与干预所带来的苦难。

教父学术最繁盛的中心——亚历山大里亚 (Alexandria) 和安提阿 (Antioch)——因伊斯兰教的征服而丧失。亚历山大里亚的庞大图书馆，奉欧麦尔 (Omar) 之命被焚毁（638年）；他的理由是：“如果这些希腊人的著作与上帝之书（《古兰经》〔Koran〕）相合，它们便毫无用处，无须保存；如果不合，它们便是有害的，应当销毁。”1 然而在第八世纪，撒拉逊人 (Saracens) 自己也开始钻研学问，翻译希腊作家的著作，并在巴格达 (Bagdad)、开罗 (Cairo) 和科尔多瓦 (Cordova) 建立庞大的藏书。阿拉伯学术的时代延续了约五百年，直至蒙古人 (Moguls) 入侵。它通过科尔多瓦的阿威罗伊 (Averroës，卒于1198年)——亚里士多德 (Aristotle) 著作的译者和注释者——的著作，对教会学术，尤其对经院哲学的发展，产生了激励作用。

君士坦丁堡 (Constantinople) 是中世纪希腊教会学术活动的中心。大公会议在这里或其近郊（迦克墩〔Chalcedon〕、尼西亚〔Nicaea〕）举行；这里有学者、图书馆、皇帝的赞助，以及从事研究的一切便利条件。许多图书馆曾遭毁坏，却总能重新建立。2 帖撒罗尼迦 (Thessalonica) 和阿索斯山 (Mount Athos) 也是重要的学术中心，尤其在第十二世纪。

拉丁语是拜占庭 (Byzantine) 宫廷的官方语言；查士丁尼 (Justinian) 凭借贝利撒留 (Belisarius) 的勇武，在分离六十年之后重新取得了古罗马的统治权（536年），傲然宣称自己是罗马人的皇帝，并以拉丁文颁布其法典。但希腊语始终是、也一直保持为民众、文学、哲学与神学的语言。

第九世纪，古典学术在宫廷的赞助下复兴。凯撒巴尔达斯 (Caesar Bardas，860–866年)、马其顿人巴西尔一世 (Basilius I. the Macedonian，867–886年)、本身也是作家的哲学家利奥六世 (Leo VI. the Philosopher，886–911年)，以及同样也是作家的紫衣贵胄君士坦丁七世 (Constantine VII. Porphyrogenitus，911–959年) 的统治，标志着拜占庭文学最繁荣的时期。科穆宁家族 (Comneni) 从1057至1185年间支撑着日趋衰落的帝国，继续赞助文学；皇后欧多基娅 (Eudocia) 和公主安娜·科穆宁娜 (Anna Comnena) 则致力于修辞艺术与哲学研究。

即使在十字军东征的混乱和帝国遭遇的种种灾难之中，对学问的热爱仍未消歇；当君士坦丁堡终于落入土耳其人手中时，希腊学术避难于西方，点燃了文艺复兴的火焰，并成为为宗教改革预备道路的一个重要因素。

拜占庭文学呈现出浩瀚的学识，却缺乏一种赋予其生气、统摄并组织它的天才。吉本 (Gibbon) 说，3 其中不免有些修辞上的夸张：“君士坦丁堡的希腊人，以他们毫无生气的双手握着教父们的财富，却未能承继创造并丰富这份神圣遗产的精神：他们阅读，他们赞美，他们编纂；但他们萎靡的灵魂，似乎既不能思想，也不能行动。在十个世纪的流转中，没有一项发现提高人类的尊严或增进人类的幸福。古代的思辨体系没有增添一个新观念；一代代耐心的门徒，又依次成为下一代奴性十足者的独断教师。没有一部历史、哲学或文学作品，能凭借文体或情思、独创的想象、甚至成功的模仿所具有的内在美，而免于湮没……希腊教会的领袖谦卑自足，只知赞叹和抄录古代的权威之言；学校或讲坛也未产生任何足以与亚他那修 (Athanasius) 和金口约翰 (Chrysostom) 齐名的人物。”

神学争论发展了辩证的技巧、对形而上学精微分别的偏爱，以及对理论上的正统的过高估计，却以牺牲实际的敬虔为代价。基督一志论争论使基督论信条有所增补；圣像破坏运动的争论则决定了公共礼拜的性质，以及宗教与艺术的关系。

东方最有才华的神学家，是第七世纪的宣信者马克西穆斯 (Maximus Confessor)、第八世纪的大马士革的约翰 (John of Damascus)，以及第九世纪的佛提乌斯 (Photius)。马克西穆斯是基督一志论争中的英雄，是一位敏锐而深邃的思想家，也是第一个利用伪狄奥尼修斯哲学来支持神秘主义正统信仰的人。大马士革的约翰是圣像崇拜的捍卫者；他将正统教父，尤其是卡帕多西亚三大教父——巴西尔 (Basil)、拿先斯的贵格利 (Gregory of Nazianzum) 和尼撒的贵格利 (Gregory of Nyssa)——的教义系统化，写成一部不朽的神学著作；这部著作在希腊教会中，至今仍享有与托马斯·阿奎那 (Thomas Aquinas) 的《神学大全》(Summa) 在拉丁教会中同等的权威。佛提乌斯是教皇尼古拉 (Nicolas) 的对手，也是他那个时代最伟大的学者；他以独立的判断，阅读并消化了古代异教与基督教有关语文学、哲学、神学、教会法、历史、医学及一般文学的所有书籍。在知识的广博与著述的丰赡方面，米迦勒·普塞洛斯 (Michael Psellus，卒于1106年) 是他的继承者。

第十世纪的厄库梅尼乌斯 (Oecumenius)、第十一世纪的狄奥菲拉克特 (Theophylact)，以及第十二世纪的欧提米乌斯·齐加贝努斯 (Euthymius Zygabenus)，都致力于释经。他们编成了称为“连锁注释” (Catenae) 的珍贵释经汇编。4 西缅·梅塔弗拉斯特斯 (Simeon Metaphrastes，约900年) 为一百二十二位圣徒撰写了传奇传记和颂词。第十一世纪的苏伊达斯 (Suidas) 编纂了一部《词典》，其中包含许多宝贵的语文学和历史资料。5 拜占庭史家狄奥法尼斯 (Theophanes)、辛凯卢斯 (Syncellus)、凯德雷努斯 (Cedrenus)、文法家利奥 (Leo Grammaticus) 等人，记述了这个缓慢衰落的帝国中的政治与教会事件。第十二世纪最杰出的学者，是帖撒罗尼迦大主教欧斯塔修斯 (Eustathius)；他以注释荷马 (Homer) 最为著名，但作为神学家、教会领袖和修道制度的改革者，也理当占有崇高的地位。

真实的与虚构的狄奥尼修斯

神秘思辨的倾向，主要藉着那些融合新柏拉图主义 (Neo-Platonism) 与基督教的著作而得到维系和滋养；这些著作以亚略巴古的狄奥尼修斯 (Dionysius Areopagita) 的名义流传，此人是圣保罗在雅典所带领归信的一位杰出人物（《使徒行传》17:34）。依照第二世纪的一项传统，他是雅典 (Athens) 的第一任主教。6 第九世纪，法国人开始接触这些据称出自他的著作时，便把他与圣德尼 (St. Denis) 混为一人；后者是巴黎的第一任主教和法国的主保圣徒，其生卒年代比那位亚略巴古人约晚二百年。7 这样，藉着一个显而易见的时代错置，他便成了雅典与巴黎之间、希腊哲学与基督教神学之间的纽带，并获得了近乎使徒的权威。他是佚名作者的身后影响以及死者支配生者之力量的最显著例证之一。数百年来，他被尊为神学家之首。在希腊与拉丁教会看来，他代表着福音的奥秘智慧，以及信仰与理性、天上与地上教阶制度之间的神秘和谐。

伪狄奥尼修斯是伪伊西多尔 (Pseudo-Isidor) 在哲学上的对应者：两者都是为大公教会体制的利益而作的虔诚伪造，一者关乎神学，一者关乎教会政制；两者都反映了中世纪基督教缺乏批判精神的特征；两者都因人们相信其年代古老，而获得了远超其内在价值的虚假重要性。洛伦提乌斯·瓦拉 (Laurentius Valla)、伊拉斯谟 (Erasmus) 和枢机主教卡耶坦 (Cardinal Cajetan)，都曾怀疑《亚略巴古文集》(Areopagitica) 的真伪；然而，直到第十七世纪，历史批判的火炬才最终驱散了将伪狄奥尼修斯、那位由使徒带领归信的人，以及法国的主保圣徒视为同一人的迷误。自那时起，他的著作便失去了权威和吸引力；但在文学珍品之中，以及最引人注目的神秘哲学体系之中，它们仍将永远占有显著的地位。

作者身份

这些作品的真正作者是谁？作者仅被称为狄奥尼修斯，而且只出现过一次。8 他屡次提到一位不为人知的希罗狄奥斯 (Hierotheos)，称他为自己的老师；但也称颂“神圣的保罗”，视他为两人共同的属灵导师，并向那些具有使徒时代姓名的人物致书，如提摩太 (Timothy)、提多、该犹 (Caius)、坡旅甲 (Polycarp) 和圣约翰。他说自己曾与希罗狄奥斯、主的兄弟雅各 (ἀδελφόθεος*)，以及“受默示的众使徒中居首而最尊贵的领袖”彼得一同前往，瞻仰那位“生命的开端与上帝的承受者”（马利亚）的遗体；在那场合，希罗狄奥斯以出神的颂歌抒发了他们的感受。显然，他要么生活在使徒时期及其环境中，要么凭想象把自己移回了那个时代。9 前一种可能是不成立的。他浮夸的文风，对较晚人物（如安提阿的伊格那丢和亚历山大里亚的革利免）的提及，对新柏拉图主义观念的熟悉，既诉诸圣经又诉诸教会的“古老传统” (ἀρχαῖα παράδοσις*)，以及他所预设的周密的教会政制与礼仪体系，都清楚证明他属于使徒以后的时代。尤西比乌斯 (Eusebius)、哲罗姆，以及533年以前的任何教会作家，都不知道他。那一年，在查士丁尼一世 (Justinian I.) 治下的君士坦丁堡，正统主教与异端的塞维鲁派 (Severians) 举行会谈时，他的著作才首次被提及。10 塞维鲁派援引这些著作，作为支持其一性论 (Monophysitic) 基督论、反对迦克墩会议的权威；面对这些著作不为人知的异议，他们回答说，亚历山大里亚的区利罗曾用这些著作反驳聂斯托利派 (Nestorians)。若果真如此，这些著作必定在区利罗去世的444年以前已经存在；然而，在区利罗的著作中却找不到任何痕迹。另一方面，狄奥尼修斯以第五世纪的基督论争为前提，表现出对一性论观点的倾向，并熟悉新柏拉图主义最后而最优秀的代表人物，尤其是于公元485年卒于雅典的普罗克洛斯 (Proclus)。两者如此相似，以致狄奥尼修斯的崇拜者反而指控普罗克洛斯抄袭。11 因此，这位作者是一名基督教新柏拉图主义者，于第五世纪末或第六世纪初在希腊 (Greece) 或埃及 (Egypt) 写作，并藉着一种文学虚构，为自己的宗教思辨披上了雅典第一任基督教主教的名义与权威。12

同样，伪革利免著作 (pseudo-Clementine writings) 也被归于罗马 (Rome) 的第一任主教名下。

伪狄奥尼修斯的际遇

伪狄奥尼修斯最初出现时，是为基督位格中只有一个本性和一个意志的异端教义效力的。13 但他很快便更得正统神学家的青睐。第六世纪的斯基托波利斯的约翰 (Johannes Scythopolitanus)，以及第七世纪的圣宣信者马克西穆斯，都为他作过注释。大马士革的约翰经常引述他，奉为崇高的权威。甚至佛提乌斯，虽以批评家的身份怀疑这些著作的真实性，仍将他列入伟大的教会教师之中，并赞扬其思想的深度。14

在西方，伪狄奥尼修斯的著作约于590年首次受到教皇格列高利一世 (Gregory I.) 的注意；他可能是在君士坦丁堡担任使节期间接触到这些著作的。教皇哈德良一世 (Hadrian I.) 在致查理曼的一封信中也提到了它们。827年，皇帝口吃者米迦勒二世 (Michael II. the Stammerer) 将一部抄本送给虔诚者路易 (Louis the Pious)。这部抄本在安息于圣德尼 (St. Denis) 的那位圣徒之庆日的前夕抵达该地，随后附近竟发生了不下十九起神迹医治。它们自然唤起了人们对法国主保圣徒的记忆，并被认定为他的作品。皇帝命圣德尼的修道院院长希尔杜因 (Hilduin) 将其译成拉丁文；但他的学力不足以胜任这项任务。西方最优秀的希腊文学者约翰·司各脱·爱留根纳 (John Scotus Erigena)，应秃头查理 (Charles the Bald) 之请，于850年左右完成了附有评注的直译本，并赞扬作者“无论因其年代古老，还是因其所论天上奥秘之崇高，都同样值得尊敬”。15 教皇尼古拉一世 (Nicolas I.) 抱怨，这部作品没有“依照教会的惯例”送交他批准（861年）；但数年以后，教皇图书馆馆长阿纳斯塔修斯 (Anastasius) 却对它大加赞扬（约865年）。

《亚略巴古文集》激发了直觉与思辨的心智倾向，成为经院神学和神秘神学发展的一个重要因素。圣维克多的雨果 (Hugo of St. Victor)、伦巴德的彼得 (Peter the Lombard)、阿尔伯特大士 (Albertus Magnus)、托马斯·阿奎那 (Thomas Aquinas)、罗伯特·格罗斯泰斯特 (Robert Grosseteste)，以及加尔都西会的狄奥尼修斯 (Dionysius Carthusianus)，都为这些著作写过注释，并从中汲取灵感，用于自己的著述。16 意大利文艺复兴时期的柏拉图主义者也同样受到它们的影响。

但丁 (Dante) 把狄奥尼修斯置于太阳天中的神学家之列：

“接着你看见那盏明灯的光辉，

他在下界肉身中时，最深地洞察了

天使的本性及其职分。”17

路德 (Luther) 称他为梦想家，而这也成了巴黎索邦学院 (Sorbonne) 所谴责的路德异端观点之一。

各部著作

现存的狄奥尼修斯著作，包括写给他“同任长老”的提摩太的四篇论著，即：1)《天上等级论》(περὶ τῆς οὐρανίας ἱεραρχίας*)。2)《教会等级论》(περὶ τῆς ἐκκλησιαστικῆς ἱεραρχίας*)。3)《神名论》(περὶ θείων ὀνομάτων*)。4)《神秘神学》(περὶ μυστικῆς θεολογίας*)。此外还有写给使徒时期各个人物的十封书信。18

狄奥尼修斯的体系

这些著作显出同一作者的手笔，也呈现同一套神秘象征体系，其中新柏拉图主义 (Neo-Platonism) 与基督教交织在一起。希腊哲学的最后阶段，此前一直敌视教会，在这里却被用来为教会服务。将二者联结起来的观念，是无限者的渐进启示、等级性的三联结构、对恶的否定性理解，以及人追求与超越的上帝神秘合一的努力。这一体系与亚历山大里亚的斐洛 (Philo of Alexandria) 的希腊—犹太神学相对应；斐洛同样将柏拉图哲学与摩西宗教糅合起来。亚略巴古的这位作者与斐洛都披着哲学的外衣讲授神学；二者都诉诸圣经、传统和理性；都越过圣经的字句和历史的事实，寻求更深的象征和寓意；也都以外来的成分掺杂启示的真理。但斐洛局限于旧约，不涉及当时尚未写成的新约；而亚略巴古这位作者的体系，则是一种基督教哲学。

亚略巴古的这位作者怀着敬畏，攀登形而上学与宗教思辨的高峰，探测其深渊，给人以洞见深邃、灵性崇高的印象；因此，他对中世纪伟大的经院学者和神秘主义者具有如此强大的吸引力。但他的文字充斥着重复；他用高亢的辞藻掩饰思想的贫乏；他使用希腊秘仪的术语；19他的文体矫揉造作、浮夸、晦涩而单调。

神性的统一与宇宙的等级秩序，是亚略巴古这位作者的两个主导观念。他从神圣的统一，经由一连串显现，下降到多样性，又重新上升，达到与上帝的神秘合一。我们可以说，他所依据的经文就是圣保罗的这句话：“万有都是本于上帝，倚靠上帝，归于上帝。”（《罗马书》11:36）

他的出发点是新柏拉图主义的神性观：上帝是超越一切本体与存在的存在者，20却又是一切存在的起点和终点；他不可知，却又是一切理性与知识的源泉；他无名而不可言说，却又给万物命名；他是单纯的统一，却又造成一切多样性。他将上帝描述为“三个位格的统一，以慈爱的护理遍及万物，从超乎诸天的本体，直到地上最末的事物；他是一切存在者的起源和原因，超越一切起源，以无限的怀抱，超然地包容万物”。我们若要认识上帝，就必须走出自身，完全融入他。一切存在都以某种流溢的方式出于上帝，又向上趋归于他。

世界构成双重的教阶制度 (hierarchy)，按他的定义，就是“一种神圣的秩序、知识和活动或能力，尽可能与神相似，并按照所获赐的神圣光照，被提升到效法上帝的境地”。有两种教阶制度，一在天上，一在地上，各有三个等级，每个等级又各分三类。

天上的、亦即超尘世的教阶制度，由三个等级的天使性存在者组成：1）宝座者、基路伯和撒拉弗，直接侍立在上帝面前；2）掌权者、有能者和主治者；3）天使（狭义而言）、天使长和执政者。21第一等级由上帝光照、洁净并成全，第二等级由第一等级如此施行，第三等级则由第二等级如此施行。

地上的、亦即教会的教阶制度，是天上教阶制度的映照，也是训练我们尽可能与上帝密切相交的学校。它的各等级构成天梯的较低阶梯，这天梯的顶端直达上帝的宝座。它需要感官可及的象征或圣礼；这些如同我们主的比喻，具有双重目的：向圣洁者显明真理，向亵渎者隐藏真理。教会教阶制度中第一个、也是最高的三联组，由三种圣礼组成：洗礼，称为光照 (φώτισμα*)；圣餐 (σύναξις*，意为聚集、相交)，是最神圣的祝圣礼；以及圣膏礼，或称傅油礼，代表我们的成全。另外还提到三种圣礼：祭司的按立、修士的祝圣，以及丧葬仪式，尤其是为死者傅油。教职的三个等级构成第二个三联组。22第三个三联组则由修士、圣洁的平信徒和慕道者组成。

这两个教阶制度，连同其天上与地上各有九类的服事等级，可以说是上帝施行治理并向人传达自身的运作机构。它们表达了不同等级的存在者彼此隶属、相互依存的神圣法则。

神名，亦即神的属性，是一篇长论著的主题；这些名号透过帷幕与影像，向我们揭示一切生命与光明、思想与愿望的泉源。上帝的良善、美丽与可爱，如同太阳的光芒，照耀一切受造之物，吸引万有归向他。那么，恶怎能存在呢？恶并不是什么实在而积极的东西，只是否定，是缺陷。冷是热的缺乏，暗是光的缺乏；恶也是如此，是善的缺乏。但这样一来，上帝又怎能惩罚恶呢？关于这一问题的答案，作者请读者参阅另一篇现已失传的论著。23

《神秘神学》(Mystic Theology) 简要说明人的灵魂如何上升，与上帝达到神秘的合一；这位上帝，就是此前在各神名之下所阐述的上帝。灵魂如今超越记号和象征，超越属世的概念与定义，达到对上帝的纯粹认识和直观。

狄奥尼修斯区分断言式或肯定式神学 (cataphatic 或 affirmative theology)24，以及否定式或消极式神学 (apophatic 或 negative theology)。25前者从作为一切名号之统一的无限上帝，下降到有限与多样；后者从有限与多样上升到上帝，直至达到那崇高的境界：在那里，它变得完全被动，声音归于寂静，而人与那无名、不可言说、超乎本体的万有之本体合而为一。

十封《书信》(Letters) 分别讨论神学或道德问题，其中四封写给修士该犹 (Caius，θεραπεύτης*)，一封写给执事多罗提乌斯 (Dorotheus，λειτουργός*)，一封写给祭司索西帕特 (Sosipater，ἱερεύς*)，一封写给修士德摩菲卢斯 (Demophilus)，一封写给坡旅甲 (Polycarp，称为 ἱεράρχης*，无疑就是那位著名的士每拿主教)，一封写给提多 (Titus，ἱεράρχης*，克里特主教)，第十封则写给“神学家”约翰，即在拔摩岛的使徒约翰，预言他日后将获释，结束流放。

狄奥尼修斯的传说

伪狄奥尼修斯著作中的两个传说，以夸大的形式进入了拉丁文《日课经》及其他敬虔书籍。一个传说是，他与使徒们一同聚集在童贞女马利亚的临终床前。26另一个传说是，狄奥尼修斯在埃及 (Egypt) 的赫利奥波利斯 (Heliopolis)，目睹基督被钉十字架时那神迹般的日食，27便惊呼：“不是自然之神正在受苦，就是他在同情一位受苦的神。”28在狄奥尼修斯的著作中，并没有这样一句由他自己说出的话；但他将一句类似的话归于诡辩家阿波罗法内斯 (Apollophanes)，即他在赫利奥波利斯的同窗。29

《罗马日课经》(Roman Breviary) 为了敬虔用途，郑重认可了几项与亚略巴古的狄奥尼修斯有关的历史错误：1）认定他就是三世纪法国的圣德尼 (St. Denis)；2）认定他是《神名论》《天上与教会的等级论》《神秘神学》以及“其他多种著作”的作者，而这些著作不可能写于五世纪末以前；3）认定他目睹了基督被钉十字架时超自然的日食，并说出了刚才提及的惊叹语，而他自己却将这句话归于阿波罗法内斯。《日课经》还叙述说，罗马教皇革利免 (Clement) 差遣狄奥尼修斯，与祭司鲁斯提库斯 (Rusticus)、执事埃留特里乌斯 (Eleutherius) 一同前往高卢 (Gaul)；他在铁格上遭火刑，并于图密善 (Domitian) 在位期间的10月9日被斧头斩首，时年已逾百岁；然而，“头被砍下以后，他便用双手捧起，走了二百步，一直捧着自己的头！”30

§ 138. 西方教会中普遍的无知

古罗马文明在安敦尼诸帝 (Antonines) 统治之后不久，便开始衰落，最终被北方蛮族摧毁。文学与艺术的珍宝被埋没，黑夜笼罩了欧洲 (Europe)。少数有学问的人感到孤立而悲哀。图尔的格列高利 (Gregory, of Tours，540–594年) 在其《法兰克教会史》的序言中抱怨说，文史之学在高卢几乎已经消亡，竟找不到一个能将当代事件记录下来的人。31

“中世纪”与“黑暗时代”已经成了同义语。第十世纪尤其被称为铁的时代，或黑暗世纪 (saeculum obscurum)。32第七和第八世纪也好不到哪里去。33道德败坏与无知相伴而行。据传，当教皇制度堕落到最低谷时，罗马几乎无人懂得最初步的文字知识。我们听到有人抱怨，说有些祭司甚至连主祷文和信经都不知道。若以著作数量衡量，第七、第八和第十世纪成果最少；第九世纪最多；第十一世纪比第十世纪略有增加，第十二世纪增长大得多，但到第十三世纪又再度衰退。34

但我们不可受个别事实误导，作出笼统的概括。对英格兰 (England) 和德国 (Germany) 而言，第十世纪比第九世纪有所进步。在法国 (France)，第八和第九世纪产生了新文化的种子；这些种子固然被冬霜掩盖，却没有毁灭，并在第十一和第十二世纪结出了丰硕的果实。

世俗与神圣的学问，都局限在教士和修士之中。广大平信徒，包括贵族，既不会读，也不会写，多数契约都以十字记号签署。即使查理曼 (Charlemagne) 皇帝，也只能勉强书写。民众有限的知识，仰赖于教育程度低下的祭司群体所施的教导。不过，有几位皇帝和国王，特别是查理曼和阿尔弗雷德 (Alfred)，慷慨资助学术，甚至亲自从事著述。

图书馆的匮乏

无知普遍存在的主要原因之一，是书籍稀少。古老的图书馆被残暴的蛮族和战祸摧毁。撒拉逊人 (Saracens) 征服亚历山大里亚 (Alexandria) 之后，埃及纸莎草的种植与输出便告停止；取而代之的羊皮纸或犊皮纸价格极其昂贵，以至一部完整的圣经抄本，价值竟相当于一座宫殿或一处农庄。阿尔弗雷德国王曾用八英亩土地换取一卷宇宙志。因此，便有了用锁链拴住珍贵书籍的习俗，一直延续到十六世纪。也因此，人们惯于刮去古典著作手抄本的原文，换写上毫无价值的修士传说和禁欲主义讲章。甚至圣经有时也遭到这样的处理，于是“上帝的话便被人的遗传废去了”。35

修道院学校和座堂学校的图书馆，藏书往往只有六卷或十二卷，例如拉丁文圣经或其中若干部分、礼仪用书、圣奥古斯丁 (St. Augustin) 和圣格列高利 (St. Gregory)、卡西奥多鲁斯 (Cassiodorus) 和波爱修斯 (Boëthius) 的一些著作、多纳图斯 (Donatus) 和普里西安努斯 (Priscianus) 的语法书，以及维吉尔 (Virgil) 和贺拉斯 (Horace) 的诗篇。大多数书籍必须从意大利 (Italy)，尤其是罗马输入。

第十或第十一世纪棉纸的引入，以及第十二世纪麻纸的引入，使书籍的增殖更为便利。36

§ 139. 教会在教育方面的努力

中世纪教会常被不公正地指责为敌视世俗学问。有人把帕拉丁图书馆 (Bibliotheca Palatina) 及罗马古典雕像的毁坏，归罪于教皇格列高利一世 (Gregory I.)。但这种说法所依据的，只是年代很晚的一则不可靠传说。37 格列高利本人是当时仅次于塞维利亚的伊西多尔 (Isidore of Seville) 的博学之士，也是最受欢迎的作家；599年，他曾将披带授予伊西多尔。他的传记作者和图尔的格列高利 (Gregory of Tours) 都称赞他是学问的赞助者。他在致主教的两封信中，虽曾对拉丁文语法和句法发表一些贬抑之辞，但必须将其理解为一种抗议：相较于主教更高的职责，以及圣经的寓意解释，人们过分看重这些初级学科和异教作家；而他自己在《约伯记》注释中，却把这种寓意解释推到了任意发挥的极端。38 在归于他名下的《列王纪注释》中，他赞许研习博雅诸艺，认为这是正确理解圣经的有益而必要的手段，并援引摩西、以赛亚和圣保罗为佐证。39 因此，我们可以说，他提倡学问与艺术，但要使之从属于大公教会的利益，并为之服务。此后，教皇宝座始终持守这一立场。40

整个中世纪期间，古代文献的保存与研究，主要归功于教士、修士以及少数世俗统治者。修道院是手抄本的苗圃。

与古典时代的联系从未完全中断。波爱修斯 (Boëthius，约525年在帕维亚被斩首) 和卡西奥多鲁斯 (Cassiodorus，退居维维耶修道院，约570年卒于该处)，都曾在意大利东哥特国王狄奥多里克 (Theodoric) 手下担任国务大臣，成为连接古代与中世纪学问的纽带。他们是古罗马人的最后代表；他们把西塞罗 (Cicero) 和塞涅卡的笔蘸入蛮族的墨水，41 激发了罗曼诸民族和日耳曼诸民族方兴未艾的活力：波爱修斯凭借他在狱中写成的《哲学的慰藉》(Consolation of Philosophy)，42 卡西奥多鲁斯则凭借其百科全书式的《圣学纲要》(Institutes of Divine Letters)，这是一部简要指导人如何有益地研读圣经的入门书。43 前者回望希腊哲学，后者展望基督教神学。由于书籍稀少，他们著作的影响超出了其本身价值所应有的程度。

波爱修斯有一种独特的际遇，竟享有圣徒和殉道者的声誉；相传他被处死，不是因为所指控的政治叛逆，而是因为捍卫正统信仰，抵挡狄奥多里克 (Theodoric) 的阿里乌主义 (Arianism)。但丁 (Dante) 把他安置在第四重天，与阿尔伯特大士 (Albertus Magnus)、托马斯·阿奎那 (Thomas Aquinas)、格拉提安 (Gratian)、伦巴德的彼得 (Peter the Lombard)、亚略巴古的狄奥尼修斯 (Dionysius the Areopagite)，以及教会其他伟大的教师为伴：

“那圣洁的灵魂，向一切善于聆听的人

显明世间的虚妄，

如今正饱享一切美善的景象。

那灵魂被逐出的躯体，如今安卧

在下界的切尔道罗 (Cieldauro)；而从殉道

与流放中，灵魂升入了这般平安。”44

然而，波爱修斯究竟是不是基督徒，仍有疑问。他确实与卡西奥多鲁斯交往密切，又生活在基督教的氛围之中，这足以解释他的哲学在道德上的崇高。但除去少数基督教用语，45 他的《慰藉》几乎完全可能出自柏拉图 (Plato) 或塞涅卡学派中一位高尚的异教徒之手。它是希腊哲学的回声；它对人生持乐观的看法；它洋溢着优美的顺服与盼望的精神，从对上帝、对统管万有的上帝的护理以及对祷告的坚定信念中汲取安慰，却对基督和他的福音只字不提。46 这是一部散文与韵文相间的对话录，对话发生于作者与化身为庄严女子的哲学之间；她充当他的天上向导，如同但丁的贝雅特丽齐 (Beatrice)。这部作品在整个中世纪极为流行，被译成数种语言：希腊文、古高地德文（由圣加仑的诺特克译出）、盎格鲁—撒克逊（由阿尔弗雷德国王译出）、诺曼英语（由乔叟译出）、法文（由默恩译出）和希伯来文（由本·班谢特译出）。吉本 (Gibbon) 因它不提基督教，反而更为赞赏，称之为“一部黄金般的著作，即使柏拉图或图利 (Tully) 闲暇时写出，也不失其身份；而鉴于时代的野蛮和作者的处境，它更具有无可比拟的价值。他在罗马和雅典长久呼求的那位天上向导，如今俯就于他，照亮他的地牢，重振他的勇气，把有疗效的香膏倾在他的伤口上……波爱修斯从地上升向天上，寻求至善；探索由偶然与命运、预知与自由意志、时间与永恒构成的形而上学迷宫；并以宽宏的胸怀，试图调和神性完美的属性，与上帝在道德世界和自然世界的治理中所呈现的紊乱。”47

希腊文与希伯来文学问

西方很少有人通晓圣经的原文。拉丁文取代希腊文，成为文学与神圣典籍的语言，并在不同民族的学者之间形成联合的纽带。作为口语，拉丁文因蛮族方言的涌入而迅速退化，但随着时间的推移，却孕育出南欧富于音乐性的罗曼诸语言。

教父中只有极少数人（奥利金和哲罗姆）通晓希伯来文；这种语言继续存留于犹太会堂，以及杰出的犹太语法学家和旧约注释家之中，但直到宗教改革前不久，才在基督教会中复兴。本时期的神学家中，只有极少数人（伊西多尔，以及或许还有司各脱·爱留根纳）显露出通晓希伯来文的迹象。

直到德尔图良 (Tertullian) 和居普良 (Cyprian) 的时代，就连西方教会的作家也几乎一概使用希腊文，此后希腊文便让位于拉丁文。因此，绝大多数西方神学家甚至无法阅读新约原文。教皇格列高利虽然曾以教皇使节身份在君士坦丁堡住过数年，却不懂希腊文。直到十六世纪，大多数经院学者也是如此。

但也有不少值得称道的例外。48 基督一志论与圣像破坏之争，使希腊教会与拉丁教会有了活跃的接触。佛提乌斯 (Photius) 与尼古拉 (Nicolas) 之间的冲突，促使拉丁神学家奋起自卫。

在意大利，卡拉布里亚 (Calabria) 和西西里 (Sicily) 的希腊殖民地直到十一世纪仍然使用希腊语。波爱修斯熟悉希腊哲学家。卡西奥多鲁斯常为拉丁文专业术语列出相应的希腊文。49

本时期有几位教皇生为希腊人，如狄奥多尔一世 (Theodore I.，642年)、约翰六世 (John VI.，701年)、约翰七世 (John VII.，705年)、撒迦利 (Zachary，741年)；另有一些是叙利亚人，如约翰五世 (John V.，685年)、塞尔吉乌斯一世 (Sergius I.，687年)、西辛尼乌斯 (Sisinnius，708年)、君士坦丁一世 (Constantine I.，708年)、格列高利三世 (Gregory III.，731年)。撒迦利把格列高利的《对话录》从拉丁文译成希腊文。教皇保罗一世 (Paul I.，757—768年) 努力传播希腊文学问，并将几部希腊文书籍送给法国国王丕平 (Pepin)，其中包括一部语法书、亚里士多德的若干著作，以及亚略巴古的狄奥尼修斯的著作。他为几位被破坏圣像的皇帝科普罗尼穆斯 (Copronymus) 从东方放逐的修士，安排了希腊语礼拜。梵蒂冈 (Vatican) 图书馆馆长阿纳斯塔修斯 (Anastasius)，奉教皇哈德良二世 (Hadrian II.) 之命，将第八次大公会议，即869年君士坦丁堡会议的教规译成拉丁文。50

塞维利亚的伊西多尔（卒于636年）提到一位博学的西班牙主教赫罗纳的约翰 (John of Gerona)，他年轻时曾在君士坦丁堡求学七年。伊西多尔本人在《词源》(Etymologies) 中引用了许多希腊作家的作品，并被誉为“精通拉丁文、希腊文和希伯来文”。

爱尔兰 (Ireland) 长期领先于英格兰，不仅向这个姊妹岛屿，也向欧洲大陆派出博学的传教士。从司各脱·爱留根纳身上可以清楚看出，那里并非无人通晓希腊文。

英格兰的希腊文学问得自大主教狄奥多尔 (Theodore)；他生于大数 (Tarsus)，在雅典 (Athens) 受教育，后由教皇任命执掌坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 主教座（公元668年）。51 他与同伴哈德良院长 (Hadrian)52——一位非洲裔的意大利修道院院长——在教士中传播希腊文学问。比德 (Bede) 说，在他那个时代，他们的一些门生仍然在世，精通希腊文与拉丁文，如同通晓本族的撒克逊语。其中必须提及舍伯恩 (Sherborne) 主教奥尔德赫尔姆 (Aldhelm) 和罗切斯特 (Rochester) 主教托比亚斯 (Tobias，卒于726年)。53 尊者比德（卒于735年）的注释、54 他对一部希腊文《使徒行传》抄本的引用，尤其是他的《诗艺》(Art of Poetry) 一书，都显出他通晓希腊文。55 在法国 (France)，人们从查理大帝 (Charles the Great) 统治时期开始研习希腊文。阿尔昆 (Alcuin，卒于804年) 从故乡英格兰带来一些希腊文学问，但他的引文可能全都取自哲罗姆 (Jerome) 和卡西奥多鲁斯 (Cassiodorus)。56 执事保罗 (Paulus Diaconus) 经常使用希腊词。查理曼 (Charlemagne) 本人学过希腊文，而《加洛林书》(Libri Carolini) 则显示出对希腊教会圣像争论细节的熟悉。他的姊妹吉塞拉 (Giesela) 是巴黎 (Paris) 附近沙勒 (Challes) 的女修道院院长；她在与阿尔昆的通信中，用拉丁字母写了几个希腊词，57 不过这些词也可能是从拉丁文中得来的。

九世纪乃至本时期整个西方最伟大的希腊文学者，是约翰·司各脱·爱留根纳 (John Scotus Erigena，850年)。他生于爱尔兰，也在那里受教育，却居住于法国秃头查理 (Charles the Bald) 的宫廷。他在自己的拉丁文著作中展示了希腊文学问，翻译了伪狄奥尼修斯著作，并尝试用希腊文进行原创写作。

在德国，拉巴努斯·毛鲁斯 (Rabanus Maurus)、哈尔伯施塔特的海莫 (Haymo of Halberstadt) 和瓦拉弗里德·斯特拉波 (Walafrid Strabo) 略通希腊文，但还不足以在解释圣经时发挥任何实质性的作用。

学习课程58

教育在座堂学校和修道院学校中进行，这些学校为十二世纪开始创办的大学预备了道路。

世俗学问的课程包括所谓七种博雅诸艺，即语法、辩证法（逻辑）、修辞、音乐、算术、几何和天文。前三种构成三艺 (Trivium)，后四种构成四艺 (Quadrivium)。59 七、三、四都被视为神圣的数字。这种划分源自圣奥古斯丁，60 后为波爱修斯和卡西奥多鲁斯所采用。中世纪最早且最流行的纲要，是卡西奥多鲁斯的《论七艺》(De Septem Disciplinis)。61

这些学科是神圣学问的预备课程；神圣学问以拉丁文圣经和拉丁教父为基础。

主要神学家

少数神学家兼通其时代的一切世俗与宗教学问。在西班牙，塞维利亚的伊西多尔（卒于636年）是六世纪末、七世纪初最博学的人。他的《起源》(Origins)，又名《词源》(Etymologies)，共二十卷，囊括了七种博雅诸艺的全部内容，以及神学、法学、医学、博物学等，并显示他熟悉柏拉图、亚里士多德、波爱修斯、德摩斯梯尼 (Demosthenes)、荷马、赫西俄德 (Hesiod)、品达 (Pindar)、阿那克里翁 (Anacreon)、希罗多德 (Herodotus)、西塞罗、贺拉斯、维吉尔、奥维德 (Ovid)、泰伦提乌斯 (Terence)、尤维纳利斯 (Juvenal)、凯撒 (Caesar)、李维 (Livy) 和萨卢斯特 (Sallust)。62 一个世纪后，尊者比德在百科全书式学问上也达到了同样的高度。阿尔昆是加洛林时代首屈一指的神学家。他的门下出了拉巴努斯·毛鲁斯，他是德国学问与高等教育的奠基者。63 司各脱·爱留根纳（约卒于877年）在秃头查理统治时期，不仅以博学，也以独立思想而堪称奇才；他以先知般的先见，显示了西方教会在思辨神学方面的潜在能力。64 十一世纪中叶，贝伦加尔 (Berengar) 和兰弗兰克 (Lanfranc) 运用辩证技巧，分别反对和捍卫化质说的教义。65 围绕嗣子论、预定论和真实临在的教义争论，促进了对圣经和教父的研究，也使思想活动保持活力。

圣经研究

拉丁教会的文献包括忏悔手册、讲道、编年史、译著、汇编、论战著作和注释。最后一类最为重要，但远不及教父与宗教改革者的成就。

大格列高利 (Gregory the Great)、伊西多尔 (Isidore)、比德、阿尔昆、都灵的克劳狄乌斯 (Claudius of Turin)、帕斯卡修斯·拉德贝尔图斯 (Paschasius Radbertus)、拉巴努斯·毛鲁斯、海莫、瓦拉弗里德·斯特拉波等人，完全以实用和讲道的精神与目的从事解经。他们采用拉丁文《武加大译本》(Vulgate) 为文本，很少参照希腊文或希伯来文。奥古斯丁和哲罗姆是主要的资料来源。查理曼感到有必要修订《武加大译本》中讹误的文本，便将这项任务交给阿尔昆。逐字默示说普遍为人接受，只有里昂的阿戈巴尔德 (Agobard of Lyons) 反对；他将默示限于意义与论证，而不包括“ipsa corporalia verba”（具体的字句本身）。

最受青睐的解释方式是属灵的解释，即寓意与神秘的解释。字面解释，即语法—历史解经法 (grammatico-historical exegesis)，则受到忽视。属灵解释又分为三个支派：严格意义的寓意解释、道德解释和上升解释 (anagogical)66，与基督徒的三种主要德性相对应：第一种关乎信仰（credenda，所当信的），第二种关乎实践或爱（agenda，所当行的），第三种关乎盼望（speranda, desideranda，所当盼望、渴慕的）。因此，耶路撒冷按字面或历史意义，是指巴勒斯坦 (Palestine) 的城；按寓意，是指教会；按道德意义，是指信主的灵魂；按上升意义，是指天上的耶路撒冷。这四重意义用以下便于记忆的诗句表达：

“Litera Gesta docet; quid Credas, Allegoria; 字面教你往事；寓意教你当信什么；

Moralis, quid Agas; quo Tendas, Anagogia." 道德教你当行什么；上升教你当趋向何处。”

附注

里昂主教圣欧凯里乌斯 (St. Eucherius) 最初与居普良、安布罗斯一样，是一位杰出的平信徒，也是四个孩子的父亲，后来先成为修士，再成为主教。他在五世纪中叶写了一部中世纪释经学简明手册，题为《属灵理解的范式》(Liber Formularum Spiritalis Intelligentiae)（罗马，1564年等版本；收入米涅《教父集》［Patrol.］第50卷，第727–772栏）；他约卒于450年。比德经常引用此书，此书有时也被误归于比德名下。欧凯里乌斯表现出广博的圣经知识和虔敬的精神。中世纪注释家和神秘主义者所喜爱的许多解释，在他这里已见先声。他根据圣经本身，以及其中不能按字面理解的神人同形、神人同情的表达，为寓意解经法辩护。不过，他也承认，在历史叙述中，字面意义与道德、神秘意义一样，都有其适当的地位。他将神的指头（Digitus Dei）等同于神的灵（第2章；比较《路加福音》11:20与《马太福音》12:28），并解释下列各词的多重意义：Jerusalem（耶路撒冷：ecclesia, vel anima，教会或灵魂，第10章）、方舟（caro Dominica, corda sanctorum Deo plena, ecclesia intra quam salvanda clauduntur，主的肉身、充满神的圣徒之心、将得救者容纳其中的教会）、Babylon（巴比伦：mundus, Roma, inimici，世界、罗马、仇敌）、fures（盗贼：haeretici et pseudoprophetae, gentes, vitia，异端与假先知、外邦人、恶习）、chirographum（字据）、pactum（约）、praeputium（未受割礼）、circumcisio（割礼）等。在最后一章中，他论述数字的象征意义，例如：1＝神的独一性；2＝两约、两条最大的诫命；3＝天上与地上的三一（他引用了《约翰一书》5:7那段伪文）；4＝四福音、乐园的四条河；5＝摩西五经、五个饼、基督的五处伤口（《约翰福音》20:25）；6＝创造的日数、世界的各个时代；7＝安息与完全之日；8＝复活之日；10＝十诫；12＝使徒、全体信徒，等等。

拉巴努斯·毛鲁斯（776–856年）在其颇为奇特的《全圣经寓意释义》(Allegoriae in Universam Sacram Scripturam) 中，更充分地发展了四重解释的理论（《著作集》［Opera］，米涅编，第VI卷，第849–1088栏）。他称这四种意义为智慧的四个女儿，智慧借她们养育自己的儿女：给初学者饮以 in lacte historiae（历史之乳），给信徒食以 in pane allegoriae（寓意之饼），给行善者以 in refectione tropologiae（道德解释的滋养）作为鼓励，给渴望天上安息者以 in vino anagogiae（上升解释之酒）作为喜乐。他又在本书开头给出以下定义：“Historia ad aptam rerum gestarum narrationem pertinet, quae et in superficie litterae continetur, et sic intelligitur sicut legitur. Allegoria vero aliquid in se plus continet, quod per hoc quod locus [loquens] de rei veritate ad quiddam dat intelligendum de fidei puritate, et sanctae Ecclesiae mysteria, sive praesentia, sive futura, aliud dicens, aliud significans, semper autem figmentis et velatis ostendit. Tropologia quoque et ipsa, sicut allegoria, in figuratis, sive dictis, sive factis, constat: sed in hoc ab allegoria distat quod Allegoria quidem fidem, Tropologia vero aedificat moralitem. Anagogia autem, sive velatis, sive apertis dictis, de aeternis supernae patriae gaudiis constat, et quae merces vel fidem rectam, vel vitam maneat sanctam, verbis vel opertis, vel apertis demonstrat. Historia namque perfectorum exempla quo narrat, legentem ad imitationem sanctitatis excitat; Allegoria in fidei revelatione ad cognitionem veritatis; Tropologia in instructione morum ad amorem virtutis; Anagogia in manifestatione sempiternorum gaudiorum ad desiderium aeternae felicitatis. In nostrae ergo animae domo Historia fundamentum ponit; Allegoria parietes erigit; Anagogia tectum supponit; Tropologia vero tam interius per affectum quam exterius per effectum boni operis, variis ornatibus depingit.”意即：“历史解释旨在恰当地叙述所发生的事；这些事包含在字面的表层，读来如何，便如何理解。寓意解释本身则包含更多的意义：经文述说事实的真相时，也使人领悟信仰的纯正，以及圣教会现今或将来的奥秘；它所说的是一事，所指的是另一事，始终借形象与隐语来表明。道德解释也与寓意解释一样，存在于比喻性的言语或事迹之中；但两者的差别在于，寓意解释建立信仰，道德解释则建立德行。上升解释无论用隐晦或明白的言语，都是论天上家乡永恒的喜乐，并以隐晦或明白的话，显明正确信仰或圣洁生活将得到怎样的赏赐。历史解释叙述完全人的榜样，激励读者效法圣洁；寓意解释揭示信仰，引人认识真理；道德解释教导品行，引人爱慕德性；上升解释显明永恒的喜乐，引人渴慕永远的福乐。因此，在我们灵魂的房屋中，历史解释奠定根基；寓意解释竖起墙壁；上升解释架设屋顶；道德解释则以种种装饰加以彩绘，在内借着情感，在外借着善行的实际果效。”

§ 140. 查理大帝与秃头查理对学术的扶植

在他那个时代的黑暗中，查理曼犹如一座光照远方的灯塔，卓然矗立。他既是一个新帝国的创建者，也是一个学术新时代的开创者。他是法国与德国文明的先驱。他在战争中固然伟大，作为立法者与和平事业的促进者则更为伟大。他清楚地看出，宗教与教育是国家唯一坚实而持久的根基。在这方面，他远远超越了亚历山大大帝 (Alexander the Great) 和凯撒，在基督教君主中也无人能出其右。

他从意大利和英格兰延请最优秀的学者到宫廷来——比萨的彼得 (Peter of Pisa)、保罗·瓦尔内弗里德 (Paul Warnefrid)、阿奎莱亚的保利努斯 (Paulinus of Aquileia)、奥尔良的狄奥杜尔夫 (Theodulph of Orleans)、约克的阿尔昆 (Alcuin of York)。67 他们组成了一种皇家科学与文艺学院，并举行文学雅集。每个成员都有一个取自圣经或古典学问的笔名（nom de plume）：国王以“大卫”或“所罗门”之名主持；极其推崇贺拉斯和维吉尔的阿尔昆称为“弗拉库斯”(Flaccus)；安吉尔贝特 (Angilbert，国王的女婿) 称为“荷马”(Homerus)；艾因哈德 (Einhard，国王的传记作者) 称为“比撒列”(Bezaleel)，取自建造会幕的那位巧匠（《出埃及记》31:2）；维佐 (Wizo) 称为“坎迪杜斯”(Candidus)；阿尔诺 (Arno) 称为“阿奎拉”(Aquila)；弗雷德吉苏斯 (Fredegisus) 称为“拿但业”(Nathanael)；里希博德 (Richbod) 称为“马卡里乌斯”(Macarius)，等等。就连妇女也未被排除在外：皇帝的姊妹吉塞拉 (Gisela) 名为“露琪亚”(Lucia)；他博学的表亲贡德拉德 (Gundrad) 名为“欧拉利亚”(Eulalia)；他的女儿罗特鲁德 (Rotrude) 名为“科伦巴”(Columba)。他称阿尔昆为自己“亲爱的老师”，而他本人则是这位老师最受教的学生；他们于781年在意大利初次相遇。他有永不餍足的求知欲，在信中向阿尔昆提出各式各样的问题，甚至涉及神学中最困难的难题。他在成年以后学习书写之艺、拉丁文语法和少量希腊文（为的是将拉丁文新约与原文比较），并获得了一些修辞学、辩证法、数学和天文学知识。他喜爱阅读古罗马诗人、历史家的著作，以及奥古斯丁的《上帝之城》。他渴望得到十二位哲罗姆和奥古斯丁，阿尔昆却劝他知足，因为天地的创造主也只愿赐给世界两位这样的巨人。他在《查理书》(Libri Carolini) 的编写中有所参与；此书以开明的精神抗议圣像崇拜的迷信。有些诗也被归于他的手笔，或认为是受他的启发而作。他命保罗·瓦尔内弗里德（执事保罗，Paulus Diaconus）选编拉丁教父最优秀的讲道，供各教会使用，并为此书作序出版，在序中劝勉教士殷勤研读圣经。他在位期间于813年在兰斯 (Rheims)、图尔、沙隆 (Chalons)、美因茨 (Mainz) 召开的数次教会会议，命教士备有一本《讲道集》(Homiliarium)，并将拉丁文讲章明白地译成 rusticam Romanam linguam aut Theotiscam（通俗罗曼语或日耳曼语），使众人都能听懂。

查理不仅致力于教士的高等教育，也致力于高等贵族和国家官员的高等教育。他的儿女都学识广博。787年，他向帝国内所有主教与修道院长发出通函，敦促他们在主教座堂和修道院附设学校。后来，他甚至进一步提出普及教育这一宏大却为时尚早的计划，并在802年的一道敕令中，要求每位家长都送儿子入学，使他们能够学习阅读。奥尔良的狄奥杜尔夫（卒于821年）指示教区内的司铎在每个城镇和村庄开办学校，68 亲切接纳学生，不得索取学费，只可接受自愿的馈赠。

皇帝创立了宫廷学校或宫学（Schola Palatina），以实施高等教育，并交由阿尔昆主持。69 这仿照的是罗马皇帝的 Paedagogium ingenuorum（自由民子弟学校）。它随着皇帝居所的迁移，辗转于亚琛 (Aix-la-Chapelle)、沃尔姆斯 (Worms)、法兰克福 (Frankfurt)、美因茨、雷根斯堡 (Regensburg)、英格尔海姆 (Ingelheim) 和巴黎。它并不是巴黎大学的开端，后者的创立年代要晚得多；它却是那个时代培养有学识的教士、贵族和政治家的主要摇篮。其课程涵盖世俗与神圣学问的一切门类。70 它成为图尔、里昂、奥尔良 (Orleans)、兰斯、沙特尔 (Chartres)、特鲁瓦 (Troyes)、旧科尔比 (Old Corbey)、新科尔比 (New Corbey)、梅斯 (Metz)、圣加仑修道院 (St. Gall)、乌得勒支 (Utrecht)、列日 (Lüttich) 等地新旧同类学校的典范。71 加洛林时代丰富的文献，显示了皇帝扶植与垂范的成果。然而，这与其说是本土的产物，不如说是外来的产物。它既非法国的，也非德国的，而在本质上是拉丁的，就此而言也是人为培植的。情形也不可能不是如此；因为拉丁古典著作、拉丁文圣经和拉丁教父，是当时唯一可以利用的学问泉源，而法语与德语尚未成为文学的载体。这一事实，既说明了它何以迅速衰落，也说明了它后来何以在与罗马教会的密切联系中复兴。

查理曼所创建的事业，在其软弱的继承者们所经历的内战中，面临彻底毁灭的威胁。但虔诚者路易之子、法国国王秃头查理（843–877年）继承了祖父对学问的热忱，使巴黎宫廷学校在约翰·司各脱·爱留根纳主持下焕发新的光彩；尽管爱留根纳有种种怪异之处，查理仍以宽宏的气度保护他。预定论争论和第一次圣餐争论都发生在他统治期间，激发了大量的思想活动与学术研究；拉巴努斯·毛鲁斯、欣克马尔 (Hincmar)、雷米吉乌斯 (Remigius)、普鲁登提乌斯 (Prudentius)、塞尔瓦图斯·卢普斯 (Servatus Lupus)、约翰·司各脱·爱留根纳、帕斯卡修斯·拉德贝尔图斯和拉特拉姆努斯 (Ratramnus) 的著作，便是明证。我们在这些作者中，可以看到保守、自由以及思辨或神秘这三种倾向；它们通常是思想活跃、著述丰盛时期的特征。

秃头查理死后，愚昧与野蛮的黑夜笼罩欧洲，比以往任何时候都更加深沉。这种情形一直延续到十一世纪中叶前后；那时，贝伦加尔所引起的圣餐争论唤醒了教会沉睡的思想力量，为十二世纪的经院哲学与神学预备了道路。

英格兰文化的开端，是随基督教由首任坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 大主教奥古斯丁 (Augustin) 传入的；他带来了圣经、教会用书、教皇格列高利的著作，以及罗马基督教的教义与实践；但此后一个世纪中，进展甚微。在他的继任者中，希腊修士、大数的狄奥多尔 (Theodore of Tarsus，668–690年) 最积极地促进教士的教育与纪律。撒克逊时期最杰出的学者是尊者比德（卒于735年）；如前所述，他代表了当时全部的历史、释经及一般知识。约克 (York) 大主教埃格伯特 (Egbert) 于732年在约克创办了一所兴盛的学校，查理曼的教师兼友人阿尔昆便出自该校。

在异教的丹麦人 (Danes) 与诺曼人 (Normans) 入侵期间，许多教堂、修道院和图书馆遭到毁坏，教士本身也重新陷入野蛮，以致连他们在公共礼拜中所用的拉丁文程式是什么意思，也不知道。

在这极度混乱的时期，阿尔弗雷德大帝 (Alfred the Great，871–901年) 于二十二岁时登上王位。在所有盎格鲁—撒克逊统治者中，无论战时还是平时，他都居于首位。查理曼之于德国与法国，正如阿尔弗雷德之于英格兰。他在陆上和海上击败了丹麦人的军队，使祖国摆脱外国统治，并开创了基督教教育的新纪元。他从威尔士 (Wales) 古老的不列颠教会、爱尔兰以及欧洲大陆延请学者，委以要职。他汇集圣经与教父著作中的嘉言。他三十六岁时，向威尔士修士阿塞尔 (Asser) 学习拉丁文，阿塞尔后来为他作传。他亲自把几部标准著作从拉丁文译成盎格鲁—撒克逊语，并附以注释；无疑，这有学者们的协助。这些著作包括《诗篇》的一部分、波爱修斯的《哲学的慰藉》、比德的《英格兰教会史》、教皇格列高利的《教牧神学》、奥古斯丁的《默想录》、奥罗修斯 (Orosius) 的《世界史》，以及《伊索寓言》(Aesop’s Fables)。他给每个教区送去一部格列高利的《教牧神学》，使教士受益。这一时期的圣经和礼拜用书附有如此之多的本国语注解，主要应归功于他的影响。

他与罗马教廷保持密切联系，视之为教会合一与文明的中心。他将一半收入用于教会和学校。他在牛津 (Oxford) 创办了一所类似宫廷学校 (Schola Palatina) 的学校；但牛津大学与剑桥、巴黎两所大学一样，成立年代要晚得多（十二或十三世纪）。他似乎甚至设想过普及国民教育的计划。72 尽管身体极为衰弱（他患有癫痫），他在二十九年的统治中却表现出非凡的活力；他以品格的纯洁与虔诚，以及为人民最高利益所作的无私奉献，留下了不朽的声名。73

§ 135. Literary Character of the Early Middle Ages.

The prevailing character of this period in sacred learning is a faithful traditionalism which saved the remains of the ancient classical and Christian literature, and transferred them to a new soil. The six centuries which intervene between the downfall of the West Roman Empire (476) and the age of Hildebrand (1049–1085), are a period of transition from an effete heathen to a new Christian civilization, and from patristic to scholastic theology. It was a period of darkness with the signs of approaching daylight. The fathers were dead, and the schoolmen were not yet born. The best that could be done was to preserve the inheritance of the past for the benefit of the future. The productive power was exhausted, and gave way to imitation and compilation. Literary industry took the place of independent investigation.

The Greek church kept up the connection with classical and patristic learning, and adhered closely to the teaching of the Nicene fathers and the seven oecumenical councils. The Latin church bowed before the authority of St. Augustin and St. Jerome. The East had more learning; the West had more practical energy, which showed itself chiefly in the missionary field. The Greek church, with her head turned towards the past, tenaciously maintains to this day the doctrinal position of the eighth century; the Latin church, looking to the future, passed through a deep night of ignorance, but gathered new strength from new blood. The Greek church presents ancient Christianity at rest; while the Latin church of the middle ages is Christianity in motion towards the modern era.

§ 136. Learning in the Eastern Church.

The Eastern church had the advantage over the Western in the knowledge of the Greek language, which gave her direct access to the Greek Testament, the Greek classics, and the Greek fathers; but, on the other hand, she had to suffer from the Mohammedan invasions, and from the intrigues and intermeddling of a despotic court.

The most flourishing seats of patristic learning, Alexandria and Antioch, were lost by the conquests of Islam. The immense library at Alexandria was burned by order of Omar (638), who reasoned: "If these writings of the Greeks agree with the book of God (the Koran), they are useless and need not be preserved; if they disagree, they are pernicious and ought to be destroyed."(1) In the eighth century, however, the Saracens themselves began to cultivate learning, to translate Greek authors, to collect large libraries in Bagdad, Cairo, and Cordova. The age of Arabic learning continued about five hundred years, till the irruption of the Moguls. It had a stimulating effect upon the scholarship of the church, especially upon the development of scholastic philosophy, through the writings of Averroës of Cordova (d. 1198), the translator and commentator of Aristotle.

Constantinople was the centre of the literary, activity of the Greek church during the middle ages. Here or in the immediate vicinity (Chalcedon, Nicaea) the oecumenical councils were held; here were the scholars, the libraries, the imperial patronage, and all the facilities for the prosecution of studies. Many a library was destroyed, but always replaced again.(2) Thessalonica and Mount Athos were also important seats of learning, especially in the twelfth century.

The Latin was the official language of the Byzantine court, and Justinian, who regained, after a divorce of sixty years, the dominion of ancient Rome through the valor of Belisarius (536), asserted the proud title of Emperor of the Romans, and published his code of laws in Latin. But the Greek always was and remained the language of the people, of literature, philosophy, and theology.

Classical learning revived in the ninth century under the patronage of the court. The reigns of Caesar Bardas (860–866), Basilius I. the Macedonian (867–886), Leo VI. the Philosopher (886–911), who was himself an author, Constantine VII. Porphyrogenitus (911–959), likewise an author, mark the most prosperous period of Byzantine literature. The family of the Comneni, who upheld the power of the sinking empire from 1057 to 1185, continued the literary patronage, and the Empress Eudocia and the Princess Anna Comnena cultivated the art of rhetoric and the study of philosophy.

Even during the confusion of the crusades and the disasters which overtook the empire, the love for learning continued; and when Constantinople at last fell into the hands of the Turks, Greek scholarship took refuge in the West, kindled the renaissance, and became an important factor in the preparation for the Reformation.

The Byzantine literature presents a vast mass of learning without an animating, controlling and organizing genius. "The Greeks of Constantinople," says Gibbon,(3) with some rhetorical exaggeration, "held in their lifeless hands the riches of the fathers, without inheriting the spirit which had created and improved that sacred patrimony: they read, they praised, they compiled; but their languid souls seemed alike incapable of thought and action. In the revolution of ten centuries, not a single discovery was made to exalt the dignity or promote the happiness of mankind. Not a single idea has been added to the speculative systems of antiquity; and a succession of patient disciples became in their turn the dogmatic teachers of the next servile generation. Not a single composition of history, philosophy or literature has been saved from oblivion by the intrinsic beauties of style or sentiment, of original fancy, and even of successful imitation .... The leaders of the Greek church were humbly content to admire and copy the oracles of antiquity, nor did the schools or pulpit produce any rivals of the fame of Athanasius and Chrysostom."

The theological controversies developed dialectical skill, a love for metaphysical subtleties, and an over-estimate of theoretical orthodoxy at the expense of practical piety. The Monotheletic controversy resulted in an addition to the christological creed; the iconoclastic controversy determined the character of public worship and the relation of religion to art.

The most gifted Eastern divines were Maximus Confessor in the seventh, John of Damascus in the eighth, and Photius in the ninth century. Maximus, the hero of Monotheletism, was an acute and profound thinker, and the first to utilize the pseudo-Dyonysian philosophy in support of a mystic orthodoxy. John of Damascus, the champion of image-worship, systematized the doctrines of the orthodox fathers, especially the three great Cappadocians, Basil, Gregory of Nazianzum, and Gregory of Nyssa, and produced a monumental work on theology which enjoys to this day the same authority in the Greek church as the "Summa" of Thomas Aquinas in the Latin. Photius, the antagonist of Pope Nicolas, was the greatest scholar of his age, who read and digested with independent judgment all ancient heathen and Christian books on philology, philosophy, theology, canon law, history, medicine, and general literature. In extent of information and fertility of pen he had a successor in Michael Psellus (d. 1106).

Exegesis was cultivated by Oecumenius in the tenth, Theophylact in the eleventh, and Euthymius Zygabenus in the twelfth century. They compiled the valuable exegetical collections called "Catenae."(4) Simeon Metaphrastes (about 900) wrote legendary biographies and eulogies of one hundred and twenty-two saints. Suidas, in the eleventh century, prepared a Lexicon, which contains much valuable philological and historical information(5) The Byzantine historians, Theophanes, Syncellus, Cedrenus, Leo Grammaticus, and others, describe the political and ecclesiastical events of the slowly declining empire. The most eminent scholar of the twelfth century, was Eustathius, Archbishop of Thessalonica, best known as the commentator of Homer, but deserving a high place also as a theologian, ecclesiastical ruler, and reformer of monasticism.

The Real and the Ficitious Doinysius.

The tendency to mystic speculation was kept up and nourished chiefly through the writings which exhibit a fusion of Neo-Platonism and Christianity, and which go under the name of Dionysius Areopagita, the distinguished Athenian convert of St. Paul (Acts 17:34). He was, according to a tradition of the second century, the first bishop of Athens.(6) In the ninth century, when the French became acquainted with his supposed writings, he was confounded with St. Denis, the first bishop of Paris and patron saint of France, who lived and died about two hundred years after the Areopagite.(7) He thus became, by a glaring anachronism, the connecting link between Athens and Paris, between Greek philosophy and Christian theology, and acquired an almost apostolic authority. He furnishes one of the most remarkable examples of the posthumous influence of unknown authorship and of the power of the dead over the living. For centuries he was regarded as the prince of theologians. He represented to the Greek and Latin church the esoteric wisdom of the gospel, and the mysterious harmony between faith and reason and between the celestial and terrestrial hierarchy.

Pseudo-Dionysius is a philosophical counterpart of Pseudo-Isidor: both are pious frauds in the interest of the catholic system, the one with regard to theology, the other with regard to church polity; both reflect the uncritical character of mediaeval Christianity; both derived from the belief in their antiquity a fictitious importance far beyond their intrinsic merits. Doubts were entertained of the genuineness of the Areopagitica by Laurentius Valla, Erasmus, and Cardinal Cajetan; but it was only in the seventeenth century that the illusion of the identity of Pseudo-Dionysius with the apostolic convert and the patron-saint of France was finally dispelled by the torch of historical criticism. Since that time his writings have lost their authority and attraction; but they will always occupy a prominent place among the curiosities of literature, and among the most remarkable systems of mystic philosophy.

Authorship.

Who is the real author of those productions? The writer is called simply Dionysius, and only once.(8) He repeatedly mentions an unknown Hierotheos, as his teacher; but he praises also "the divine Paul," as the spiritual guide of both, and addresses persons who bear apostolic names, as Timothy, Titus, Caius, Polycarp, and St. John. He refers to a visit he made with Hierotheos, and with James, the brother of the Lord (ajdelfovqeo"), and Peter, "the chief and noblest head of the inspired apostles," to gaze upon the (dead) body of her (Mary) who was "the beginning of life and the recipient of God;" on which occasion Hierotheos gave utterance to their feelings in ecstatic hymns. It is evident then that he either lived in the apostolic age and its surroundings, or that he transferred himself back in imagination to that age.(9) The former alternative is impossible. The inflated style, the reference to later persons (as Ignatius of Antioch and Clement of Alexandria), the acquaintance with Neo-Platonic ideas, the appeal to the "old tradition" (ajrcai'a paravdosi") of the church as well as the Scriptures, and the elaborate system of church polity and ritual which he presupposes, clearly prove his post-apostolic origin. He was not known to Eusebius or Jerome or any ecclesiastical author before 533. In that year his writings were first mentioned in a conference between orthodox bishops and heretical Severians at Constantinople under Justinian I.(10) The Severians quoted them as an authority for their Monophysitic Christology and against the Council of Chalcedon; and in reply to the objection that they were unknown, they asserted that Cyril of Alexandria had used them against the Nestorians. If this be so, they must have existed before 444, when Cyril died; but no trace can be found in Cyril’s writings. On the other hand, Dionysius presupposes the christological controversies of the fifth century, and shows a leaning to Monophysitic views, and a familiarity with the last and best representatives of Neo-Platonism, especially with Proclus, who died in Athens, a.d. 485. The resemblance is so strong that the admirers of Dionysius charged Proclus with plagiarism.(11) The writer then was a Christian Neo-Platonist who wrote towards the close of the fifth or the beginning of the sixth century in Greece or in Egypt, and who by a literary fiction clothed his religious speculations with the name and authority of the first Christian bishop of Athens.(12)

In the same way the pseudo-Clementine writings were assigned to the first bishop of Rome.

The Fortunes of Pseudo-Dionysius.

Pseudo-Dionysius appears first in the interest of the heretical doctrine of one nature and one will in the person of Christ.(13) But he soon commended himself even more to orthodox theologians. He was commented on by Johannes Scythopolitanus in the sixth century, and by St. Maximus Confessor in the seventh. John of Damascus often quotes him as high authority. Even Photius, who as a critic doubted the genuineness, numbers him among the great church teachers and praises his depth of thought.(14)

In the West the writings of Pseudo-Dionysius were first noticed about 590 by Pope Gregory I., who probably became acquainted with them while ambassador at Constantinople. Pope Hadrian I. mentions them in a letter to Charlemagne. The Emperor Michael II. the Stammerer, sent a copy to Louis the Pious, 827. Their arrival at St. Denis on the eve of the feast of the saint who reposed there, was followed by no less than nineteen miraculous cures in the neighborhood. They naturally recalled the memory of the patron-saint of France, and were traced to his authorship. The emperor instructed Hilduin, the abbot of St. Denis, to translate them into Latin; but his scholarship was not equal to the task. John Scotus Erigena, the best Greek scholar in the West, at the request of Charles the Bald, prepared a literal translation with comments, about 850, and praised the author as "venerable alike for his antiquity and for the sublimity of the heavenly mysteries" with which he dealt.(15) Pope Nicolas I. complained that the work had not been sent to him for approval," according to the custom of the church" (861); but a few years later Anastasius, the papal librarian, highly commended it (c. 865).

The Areopagitica stimulated an intuitive and speculative bent of mind, and became an important factor in the development of scholastic and mystic theology. Hugo of St. Victor, Peter the Lombard, Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, Robert Grosseteste, and Dionysius Carthusianus wrote commentaries on them, and drew from them inspiration for their own writings.(16) The Platonists of the Italian renaissance likewise were influenced by them.

Dante places Dionysius among the theologians in the heaven of the sun:

"Thou seest next the lustre of that taper,

Which in the flesh below looked most within

The angelic nature and its ministry."(17)

Luther called him a dreamer, and this was one of his heretical views which the Sorbonne of Paris condemned.

The Several Writings.

The Dionysian writings, as far as preserved, are four treatises addressed to Timothy, his "fellow-presbyter," namely: 1) On the Celestial Hierarchy (peri; th'" oujraniva" iJerarciva"). 2) On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy (peri; th'" ejkklhsiastikh'" iJerarciva"). 3) On the Divine Names (peri; qeivwn ojnomavtwn). 4) On Mystic Theology (peri; mustikh'" qeologiva"). To these are added ten letters addressed to various persons of the apostolic age.(18)

The System of Dionysius.

These books reveal the same authorship and the same system of mystic symbolism, in which Neo-Platonism and Christianity are interwoven. The last phase of Hellenic philosophy which heretofore had been hostile to the church, is here made subservient to it. The connecting ideas are the progressive revelation of the infinite, the hierarchic triads, the negative conception of evil, and the striving of man after mystic union with the transcendent God. The system is a counterpart of the Graeco-Jewish theology, of Philo of Alexandria, who in similar manner mingled the Platonic philosophy with the Mosaic religion. The Areopagite and Philo teach theology in the garb of philosophy; both appeal to Scripture, tradition, and reason; both go behind the letter of the Bible and the facts of history to a deeper symbolic and allegoric meaning; both adulterate the revealed truths by foreign elements. But Philo is confined to the Old Testament, and ignores the New, which was then not yet written; while the system of the Areopagite is a sort of philosophy of Christianity.

The Areopagite reverently ascends the heights and sounds the depths of metaphysical and religious speculation, and makes the impression of profound insight and sublime spirituality; and hence he exerted such a charm upon the great schoolmen and mystics of the middle ages. But he abounds in repetitions; he covers the poverty of thought with high-sounding phrases; he uses the terminology of the Hellenic mysteries;(19) and his style is artificial, turgid, involved, and monotonous.

The unity of the Godhead and the hierarchical order of the universe are the two leading ideas of the Areopagite. He descends from the divine unity through a succession of manifestations to variety, and ascends back again to mystic union with God. His text, we may say, is the sentence of St. Paul: "From God, and through God, and unto God, are all things" (Rom. 11:36).

He starts from the Neo-Platonic conception of the Godhead, as a being which transcends all being and existence(20) and yet is the beginning and the end of all existence, as unknowable and yet the source of all reason and knowledge, as nameless and inexpressible and yet giving names to all things, as a simple unity and yet causing all variety. He describes God as "a unity of three persons, who with his loving providence penetrates to all things, from super-celestial essences to the last things of earth, as being the beginning and cause of all beings, beyond all beginning, and enfolding all things transcendentally in his infinite embrace." If we would know God, we must go out of ourselves and become absorbed in Him. All being proceeds from God by a sort of emanation, and tends upward to him.

The world forms a double hierarchy, that is, as he defines it, "a holy order, and science, and activity or energy, assimilated as far as possible to the godlike and elevated to the imitation of God in proportion to the divine illuminations conceded to it." There are two hierarchies, one in heaven, and one on earth, each with three triadic degrees.

The celestial or supermundane hierarchy consists of angelic beings in three orders: 1) thrones, cherubim, and seraphim, in the immediate presence of God; 2) powers, mights, and dominions; 3) angels (in the narrower sense), archangels, and principalities.(21) The first order is illuminated, purified and perfected by God, the second order by the first, the third by the second.

The earthly or ecclesiastical hierarchy is a reflex of the heavenly, and a school to train us up to the closest possible communion with God. Its orders form the lower steps of the heavenly ladder which reaches in its summit to the throne of God. It requires sensible symbols or sacraments, which, like the parables of our Lord, serve the double purpose of revealing the truth to the holy and hiding it from the profane. The first and highest triad of the ecclesiastical hierarchy are the sacraments of baptism which is called illumination (fwvtisma), the eucharist (suvnaxi", gathering, communion), which is the most sacred of consecrations, and the holy unction or chrism which represents our perfecting. Three other sacraments are mentioned: the ordination of priests, the consecration of monks, and the rites of burial, especially the anointing of the dead. The three orders of the ministry form the second triad.(22) The third triad consists of monks, the holy laity, and the catechumens.

These two hierarchies with their nine-fold orders of heavenly and earthly ministrations are, so to speak, the machinery of God’s government and of his self-communication to man. They express the divine law of subordination and mutual dependence of the different ranks of beings.

The Divine Names or attributes, which are the subject of a long treatise, disclose to us through veils and shadows the fountain-head of all life and light, thought and desire. The goodness, the beauty, and the loveliness of God shine forth upon all created things, like the rays of the sun, and attract all to Himself. How then can evil exist? Evil is nothing real and positive, but only a negation, a defect. Cold is the absence of heat, darkness is the absence of light; so is evil the absence, of goodness. But how then can God punish evil? For the answer to this question the author refers to another treatise which is lost.(23)

The Mystic Theology briefly shows the way by which the human soul ascends to mystic union with God as previously set forth under the Divine Names. The soul now rises above signs and symbols, above earthly conceptions and definitions to the pure knowledge and intuition of God.

Dionysius distinguishes between cataphatic or affirmative theology)(24) and apophatic or negative theology.(25) The former descends from the infinite God, as the unity of all names, to the finite and manifold; the latter ascends from the finite and manifold to God, until it reaches that height of sublimity where it becomes completely passive, its voice is stilled, and man is united with the nameless, unspeakable, super-essential Being of Beings.

The ten Letters treat of separate theological or moral topics, and are addressed, four to Caius, a monk (qerapeuvth"), one to Dorotheus, a deacon (leitourgov"), one to Sosipater, a priest (iJereuv"), one to Demophilus, a monk, one to Polycarp (called iJeravrch", no doubt the well-known bishop of Smyrna), one to Titus (iJeravrch", bishop of Crete), and the tenth to John, "the theologian," i.e. the Apostle John at Patmos, foretelling his future release from exile.

Dionysian Legends.

Two legends of the Pseudo-Dionysian writings have passed in exaggerated forms into Latin Breviaries and other books of devotion. One is his gathering with the apostles around the death-bed of the Virgin Mary.(26) The other is the exclamation of Dionysius when he witnessed at Heliopolis in Egypt the miraculous solar eclipse at the time of the crucifixion:(27) "Either the God of nature is suffering, or He sympathizes with a suffering God."(28) No such sentence occurs in the writings of Dionysius as his own utterance; but a similar one is attributed by him to the sophist Apollophanes, his fellow-student at Heliopolis.(29)

The Roman Breviary has given solemn sanction, for devotional purposes, to several historical errors connected with Dionysius the Areopagite: 1) his identity with the French St. Denis of the third century; 2) his authorship of the books upon "The Names of God," upon "The Orders in Heaven and in the Church," upon "The Mystic Theology," and "divers others," which cannot have been written before the end of the fifth century; 3) his witness of the supernatural eclipse at the time of the crucifixion, and his exclamation just referred to, which he himself ascribes to Apollophanes. The Breviary also relates that Dionysius was sent by Pope Clement of Rome to Gaul with Rusticus, a priest, and Eleutherius, a deacon; that he was tortured with fire upon a grating, and beheaded with an axe on the 9th day of October in Domitian’s reign, being over a hundred years old, but that "after his head was cut off, he took it in his hands and walked two hundred paces, carrying it all the while!"(30)

§ 138. Prevailing Ignorance in the Western Church.

The ancient Roman civilization began to decline soon after the reign of the Antonines, and was overthrown at last by the Northern barbarians. The treasures of literature and art were buried, and a dark night settled over Europe. The few scholars felt isolated and sad. Gregory, of Tours (540–594) complains, in the Preface to his Church History of the Franks, that the study of letters had nearly perished from Gaul, and that no man could be found who was able to commit to writing the events of the times.(31)

"Middle Ages" and "Dark Ages" have become synonymous terms. The tenth century is emphatically called the iron age, or the saeculum obscurum.(32) The seventh and eighth were no better.(33) Corruption of morals went hand in hand with ignorance. It is re-ported that when the papacy had sunk to the lowest depth of degradation, there was scarcely a person in Rome who knew the first elements of letters. We hear complaints of priests who did not know even the Lord’s Prayer and the Creed. If we judge by the number of works, the seventh, eighth and tenth centuries were the least productive; the ninth was the most productive; there was a slight increase of productiveness in the eleventh over the tenth, a much greater one in the twelfth, but again a decline in the thirteenth century.(34)

But we must not be misled by isolated facts into sweeping generalities. For England and Germany the tenth century was in advance of the ninth. In France the eighth and ninth centuries produced the seeds of a new culture which were indeed covered by winter frosts, but not destroyed, and which bore abundant fruit in the eleventh and twelfth.

Secular and sacred learning was confined to the clergy and the monks. The great mass of the laity, including the nobility, could neither read nor write, and most contracts were signed with the mark of the cross. Even the Emperor Charlemagne wrote only with difficulty. The people depended for their limited knowledge on the teaching of a poorly educated priesthood. But several emperors and kings, especially Charlemagne and Alfred, were liberal patrons of learning and even contributors to literature.

Scarcity of Libraries.

One of the chief causes of the prevailing ignorance was the scarcity of books. The old libraries were destroyed by ruthless barbarians and the ravages of war. After the conquest of Alexandria by the Saracens, the cultivation and exportation of Egyptian papyrus ceased, and parchment or vellum, which took its place, was so expensive that complete copies of the Bible cost as much as a palace or a farm. King Alfred paid eight acres of land for one volume of a cosmography. Hence the custom of chaining valuable books, which continued even to the sixteenth century. Hence also the custom of erasing the original text of manuscripts of classical works, to give place to worthless monkish legends and ascetic homilies. Even the Bible was sometimes submitted to this process, and thus "the word of God was made void by the traditions of men."(35)

The libraries of conventual and cathedral schools were often limited to half a dozen or a dozen volumes, such as a Latin Bible or portions of it, the liturgical books, some works of St. Augustin and St. Gregory, Cassiodorus and Boëthius, the grammars of Donatus and Priscianus, the poems of Virgil and Horace. Most of the books had to be imported from Italy, especially from Rome.

The introduction of cotton paper in the tenth or eleventh century, and of linen paper in the twelfth, facilitated the multiplication of books.(36)

§ 139. Educational Efforts of the Church.

The mediaeval church is often unjustly charged with hostility to secular learning. Pope Gregory I. is made responsible for the destruction of the Bibliotheca Palatina and the classical statues in Rome. But this rests on an unreliable tradition of very late date.(37) Gregory was himself, next to Isidore of Seville (on whom he conferred the pall, in 599), the best scholar and most popular writer of his age, and is lauded by his biographers and Gregory of Tours as a patron of learning. If he made some disparaging remarks about Latin grammar and syntax, in two letters addressed to bishops, they must be understood as a protest against an overestimate of these lower studies and of heathen writers, as compared with higher episcopal duties, and with that allegorical interpretation of the Bible which he carried to arbitrary excess in his own exposition of Job.(38) In the Commentary on Kings ascribed to him, he commends the study of the liberal arts as a useful and necessary means for the proper understanding of the Scriptures, and refers in support to the examples of Moses, Isaiah, and St. Paul.(39) We may say then that he was an advocate of learning and art, but in subordination and subserviency to the interests of the Catholic church. This has been the attitude of the papal chair ever since.(40)

The preservation and study of ancient literature during the entire mediaeval period are due chiefly to the clergy and monks, and a few secular rulers. The convents were the nurseries of manuscripts.

The connection with classical antiquity was never entirely broken. Boëthius (beheaded at Pavia, c. 525), and Cassiodorus (who retired to the monastery, of Viviers, and died there about 570), both statesmen under Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king of Italy, form the connecting links between ancient and mediaeval learning. They were the last of the old Romans; they dipped the pen of Cicero and Seneca in barbaric ink,(41) and stimulated the rising energies of the Romanic and Germanic nations: Boëthius by his "Consolation of Philosophy" (written in prison),(42) Cassiodorus by his encyclopedic "Institutes of Divine Letters," a brief introduction to the profitable study of the Holy Scriptures.(43) The former looked back to Greek philosophy; the latter looked forward to Christian theology. The influence of their writings was enhanced by the scarcity of books beyond their intrinsic merits.

Boëthius has had the singular fortune of enjoying the reputation of a saint and martyr who was put to death, not for alleged political treason, but for defending orthodoxy against the Arianism of Theodoric. He is assigned by Dante to the fourth heaven in company with Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, Gratian, Peter the Lombard, Dionysius the Areopagite, and other great teachers of the church:

"The saintly soul that maketh manifest

The world’s deceitfulness to all who hear well,

Is feasting on the sight of every good.

The body, whence it was expelled, is lying

Down in Cieldauro, and from martyrdom

And exile rose the soul to such a peace."(44)

And yet it is doubtful whether Boëthius was a Christian at all. He was indeed intimate with Cassiodorus and lived in a Christian atmosphere, which accounts for the moral elevation of his philosophy. But, if we except a few Christian phrases,(45) his "Consolation" might almost have been written by a noble heathen of the school of Plato or Seneca. It is an echo of Greek philosophy; it takes an optimistic view of life; it breathes a beautiful spirit of resignation and hope, and derives comfort from a firm belief in God; in an all-ruling providence, and in prayer, but is totally silent about Christ and his gospel.(46) It is a dialogue partly in prose and partly in verse between the author and philosophy in the garb of a dignified woman (who sets as his celestial guide, like Dante’s Beatrice). The work enjoyed an extraordinary popularity throughout the middle ages, and was translated into several languages, Greek, Old High German (by Notker of St. Gall), Anglo-Saxon (by King Alfred), Norman English (by Chaucer), French (by Meun), and Hebrew (by Ben Banshet). Gibbon admires it all the more for its ignoring Christianity, and calls it "a golden volume not unworthy of the leisure of Plato or Tully, but which claims incomparable merit from the barbarism of the times and the situation of the author. The celestial guide whom he had so long invoked at Rome and Athens, now condescended to illumine his dungeon, to revive his courage, and to pour into his wounds her salutary balm .... From the earth Boëthius ascended to heaven in search of the Supreme Good; explored the metaphysical labyrinth of chance and destiny, of prescience and freewill, of time and eternity; and generously attempted to reconcile the perfect attributes of Deity with the apparent disorders of his moral and physical government."(47)

Greek And Hebrew Learning.

The original languages of the Scriptures were little understood in the West. The Latin took the place of the Greek as a literary and sacred language, and formed a bond of union among scholars of different nationalities. As a spoken language it rapidly degenerated under the influx of barbaric dialects, but gave birth in the course of time to the musical Romanic languages of Southern Europe.

The Hebrew, which very few of the fathers (Origen and Jerome) had understood, continued to live in the Synagogue, and among eminent Jewish grammarians and commentators of the Old Testament; but it was not revived in the Christian Church till shortly before the Reformation. Very few of the divines of our period (Isidore, and, perhaps, Scotus Erigena), show any trace of Hebrew learning.

The Greek, which had been used almost exclusively, even by writers of the Western church, till the time of Tertullian and Cyprian, gave way to the Latin. Hence the great majority of Western divines could not read even the New Testament in the original. Pope Gregory did not know Greek, although he lived several years as papal ambassador in Constantinople. The same is true of most of the schoolmen down to the sixteenth century.

But there were not a few honorable exceptions.(48) The Monotheletic and Iconoclastic controversies brought the Greek and the Latin churches into lively contact. The conflict between Photius and Nicolas stimulated Latin divines to self-defence.

As to Italy, the Greek continued to be spoken in the Greek colonies in Calabria and Sicily down to the eleventh century. Boëthius was familiar with the Greek philosophers. Cassiodorus often gives the Greek equivalents for Latin technical terms.(49)

Several popes of this period were Greeks by birth, as Theodore I. (642), John VI. (701), John VII. (705), Zachary (741); while others were Syrians, as John V. (685), Sergius I. (687), Sisinnius (708), Constantine I. (708), Gregory III. (731). Zachary translated Gregory’s "Dialogues" from Latin into Greek. Pope Paul I. (757–768) took pains to spread a knowledge of Greek and sent several Greek books, including a grammar, some works of Aristotle, and Dionysius the Areopagite, to King Pepin of France. He provided Greek service for several monks who had been banished from the East by the iconoclastic emperor Copronymus. Anastasius, librarian of the Vatican, translated the canons of the eighth general Council of Constantinople (869) into Latin by order of Pope Hadrian II.(50)

Isidore of Seville (d. 636) mentions a learned Spanish bishop, John of Gerona, who in his youth had studied seven years in Constantinople. He himself quotes in his "Etymologies" from many Greek authors, and is described as "learned in Latin, Greek, and Hebrew."

Ireland was for a long time in advance of England, and sent learned missionaries to the sister island as well as to the Continent. That Greek was not unknown there, is evident from Scotus Erigena.

England derived her knowledge of Greek from Archbishop Theodore, who was a native of Tarsus, educated in Athens and appointed by the pope to the see of Canterbury (a.d. 668).(51) He and his companion Hadrian,(52) an Italian abbot of African descent, spread Greek learning among the clergy. Bede says that some of their disciples were living in his day who were as well versed in Greek and Latin as in their native Saxon. Among these must be mentioned Aldhelm, bishop of Sherborne, and Tobias, bishop of Rochester (d. 726).(53) The Venerable Bede (d. 735) gives evidence of Greek knowledge in his commentaries,(54) his references to a Greek Codex of the Acts of the Apostles, and especially in his book on the Art of Poetry.(55) In France, Greek began to be studied under Charles the Great. Alcuin (d. 804) brought some knowledge of it from his native England, but his references may all have been derived from Jerome and Cassiodorus.(56) Paulus Diaconus frequently uses Greek words. Charlemagne himself learned Greek, and the Libri Carolini show a familiarity with the details of the image-controversy of the Greek Church. His sister Giesela, who was abbess of Challes near Paris, uses a few Greek words in Latin letters,(57) in her correspondence with Alcuin, though these may have been derived from the Latin.

The greatest Greek scholar of the ninth century, and of the whole period in the West was John Scotus Erigena (850), who was of Irish birth and education, but lived in France at the court of Charles the Bald. He displays his knowledge in his Latin books, translated the pseudo-Dionysian writings, and attempted original Greek composition.

In Germany, Rabanus Maurus, Haymo of Halberstadt, and Walafrid Strabo had some knowledge of Greek, but not sufficient to be of any material use in the interpretation of the Scriptures.

The Course of Study.(58)

Education was carried on in the cathedral and conventual schools, and these prepared the way for the Universities which began to be founded in the twelfth century.

The course of secular learning embraced the so-called seven liberal arts, namely, grammar, dialectics (logic), rhetoric, music, arithmetic, geometry, and astronomy. The first three constituted the Trivium, the other four the Quadrivium.(59) Seven, three, and four were all regarded as sacred numbers. The division is derived from St. Augustin,(60) and was adopted by Boëthius and Cassiodorus. The first and most popular compend of the middle ages was the book of Cassiodorus, De Septem Disciplinis.(61)

These studies were preparatory to sacred learning, which was based upon the Latin Bible and the Latin fathers.

The Chief Theologians.

A few divines embraced all the secular and religious knowledge of their age. In Spain, Isidore of Seville (d. 636) was the most learned man at the end of the sixth and the beginning of the seventh century. His twenty books of "Origins" or "Etymologies" embrace the entire contents of the seven liberal arts, together with theology, jurisprudence, medicine, natural history, etc., and show familiarity with Plato, Aristotle, Boëthius, Demosthenes, Homer, Hesiod, Pindar, Anacreon, Herodotus, Cicero, Horace, Virgil, Ovid, Terence, Juvenal, Caesar, Livy, Sallust.(62) The Venerable Bede occupied the same height of encyclopaedic knowledge a century later. Alcuin was the leading divine of the Carolingian age. From his school proceeded RABANUS MAURUS, the founder of learning and higher education in Germany.(63) Scotus Erigena (d. about 877) was a marvel not only of learning, but also of independent thought, in the reign of Charles the Bald, and showed, by prophetic anticipation, the latent capacity of the Western church for speculative theology.(64) With Berengar and Lanfranc, in the middle of the eleventh century, dialectical skill was applied in opposing and defending the dogma of transubstantiation.(65) The doctrinal controversies about adoptionism, predestination, and the real presence stimulated the study of the Scriptures and of the fathers, and kept alive the intellectual activity.

Biblical Studies.

The literature of the Latin church embraced penitential books, homilies, annals, translations, compilations, polemic discussions, and commentaries. The last are the most important, but fall far below the achievements of the fathers and reformers.

Exegesis was cultivated in an exclusively practical and homiletical spirit and aim by Gregory the Great, Isidore, Bede, Alcuin, Claudius of Turin, Paschasius Radbertus, Rabanus Maurus, Haymo, Walafrid Strabo, and others. The Latin Vulgate was the text, and the Greek or Hebrew seldom referred to. Augustin and Jerome were the chief sources. Charlemagne felt the need of a revision of the corrupt text of the Vulgate, and entrusted Alcuin with the task. The theory of a verbal inspiration was generally accepted, and opposed only by Agobard of Lyons who confined inspiration to the sense and the arguments, but not to the "ipsa corporalia verba."

The favorite mode of interpretation was the spiritual, that is, allegorical and mystical. The literal, that is, grammatico-historical exegesis was neglected. The spiritual interpretation was again divided into three ramifications: the allegorical proper, the moral, and the anagogical(66) corresponding to the three cardinal virtues of the Christian: the first refers to faith (credenda), the second to practice or charity (agenda), the third to hope (speranda, desideranda). Thus Jerusalem means literally or historically, the city in Palestine; allegorically, the church; morally, the believing soul; anagogically, the heavenly Jerusalem. The fourfold sense was expressed in the memorial verse:

"Litera Gesta docet; quid Credas, Allegoria;

Moralis, quid Agas; quo Tendas, Anagogia."

§ 140. Patronage of Letters by Charles the Great, and Charles the Bald.

Comp. §§ 56, 90, 134 (pp. 236, 390, 584).

Charlemagne stands out like a far-shining beacon-light in the darkness of his age. He is the founder of a new era of learning, as well as of a new empire. He is the pioneer of French and German civilization. Great in war, he was greater still as a legislator and promoter of the arts of peace. He clearly saw that religion and education are the only solid and permanent basis of a state. In this respect he rose far above Alexander the Great and Caesar, and is unsurpassed by Christian rulers.

He invited the best scholars from Italy and England to his court,—Peter of Pisa, Paul Warnefrid, Paulinus of Aquileia, Theodulph of Orleans, Alcuin of York.(67) They formed a sort of royal academy of sciences and arts, and held literary symposiacs. Each member bore a nom de plume borrowed from the Bible or classic lore: the king presided as "David" or "Solomon"; Alcuin, a great admirer of Horace and Virgil, was "Flaccus" Angilbert (his son-in-law) was "Homerus"; Einhard (his biographer), "Bezaleel," after the skilful artificer of the Tabernacle (Ex. 31:2); Wizo, "Candidus"; Arno, "Aquila"; Fredegisus, "Nathanael"; Richbod, "Macarius," etc. Even ladies were not excluded: the emperor’s sister, Gisela, under the name "Lucia"; his learned cousin, Gundrad, as "Eulalia;" his daughter, Rotrude, as "Columba." He called Alcuin, whom he first met in Italy (781), his own "beloved teacher," and he was himself his most docile pupil. He had an insatiable thirst for knowledge, and put all sorts of questions to him in his letters, even on the most difficult problems of theology. He learned in the years of his manhood the art of writing, the Latin grammar, a little Greek (that he might compare the Latin Testament with the original), and acquired some knowledge of rhetoric, dialectics, mathematics and astronomy. He delighted in reading the poets and historians of ancient Rome, and Augustin’s "City of God." He longed for a dozen Jeromes and Augustins, but Alcuin told him to be content since the Creator of heaven and earth had been pleased to give to the world only two such giants. He had some share in the composition of the Libri Carolini, which raised an enlightened protest against the superstition of image-worship. Poems are also attributed to him or to his inspiration. He ordered Paul Warnefrid (Paulus Diaconus) to prepare a collection of the best homilies of the Latin fathers for the use of the churches, and published it with a preface in which he admonished the clergy to a diligent study of the Scriptures. Several Synods held during his reign (813) at Rheims, Tours, Chalons, Mainz, ordered the clergy to keep a Homiliarium and to translate the Latin sermons clearly into rusticam Romanam linguam aut Theotiscam, so that all might understand them.

Charles aimed at the higher education not only of the clergy, but also of the higher nobility, and state officials. His sons and daughters were well informed. He issued a circular letter to all the bishops and abbots of his empire (787), urging them to establish schools in connection with cathedrals and convents. At a later period he rose even to the grand but premature scheme of popular education, and required in a capitulary (802) that every parent should send his sons to school that they might learn to read. Theodulph of Orleans (who died 821) directed the priests of his diocese to hold school in every town and village,(68) to receive the pupils with kindness, and not to ask pay, but to receive only voluntary gifts.

The emperor founded the Court or Palace School (Schola Palatina) for higher education and placed it under the direction of Alcuin.(69) It was an imitation of the Paedagogium ingenuorum of the Roman emperors. It followed him in his changing residence to Aix-la-Chapelle, Worms, Frankfurt, Mainz, Regensburg, Ingelheim, Paris. It was not the beginning of the Paris University, which is of much later date, but the chief nursery of educated clergymen, noblemen and statesmen of that age. It embraced in its course of study all the branches of secular and sacred learning.(70) It became the model of similar schools, old and new, at Tours, Lyons, Orleans, Rheims, Chartres, Troyes, Old Corbey and New Corbey, Metz, St. Gall, Utrecht, Lüttich.(71) The rich literature of the Carolingian age shows the fruits of this imperial patronage and example. It was, however, a foreign rather than a native product. It was neither French nor German, but essentially Latin, and so far artificial. Nor could it be otherwise; for the Latin classics, the Latin Bible, and the Latin fathers were the only accessible sources of learning, and the French and German languages were not yet organs of literature. This fact explains the speedy decay, as well as the subsequent revival in close connection with the Roman church.

The creations of Charlemagne were threatened with utter destruction during the civil wars of his weak successors. But Charles the Bald, a son of Louis the Pious, and king of France (843–877), followed his grandfather in zeal for learning, and gave new lustre to the Palace School at Paris under the direction of John Scotus Erigena, whom he was liberal enough to protect, notwithstanding his eccentricities. The predestinarian controversy, and the first eucharistic controversy took place during his reign, and called forth a great deal of intellectual activity and learning, as shown in the writings of Rabanus Maurus, Hincmar, Remigius, Prudentius, Servatus Lupus, John Scotus Erigena, Paschasius Radbertus, and Ratramnus. We find among these writers the three tendencies, conservative, liberal, and speculative or mystic, which usually characterize periods of intellectual energy and literary productivity.

After the death of Charles the Bald a darker night of ignorance and barbarism settled on Europe than ever before. It lasted till towards the middle of the eleventh century when the Berengar controversy on the eucharist roused the slumbering intellectual energies of the church, and prepared the way for the scholastic philosophy and theology of the twelfth century.

The Carolingian male line lasted in Italy till 875, in Germany till 911, in France till 987.

§ 141. Alfred the Great, and Education in England.

Comp. the Jubilee edition of the Whole Works of Alfred the Great, with Preliminary Essays illustrative of the History, Arts and Manners of the Ninth Century. London, 1858, 2 vols. The biographies of Alfred, quoted on p. 395, and Freemann’s Old English History 1859.

In England the beginning of culture was imported with Christianity by Augustin, the first archbishop of Canterbury, who brought with him the Bible, the church books, the writings of Pope Gregory and the doctrines and practices of Roman Christianity; but little progress was made for a century. Among his successors the Greek monk, Theodore of Tarsus (668–690), was most active in promoting education and discipline among the clergy. The most distinguished scholar of the Saxon period is the Venerable Bede (d. 735), who, as already stated, represented all historical, exegetical and general knowledge of his age. Egbert, archbishop of York, founded a flourishing school in York (732), from which proceeded Alcuin, the teacher and friend of Charlemagne.

During the invasion of the heathen Danes and Normans many churches, convents and libraries were destroyed, and the clergy itself relapsed into barbarism so that they did not know the meaning of the Latin formulas which they used in public worship.

In this period of wild confusion King Alfred the Great (871–901), in his twenty-second year, ascended the throne. He is first in war and first in peace of all the Anglo-Saxon rulers. What Charlemagne was for Germany and France, Alfred was for England. He conquered the forces of the Danes by land and by sea, delivered his country from foreign rule, and introduced a new era of Christian education. He invited scholars from the old British churches in Wales, from Ireland, and the Continent to influential positions. He made collections of choice sentences from the Bible and the fathers. In his thirty-sixth year he learned Latin from Asser, a monk of Wales, who afterwards wrote his biography. He himself, no doubt with the aid of scholars, translated several standard works from Latin into the Anglo-Saxon, and accompanied them with notes, namely a part of the Psalter, Boëthius on the Consolation of Philosophy, Bede’s English Church History, Pope Gregory’s Pastoral Theology, Augustin’s Meditations, the Universal History of Orosius, and Aesop’s Fables. He sent a copy of Gregory’s Pastoral Theology to every diocese for the benefit of the clergy. It is due to his influence chiefly that the Scriptures and service-books at this period were illustrated by so many vernacular glosses.

He stood in close connection with the Roman see, as the centre of ecclesiastical unity and civilization. He devoted half of his income to church and school. He founded a school in Oxford similar to the Schola Palatina; but the University of Oxford, like those of Cambridge and Paris, is of much later date (twelfth or thirteenth century). He seems to have conceived even the plan of a general education of the people.(72) Amid great physical infirmity (he had the epilepsy), he developed an extraordinary activity during a reign of twenty-nine years, and left an enduring fame for purity, and piety of character and unselfish devotion to the best interests of his people.(73)

His example of promoting learning in the vernacular language was followed by Aelfric, a grammarian, homilist and hagiographer. He has been identified with the archbishop Aelfric of Canterbury (996–1009), and with the archbishop Aelfric of York (1023–1051), but there are insuperable difficulties in either view. He calls himself simply "monk and priest." He left behind him a series of eighty Anglo-Saxon Homilies for Sundays and great festivals, and another series for Anglo-Saxon Saints’ days, which were used as an authority in the Anglo-Saxon Church.(74)
