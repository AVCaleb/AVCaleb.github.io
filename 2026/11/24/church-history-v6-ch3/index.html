§ 20. 奥卡姆与经院哲学的衰落

邓斯·司各脱 (Duns Scotus，卒于1308年) 是经院哲学最后一位伟大的代表。他之后，经院方法逐渐失去声望。新的问题涌到西欧人的心头，新的关切吸引着他们的注意。学院和修道院中的神学家，让位于在小册子中、在会议场上阐述见解的实际神学论辩者。自由讨论打破了教义断言的霸权。教父和教宗制度的权威不再具有独占的支配力；思想家转而在当代基督教世界的共同判断中、唯独在圣经中，或在理性中寻求另一种权威根据。人们对文学和自然世界的新兴趣，使他们不再专注于那些煞费苦心构建的神学体系；这些体系与其说对社会具有实际价值，不如说更适于展示神学家的机巧才智。欧洲各地口语在文学中的运用，有助于迫使思想依照当前需要而成形。罗杰·培根 (Roger Bacon) 的论述表明，十四世纪初，人们对以深奥的形而上学解决或大或小的神学问题已感厌倦，正转向一个更真实、可以验证的世界。

辩证派经院学者中，留存下来的主要人物是杜兰杜斯 (Durandus) 和威廉·奥卡姆 (William Ockam)。蒂宾根的加布里埃尔·比尔 (Gabriel Biel) 在十五世纪结束前不久去世，通常被称为最后一位经院学者。1 达伊、热尔松和威克里夫 (Wyclif) 等人，有时也被列入中世纪经院学者之中，但他们显然属于另一类。

杜兰杜斯和奥卡姆的经院哲学有一个显著特征，就是他们更鲜明地区分理性与启示。他们追随邓斯·司各脱，宣称启示神学所特有的教义，不能用纯粹理性加以证明。至于教会所接受的教义真理体系，他们并未提出质疑。

第二个特征是缺乏独创性。他们只是详加阐发所承受的思想。此前各时期的经院学者，已经穷尽了神学问题的题目，并从各个角度加以讨论。

第三个特征是唯名论 (nominalism) 的复兴及其占据优势；这一原理，早在二百多年前便由罗瑟林 (Roscellinus) 提倡。唯名论者又称词项论者 (Terminists)，因为他们认为，语词只是词项，不一定有与之对应的观念和实在。共相只是代表若干事物、或代表若干事物共同之处的符号或词项。2 普遍性不过是心智构想的一种方式。巴黎大学抵制唯名论的传播；1839年，四个同乡会禁止传播奥卡姆的学说，也禁止听取公开或私下对这一学说的讲解。3 1473年，路易十一 (Louis XI.) 颁布敕令，禁止巴黎的博士讲授这一学说，并禁止使用奥卡姆、马西利乌斯 (Marsiglius) 及其他作者的著作。1481年，这项法令被撤销。

杜兰杜斯，号称“坚毅博士”——doctor resolutissimus，卒于1334年，生于克莱蒙 (Clermont) 教区的普尔桑 (Pourçain)，加入多明我会，1317年由约翰二十二世 (Fohn XXII.) 任命为利穆 (Limoux) 主教，后来又升任勒皮 (Puy) 和莫城 (Meaux) 的主教。他抨击方济各会的某些规章，以及约翰二十二世关于荣福直观 (beatific vision) 的理论；1333年，一个委员会判定他犯有十一项错误。他的神学见解见于他对伦巴德 (Lombard) 的注释；这部著作始于青年时期，到晚年才完成。他抨击托马斯·阿奎那 (Thomas Aquinas) 的某些观点，显示出独立精神。他把圣经置于传统之上，宣称圣经的陈述比亚里士多德及其他哲学家的论断更具权威，在这方面超越了他的前辈。4 一切真实的存在都在个体之中。共相并不是一种实体，可以像一块木头那样被切成碎块。共相，即把诸对象归为一类的统一性，是心智通过活动从个体中推导出来的。一个类别所共有的东西，离开这一类别中的个体，就没有真实的存在。

在圣餐教义上，杜兰杜斯似乎并不完全满意教会所持的观点，并提出“这是我的身体”这句话可能意味着“包含在此之下”——contentum sub hoc。这表明他已接近路德 (Luther) 的同质说 (consubstantiation)。热尔松极为推重这位神学家，把他与托马斯·阿奎那、布拉德沃丁 (Bradwardine) 和根特的亨利 (Henry of Ghent) 一同推荐给纳瓦拉学院 (college of Navarre) 的学生。5

十四世纪最深邃的经院思想家，是英国人威廉·奥卡姆 (William Ockam)，卒于1349年，被称为“不可战胜的博士”——doctor invincibilis；又因他提倡唯名论，被称为“可敬的开创者”——venerabilis inceptor。他的著作篇幅浩繁，却不甚明晰，在十五世纪末曾大量刊印，但此后数百年间未再付印，也没有全集。奥卡姆的思想兼有严格属于中世纪的成分，以及后来为改教家和近代哲学所采纳的成分。他支持方济各会属灵派 (Spiritual Franciscans) 的事业，因此卷入了与两位教皇约翰二十二世 (John XXII.) 和本笃十二世 (Benedict XII.) 的争论。他否认教皇无谬，看来与其说是出于神学信念的教义，不如说是在冲突中偶然拾取的一件武器，用以维护属灵派的事业。

关于奥卡姆的早年生活，人们所知甚少。他生于萨里 (Surrey)，在牛津 (Oxford) 求学，可能曾师从邓斯·司各脱 (Duns Scotus)，后来加入方济各会，1315–1320年间可能在巴黎任教。由于提倡基督绝对贫穷的教义，他奉约翰二十二世之命受审，被判有罪，遭到囚禁。6 1328年，在巴伐利亚的路易 (Lewis the Bavarian) 帮助下，他与同伴切塞纳的米迦勒 (Michael of Cesena) 和博纳格拉提亚 (Bonagratia) 逃往比萨 (Pisa)。如前所述，从此皇帝与这位经院学者彼此维护。奥卡姆随皇帝前往慕尼黑 (Munich)，并遭到绝罚。切塞纳的米迦勒去世时，方济各会的印信转入他手中，但翌年，他便将自己所掌握的一切权力交还公认的方济各会总会长法里内里乌斯 (Farinerius)。克雷芒六世 (Clement VI.) 提出，只要他宣誓弃绝自己的谬误，便可获得赦免。他是否接受了这一条件，已无从得知。他死于慕尼黑，也葬在那里。这位杰出的英国人，其声名来自他对唯名论的复兴、他的政治理论，以及他对宗教最终权威之所在的界定。

他的唯名论主张十分明确，对自安瑟伦 (Anselm) 以来伟大经院学者们的实在论毫不宽容。唯有个别事物才有实际的存在。共相只是词项或符号，是心智的虚构——fictiones, signa mentalia, nomina, signa verbalia，即虚构、心智的符号、名称、言语的符号。它们好比镜中的影像。共相所表示的，只是一种理智活动——actus intelligenda，此外别无他物。如果观念作为独立的实体存在于上帝心中，那么可见的世界便是由这些观念造出来的，而不是从无中创造的。7

奥卡姆追随邓斯·司各脱，教导决定论 (determinism)。上帝绝对的意志，使万物成为其所是。上帝若如此选择，基督也可以成为木头或石头。尽管亚里士多德 (Aristotle) 持相反意见，一个物体仍可能同时作不同种类的运动。在道德领域，现今为恶的事，上帝若如此定意，也本可以是善的。

在世俗政权方面，奥卡姆赞同选帝侯们1338年在伦塞 (Rense) 所采取的立场，宣称皇帝不需要教皇的确认。皇帝的职权直接来自上帝。8 教会是祭司性的机构，施行圣礼，向人指示救恩之路，却没有世俗司法权，9 即potestas coactiva（强制权）。

这位思想家认为，最终的权威在于圣经。三位一体和道成肉身这样的真理，不能由论证推导出来。上帝的存在不能从所谓上帝的观念得到证明。理性既可以证明独一上帝的存在，同样也可以证明有许多神。教皇和大公会议都可能犯错。唯独圣经没有错误。不能要求基督徒相信圣经中所没有的事。10

教会是信徒的共同体——communitas，或congregatio fidelium。11 罗马教会不等同于教会，这一基督徒群体可以独立于罗马教会而存在。如果教皇拥有全权，福音的律法就会比摩西的律法更令人难以忍受。那时所有人都会成为教皇的奴隶。12 教皇制并不是必不可少的制度。

在圣餐教义上，奥卡姆认为，传统观点不如基督的身体与饼并存的观点更为可信。这种由多伊茨的鲁珀特 (Rupert of Deutz) 所教导的寓饼说 (impanation)，接近路德的同质说 (consubstantiation)。然而，奥卡姆仍接受教会的观点，因为它更难理解，也因为上帝的能力是无限的。巴黎的约翰 (John of Paris)，卒于1308年，曾把基督在饼酒中的临在，比作道成肉身时两种本性的共存，并于1304年被撤去巴黎大学的教席。加布里埃尔·比尔 (Gabriel Biel) 也持类似的观点。13

奥卡姆关于世俗权力的权威、教皇可能犯错、圣经无谬以及圣餐的观点，常被拿来与路德的观点比较。14 这位德国改教家称这位英国经院学者“无疑是经院博士中的领袖，也是才思最敏捷的一位”——scholasticorum doctorum sine dubio princeps et ingeniosissimus。他称奥卡姆为自己“亲爱的老师”，并宣称自己属于奥卡姆一派——sum Occamicae factionis。15 然而，两人却截然不同。奥卡姆是理论家，而不是改革者；尽管他有大胆的言论，仍不失为中世纪时代的产儿。他在神学事务上没有开创任何派别或学派。路德高举个人对永活基督的信仰。他在圣经中发现新的原则，使之成为个人和民族信仰与实践中的活跃力量。我们可以设想，路德若生活在十四世纪，会成为一位奥卡姆；却不能设想奥卡姆会成为十六世纪的改教家。他大概不会舍弃修道生活。奥卡姆的功绩在于，他与马西利乌斯 (Marsiglius) 及其他思想领袖一样，吸收了自由讨论的新精神，并且敢于抨击当时的传统教条。这样，他便促使中世纪那种关于权威之所在的有害理论发生动摇。

§ 21. 锡耶纳的圣女凯瑟琳

在意大利诸圣徒中，声名仅次于阿西西的方济各 (Francis d’Assisi) 的，是锡耶纳的凯瑟琳 (Catherine of Siena)——Caterina da Siena，1347–1380年。她与比她早一个多世纪的图林根的伊丽莎白 (Elizabeth of Thuringia)，同为中世纪被教会册封的圣洁女子中最杰出者。她的声名在于她专一的虔诚，以及她为促进教会和本民族利益所作的努力。她没有留下任何修会来维系人们对她名字的崇敬。她是中世纪意大利所有妇女中最活跃于公共生活的一位，却能往来于街巷与宫廷之间，毫发无损，清白无玷。她时而以锡耶纳一位卑微市民之女的身份，服侍穷人和病者；时而又作为上天的女先知，向教皇和各共和国的良知发出呼吁。她的锡耶纳同乡以芬芳的称号la beata poplana，即“蒙福的人民之女”，尊她为圣。她的告解神父兼传记作者所传述的事迹，虽有许多看来近于传说，她的虔诚中也可能并非全无歇斯底里的成分，但凡见到高尚热忱而为之感动的人，仍都应当、也必将敬佩她。一个人若读到她不倦努力的记述，以及她同样倾注了灵魂之火的书信，却不觉得这位锡耶纳圣女是一位非凡的女子，是她那个时代的弗洛伦斯·南丁格尔 (Florence Nightingale)，甚至更胜于此，那么他必定苛刻得近乎狂热。正如最近为她作传的一位英国作者所断言的，她是“有史以来最奇妙的女子之一”。或者，我们是否应当与那位深入研究中世纪罗马的格雷戈罗维乌斯 (Gregorovius) 一同说：“凯瑟琳的身影如天使一般，掠过她那个时代的黑暗；她那温婉的才性，在黑暗之上洒下柔和的光辉。与她同时代教皇们的生平相比，她的一生更值得载入史册，也无疑是更富人情的历史题材。”16

凯瑟琳·贝宁卡萨 (Catherine Benincasa) 在家中二十五个孩子里排行第二十三。她的孪生姐妹乔万娜 (Giovanna) 在婴儿时便夭折了。她父亲是染匠，家境富裕。母亲蒙娜·拉帕 (Monna Lapa) 比女儿活得更久。凯瑟琳对母亲孝敬有加，曾给她写信，其中数封至今尚存；旅行时也带着母亲，在罗马度过最后岁月时，母亲亦在身旁。凯瑟琳未受过学校教育，直到成年以后才学会读写。

她自幼便容易受到宗教的感动，常到父家附近的多明我会教堂去。她幼年时期的神迹，由她的告解神父兼传记作者卡普阿的雷蒙德 (Raymund of Capua) 记述下来。十二岁时，父母为她安排婚事，凯瑟琳为逃避这门婚事，剪去了自己美丽的头发。她加入多明我会第三会，其中的女会员因身披黑色斗篷，被称为mantellate（披斗篷的女子）。雷蒙德说：“大自然并没有赐给她一张格外美丽的脸。”她的容貌又因天花留下的瘢痕而受损。然而，她神情可亲，生性开朗，唱歌欢笑都十分爽朗。献身宗教生活后，她便实行严厉的苦修，每日鞭打自己三次——一次为自己，一次为活人，一次为死人。她身穿毛制苦衣，佩戴铁链。有一年大斋期，她仅靠领圣餐时所吃的饼度日。这些苦修都是在父亲家中的一间房里进行的。她从未住过修道院。到了晚年，她不再赞许自己曾实行的这类极端苦修。

凯瑟琳很年轻时便开始经历异象和启示。有一次，她受到过寻常少女生活的诱惑，经过数小时可怕的试探后，救主向她显现，身体伸展在十字架上，说：“我自己的女儿凯瑟琳，你看见我为你受了多少苦吗？不要以为为我受苦是难事。”她因这番话深受感动，问道：“主啊，我受这般污秽的试探时，你在哪里？”他回答说：“在你心里。”据她自己的陈述，1367年，救主与她订婚，把戒指戴在她的指头上。此后，她始终能看见这枚戒指，别人却看不见。去世前五年，她直接从基督领受了圣痕 (stigmata)。圣痕的印记使她感到剧痛；凯瑟琳坚称，虽然别人同样看不见这些印记，它们对她却是真实的。

凯瑟琳遵从一次启示，放弃了此前隐居的生活，二十岁时开始公开活动，积极从事慈善工作。这是在1367年。她探访穷人和病者，不久便以全城服侍众人的天使而闻名。1374年瘟疫流行期间，她日夜不倦，医好了那些已被医生认为无望的人，甚至使死人复活。她也没有忽略城墙外的麻风病人。

她生平有一件非凡的事，她在写给雷蒙德的一封信中亲自证实了此事，那就是她对尼科洛·图尔多 (Niccolo Tuldo) 的关怀。这位年轻贵族因发表不敬市政府的言论而被判处死刑。青年本已绝望，但在凯瑟琳的影响下，他不仅恢复了镇静，甚至欢喜地期待死亡。凯瑟琳在断头台旁陪着他，托住他的头。她写道：“我刚刚把一颗头颅接到手中，它给我的甘甜，是任何心灵都想象不到、任何舌头都无法形容的。”行刑前，她陪这位不幸的人参加弥撒，他在那里第一次领受了圣餐。他最后说的“无非是耶稣和凯瑟琳。他这样说着，”他的恩人写道，“我便将他的头颅接在手中。”随后，她看见基督接纳了他；她又写道：“当他安息时，我的灵魂也安息于平静之中，鲜血的馨香如此浓郁，使我不忍拭去他身上落在我身上的血。”

这样一位女子的声名，是她故乡的城墙所不能局限的。邻近各城，甚至阿维尼翁 (Avignon) 的教皇，都听闻了她的慈善事迹和所受的启示。凯瑟琳原是寻求宗教慰藉者的心灵向导，也是病者和垂死之人的服侍者，如今则进入了更广阔的领域，参与意大利的政治生活，并为教会的福祉效力。她一方面努力支持教皇制度，另一方面则力求改善教士的状况、建立和平。她怀着先知般的热忱，敦促格列高利十一世 (Gregory XI.) 返回罗马。她设法阻止托斯卡纳 (Tuscan) 各城反抗阿维尼翁教皇，并谋求撤销对佛罗伦萨 (Florence) 颁布的禁令；她又支持乌尔班六世 (Urban VI.)，反对敌对教皇克雷芒七世 (Clement VII.)。她以同样的热情，敦促格列高利改革教士，在选任枢机和牧者时，丝毫不受买卖圣职和谄媚奉承的影响，并“把那些豺狼、那些化身为人的魔鬼逐出羊圈；他们只顾佳肴美酒、盛大的宴席和华丽的侍从制服”。她还热切地努力点燃新一次十字军东征的火焰。她致信雇佣兵首领、令意大利半岛闻风丧胆的约翰·霍克伍德爵士 (Sir John Hawkwood)，劝他既然如此喜爱征战，从今以后便不应再向基督徒动武，而应转而攻击异教徒。她也就此事与塞浦路斯女王通信。她再三敦促格列高利十一世采取行动，主要理由是，他可以“将羔羊的血施予那些可怜的异教徒”，而他们归信之后，便可协助将骄傲和其他恶习逐出基督教世界。17

受格列高利委托，她前往比萨 (Pisa)，劝该城支持教皇。大主教和共和国的元首隆重接待了她；两位教授原本前来要向她证明她是在自欺，或比自欺更糟，结果也被她折服了。她告诉他们，知道神如何创造世界，对她并不重要；但“知道神的儿子取了我们的人性，为我们的救恩而生、而死，却是不可少的”。其中一位教授摘下深红色天鹅绒帽子，跪在她面前，请求宽恕。凯瑟琳医治病人的事迹赢得了民众的信任。这次出访，她的母亲和一群志同道合的女子与她同行。

凯瑟琳生平中的一大篇章，与佛罗伦萨的历史交织在一起。意大利北部反抗阿维尼翁统治的情绪日益高涨；在一个荒年，驻博洛尼亚 (Bologna) 的教皇使节禁止向佛罗伦萨运送粮食，于是战争爆发了。应佛罗伦萨人的邀请，凯瑟琳于1375年访问该城，一年后又作为代表被派往阿维尼翁，商议和平条件。教皇虽有疑虑，仍以礼接待了她。代表团的其他成员抵达后，却拒绝承认她的权限，也不赞同她的方法。枢机们待她冷淡，或加以鄙视；一些妇女则在她祈祷时设下圈套，要使她出丑。教皇的侄女博福尔·蒂雷纳夫人 (Madame de Beaufort Turenne) 就曾这样做：她跪在凯瑟琳身旁，用一把尖刀刺入她的脚，使她因伤跛行。

这位染匠的女儿如今转而致力于坚定最高教皇返回罗马的决心，并抵制枢机们阻挠此事的阴谋。眼见心愿实现，她便启程返回意大利；母亲在里窝那 (Leghorn) 迎接她，随后她带着教皇的委任前往佛罗伦萨。她力劝该城顺服所受的禁令，取得了一定成效。她对教皇职分的尊崇，要求人无条件地服从。格列高利的继任者乌尔班六世解除了禁令。凯瑟琳随后返回锡耶纳 (Siena)，在那里口授了《对话录》(Dialogue)，这是一部神秘主义论著，教导祈祷、顺服、明辨及其他美德。凯瑟琳声称，在创作此书时，惟有神是她的引导。

乌尔班当选不久便遭遇重重困难，这位教皇于是将目光转向锡耶纳，召这座城市杰出的女儿来到罗马。他们曾在阿维尼翁见过面。1378年秋，她在母亲和其他同伴陪同下抵达圣城。他们单独住在一所房屋里，靠施舍度日。18她劝乌尔班“只用悔改、祈祷、美德和爱这些武器争战”，却未获听从。不过，她的在场仍带来了有益的影响。有一次，暴民狂怒，涌入梵蒂冈 (Vatican)，她出面调解；人们看见她的面容，听见她的话语，骚乱便平息了。

1380年4月29日，她躺在木板上去世。她对站在身旁的同伴说：“亲爱的孩子们，不要因我的死而悲伤，倒要欢喜，因为我将离开这苦难众多之地，到那宁静的海洋，就是永恒的神里面安息，永远与我至甘甜、至慈爱的新郎联合。我应许要更多地与你们同在，对你们更有帮助，因为我要离开黑暗，进入真实而永恒的光明。”她一再低声说：“主啊，我犯了罪；求你怜悯我。”她为乌尔班、为全教会，也为她的同伴祈祷，然后反复说着“我将我的灵魂交在你手里”，便离世了。

锡耶纳的凯瑟琳去世时，还不满三十三岁。乌尔班下令为她举行隆重的葬礼。一年后，她的头颅被装入圣物匣，送回故乡锡耶纳；1461年，这座城市的著名人物教皇庇护二世 (Pius II.) 将她封为圣徒，并给予她极高的赞誉，说“凡亲近过她的人，离去时无不变得更好”。1865年，罗马的密涅瓦圣母堂 (Santa Maria sopra Minerva) 重新开放时，人们抬着她的骨灰游行街头，盛放骨灰的银瓮由四位主教肩抬。教堂内献给她的祭坛前，灯火长明。1866年，庇护九世 (Pius IX.) 将这位染匠的女儿擢升为罗马的主保圣徒和守护者，使她与使徒之首共享这一尊荣。她和彼特拉克 (Petrarch) 一样，曾最热切地主张罗马有权成为教皇驻地，而她的热忱完全出于宗教。

在她的书信和《对话录》中，我们看见了凯瑟琳灵魂的传记。她现存的书信将近四百封。19这些书信不仅因揭示了一位圣洁女子的思想和内在生活而占有崇高地位，而且也是十四世纪书信文学中仅次于彼特拉克书信的主要典范。她写信给各个阶层的人：给母亲，给修道院中的隐修者，给她的告解神父卡普阿的雷蒙德，给沉迷世俗享乐的男男女女，给城市的行政长官、女王和国王，给枢机，也给格列高利十一世和乌尔班六世两位教皇。她提出劝告，详细阐述行动的措施与动机，以恳求和劝诫相告；有时甚至毫不犹豫地以神的审判相威警，如她写给那不勒斯女王的信便是如此。这些书信充满智慧的劝勉。

这些书信表明，凯瑟琳对《新约》颇有一些认识；她引用其中的重要诫命，并从水变为酒的神迹、赶出圣殿中兑换银钱之人的事迹，以及十个童女、婚筵等比喻中，汲取描绘的素材。她最常用的说法之一，就是基督的血。她以十足的神秘主义或修道院式的口吻，嘱咐收信者，甚至教皇和枢机，要在这血中沐浴、沉浸、陶醉，甚至以这血为衣，使自己充满这血，“因为基督赎买我们，不是用金、银、珍珠或其他宝石，而是用他自己宝贵的血”。20

在凯瑟琳看来，宗教生活就是使自己的意志顺服神的旨意，并使灵魂在祈祷操练和爱的实践中向外倾注。她写给一位失去儿女的母亲说：“我要你彻底消灭自己的意志，使它紧紧依附被钉十字架的基督。”在写给隐修女子巴尔托洛梅娅·德拉·塞塔 (Bartolomea della Seta) 的信中，她让救主这样说：“罪与美德都在于意志的认可；凡不是出于自愿而行的，就无所谓罪或美德。”

她写给另一个人说：“我已经见过许多苦修者，他们既无忍耐，也不顺服，因为他们一心要治死自己的身体，却不肯治死自己的意志。”21

她健全的宗教哲学，表现于她一再强调：外在的操练并非使灵性得胜的唯一途径，也并非总是最好的途径。如果身体虚弱或患病，明辨的准则就要求搁下肉体的苦修。她写道：“不但应当停止禁食，还应当吃肉；如果一天一次不够，那就一天四次。”她一再把苦修视为一种工具。在写给奥尔维耶托的达涅拉修女 (Sister Daniella of Orvieto) 的一封非凡书信中，她说：“苦修这一较小的善，可能妨碍内在敬虔这一更大的善。苦修虽能剪除枝叶，你却总会发现，根仍在你里面，随时准备重新萌发；但美德却将它连根拔起。”

凯瑟琳虽有浓厚的修道精神，但在大多数细节上，任何福音派的指导手册，都不能比她写得更切合真理。这位高尚的女子最为卓越之处，莫过于她不肯以自己的境况作为他人的标准，并谴责那些“不加分辨，想用同一把尺度衡量所有身体的人，而这把尺度就是他们衡量自己的尺度”。她写给侄女南娜·贝宁卡萨 (Nanna Benincasa) 时，将心比作一盏上宽下窄的灯。基督的新妇必须有灯、有油、有光。心应当上宽，充满圣洁的思想与祈祷，牢记神的恩惠，尤其是我们赖以蒙赎的宝血之恩。心也应当像灯一样下窄，“不过分爱慕或渴求属世之物，也不贪求超过神愿意赐给我们的东西”。

她不断回到祈祷与爱这两种基督徒的美德。她将基督徒的爱比作海洋，如神自己一样宁静而深邃，因为“神就是爱”。这段话阐明了道成肉身之圣言那不可测度的奥秘：祂受爱的催迫，极其谦卑地舍弃了自己。我们爱，是因为我们蒙了爱。祂的爱出于恩典，我们爱祂则出于本分，因为我们理当如此；为表明我们对祂的爱，我们应当服事并爱一切有理性的受造物，将我们的爱施予好人和坏人，施予各等人，爱伤害我们的人，如同爱服事我们的人一样，因为神不偏待人，祂的仁爱临到义人，也临到罪人。五旬节以前，彼得的爱是甘甜的，却不刚强；五旬节以后，他以儿子的心来爱，忍耐地承受一切患难。我们也是如此，若保持警醒，不住祈祷，等候十日，就必得着圣灵的丰盛。她在写给格列高利的信中，不止一次热烈地颂扬爱，称之为医治一切弊病的良药。她在《对话录》 (Dialogue) 中写道：“灵魂不能没有爱而生存，总要爱某种事物，因为它是藉着爱而受造的。情感推动理智，仿佛在说：‘我要爱，因为养育我的食物就是爱。’”22

这些教导使凯瑟琳的书信成为一部宝贵的宗教虔修手册，尤其适合那些警惕自己、不致被其中潜在的寂静主义色彩所左右的人。这些书信不仅因揭示了一位虔诚女子的心灵而占有崇高地位，也毫不掩饰地以大胆坦率的态度，论及教会所陷入的衰败境况。她要求教皇在任命教牧人员方面实行改革，并纠正其他方面的弊端。至于使托斯卡纳 (Tuscan) 诸城恢复和平这一她极为关切的事业，她力劝教皇采用和平而非战争的手段，像父亲对待叛逆的儿子一样行事——施行宽仁，而不是逞权威的骄傲。这样，连豺狼也会像羔羊一样依偎在他的怀中。23

至于教皇返回罗马，她力言这是他对立他为代理人的神所应尽的本分。面对罗讷河 (Rhone) 畔几乎以强力挟持他的反对势力，她呼吁他“作大丈夫”，“作一个有男子气概的人，不要惧怕，也不要以属肉体的爱来爱自己或任何与自己有亲属关系的人”，“坚定自己的决心，信靠基督，不顾一切预言他回到罗马以后将有灾祸临到的说法”。24在这位热情激越的托斯卡纳女子看来，人们反抗教皇权威，责任在于任用了不配的牧人和恶劣的治理者；这些牧人被自爱吞噬，不但没有从豺狼口中救出基督的羊，反而亲自吞吃羊群。这是因为他们没有效法那位为羊舍命的真牧人。她将教会比作园圃，恳求教皇拔除那些充满贪婪、污秽和骄傲、散发恶臭的植物，将它们抛弃，使那些毒害园圃的恶劣祭司和治理者不再掌权。她向乌尔班六世 (Urban VI.) 发出炽烈的谴责：“你的儿子们靠施行基督宝血之礼所得的财富养肥自己，作了兑换银钱的人却不以为耻。他们贪欲深重，犯买卖圣职之罪，以礼物、谄媚或金钱购买圣俸。”她又写信给驻博洛尼亚 (Bologna) 的教皇使节德斯坦枢机 (Cardinal d’Estaing)，说：“要使圣父看重灵魂的丧失，过于城池的丧失，因为神所要的是灵魂。”

凯瑟琳强调教皇对神所负的责任，又热切斥责不配职分、只为雇价的教牧，这使一些人将她列在新教宗教改革的先驱之中。弗拉修斯·伊利里库斯 (Flacius Illyricus) 在他的《真理见证人名录》——Catalogus testium veritatis——中，将她列入其中。25她以炽热的心情谈到一场将要临到教会的彻底改革。她高声说：“新妇如今容颜尽毁，衣衫褴褛，那时却要焕发美丽，佩戴珠宝，以一切美德的冠冕为冠。所有信主的民族，都将因有优良的牧人而欢喜；不信的世界也要被她的荣光吸引，归向她。”不信的民族将被领入大公教会的羊圈——ovile catholicum——归向灵魂的真牧人和监督。然而，这些情怀虽令人钦佩，凯瑟琳的活动仍在中世纪教会的范围之内。她认为，苦修操练背后的根基在于爱、祈祷和忍耐中的敬虔，却从未超越禁欲与修院式的基督徒生活观，进入因信而自由的开阔天地。她具有萨伏纳罗拉 (Savonarola) 的精神，即为人民的福祉和基督教世界的复兴而热烈牺牲自己的精神，却没有看见过去传统以外的事物。她比这位佛罗伦萨的先知早一百多年；论到热切努力拯救自己的人民、帮助推行公义，她在自己时代无人能及，在她与萨伏纳罗拉之间的一个世纪中，她的同胞也无人能望其项背。她的声音就是先知的声音，在旷野呼喊：“预备主的道。”

回顾1350至1450年这一世纪的妇女，人们很容易将锡耶纳的凯瑟琳 (Catherine of Siena) 与贞德 (Joan of Arc，1411–1431年) 联系起来：前者热烈维护教会，后者热烈维护法国的民族荣誉。这位出身农家的奥尔良少女 (Maid of Orleans)，1431年在鲁昂 (Rouen) 街头被烧死于火刑柱上时，年仅二十岁。与她那位意大利姊妹不同，她容貌秀美，体格健壮；她也自认为领受了天使的传谕和神的引导。她对祖国无私的献身起初为国家带来了胜利，最终却使自己被俘丧命。英国人以异端和巫术的罪名审判并处死她，构成了那个世纪历史中黑暗的一页。她死后二十五年，教皇撤销了这一判决；这位法国女英雄的旗帜绣有百合花，并饰有创造和天使报喜的图像，她于1909年被宣为真福，如今正等候罗马赐予封圣的冠冕。这两位女子在方法、理想及生涯结局上虽大相径庭，她们崇高的热情却在那个时代自私的追逐之上投射出明亮的光辉，使当时许多宫廷的目标显得卑下而鄙陋。

§ 22. 教会政治家彼得·达伊

彼得·达伊 (Peter d’Ailly) 是谋求弥合教皇分裂的谈判中最杰出的人物之一，也是他那个时代最重要的人物之一；1350年生于贡比涅 (Compiegne)，1420年卒于阿维尼翁 (Avignon)。他的雄辩使人想起波舒哀 (Bossuet) 和路易十四宫廷中的其他法国演说家，为他赢得了“法国之鹰”——aquila Francia——的称号。26

1372年，他进入纳瓦尔学院 (College of Navarre) 攻读神学，三年后撰成伦巴德《箴言集》 (Sentences) 的注释，1380年获得神学博士学位。他随即参与弥合教会分裂的工作，并于1381年代表大学，在法国摄政安茹公爵 (duke of Anjou) 面前发表了一篇著名演说，争取宫廷支持通过召开大公会议解决教皇争端的方针。他的呼吁未获赞同，便退居努瓦永 (Noyon)，在那里写了一封假托魔鬼所作的书信，以持续的教会分裂为题材加以讽刺。信中，黑暗之君呼吁他的朋友和封臣——那些高级教士——效法他的榜样，在教会中助长分裂。他以宗主的身份警告他们，召开会议可能导致和平的建立，从而使他们蒙受永远的耻辱。他敦促他们继续把教会变成买卖的场所，谨慎地将茴香、芹菜献上十分之一，将衣裳的繸子做宽，并在其他一切事上照着他给他们留下的榜样去行。27

1384年，达伊被任命为纳瓦尔学院院长，热尔松 (Gerson) 曾在那里受教于他；1389年，他成为大学校监。

本笃十三世 (Benedict XIII.) 被选为克莱门七世 (Clement VII.) 的继任者时，法国国王派达伊前往阿维尼翁，执行一项秘密使命。本笃赢得了他的效忠，并先后于1395年任命他为勒皮 (Puy) 主教，1397年任命他为康布雷 (Cambray) 主教。1405年在热那亚 (Genoa)、1407年在萨沃纳 (Savona)，达伊都随侍本笃左右；但到那时，他似乎已经认定，本笃声称愿意退位并非出于真心，于是返回康布雷。他不在期间，康布雷已决定撤回对阿维尼翁的效忠。达伊被捕并押往巴黎，却得到身为其友人的国王保护。从此，他赞成召开大公会议。

在比萨 (Pisa) 和康斯坦茨 (Constance)，达伊主张大公会议高于教皇，并可将教皇罢黜。1411年，约翰二十三世 (John XXIII.) 任命他为枢机；次年，他出席在罗马举行的会议，并试图推动历法改革，却未能成功。在康斯坦茨，他认为，比萨会议虽然由圣灵召集，并代表普世教会，仍可能犯错，正如其他被认为是大公会议的会议也曾犯错一样。他宣称，比萨、罗马和康斯坦茨这三次会议虽非同一团体，实际上却是一体，正如莱茵河在不同地点的水流仍是同一条河。他认为，康斯坦茨会议无须通过法令来确认比萨会议，因为两者地位相等。28

在针对约翰二十三世的诉讼中，这位枢机站在反对他的一边。1415年6月7、8日，他主持了审理胡斯 (Huss) 信仰问题的委员会；这位改革者被判处死刑时，他也在场。会议结束时，他成为三名教皇候选人之一，他的落选令法国人失望。29马丁五世 (Martin V.) 任命他为教皇使节，驻阿维尼翁，他在那里度过了最后的岁月。

达伊追随奥卡姆 (Ockam)，是一位唯名论者。他除撰写哲学、神学和教会治理方面的著作外，还著有天文学和地理学作品，以及一部广为阅读的亚里士多德《气象学》注释。30他于1410年写成的地理著作《世界图景》——The Picture of the World，imago mundi——是哥伦布 (Columbus) 爱读的书。塞维利亚 (Seville) 的哥伦布图书馆 (biblioteca Colombina) 保存着该书的一部印本，页边有这位航海家亲笔写下的批注。他第三次赴美洲航行时，很可能随身带着这部书，因为他于1498年从海地 (Hayti) 写信时，曾大段引述其中第八章。作者主要依据罗杰·培根 (Roger Bacon)，将印度或契丹 (Cathay) 的海岸描绘为向欧洲方向延伸甚远，以至船只若乘顺风向西航行，几天就能抵达。这一观念当时已在流传，但这位新大陆的发现者，可能最初正是通过阅读达伊的著作，才将它铭记在心。洪堡 (Humboldt) 是第一个指出此书对于地理发现史之价值的人。31

§ 23. 神学家与教会领袖约翰·热尔松

约翰·热尔松 (John Gerson，1363–1429年) 是十五世纪上半叶最有吸引力、也最有影响力的神学领袖。在巴黎大学于欧洲影响最为广泛的时期，他先后担任教授和校监，与这所大学有着极为密切的联系。在解决教皇分裂所引发的种种问题时，他的声音具有举足轻重的分量。

让·沙利耶·热尔松 (Jean Charlier Gerson) 于1363年12月14日生于兰斯 (Rheims) 教区的热尔松村，是十二个孩子中的长子。他的母亲是一位敬虔的妇人，在一封写给他、至今尚存的信中，32倾吐心声，祈求她的儿女彼此和睦，并与上帝合一。约翰的两个弟弟也成了教士。1377年，热尔松前往巴黎，进入纳瓦拉学院 (College of Navarre)。这所学院由纳瓦拉王后约翰娜 (Johanna) 于1304年创办，设有三个学部：文科学部有20名学生，哲学部30名，神学部20名。她也为他们提供生活津贴：文科生每周4巴黎苏，逻辑学学生6苏，神学生8苏。这些津贴一直发放到毕业生分别获得年值30、40和60镑的圣俸为止。规章允许神学生在11月至3月间，每天午餐和晚餐后各生火取暖半小时。乌尔班五世 (Urban V.) 于1366年任命的一个委员会禁止使用长凳这一奢侈之举。节日期间，神学生应当向三个学部的同学作训勉。主持学院的院长，原由大学教师团任命，如今则由国王的告解神父任命。学生穿着特定服装，剃发受冠，彼此用拉丁语交谈，并共同进餐。

热尔松也许是巴黎大学学生名录中最卓著的人物；他忠于母校，对她满怀热忱，称她为“我的母亲”“圣教会之光的母亲”“基督教世界一切智慧与良善事物的养育者”“天上耶路撒冷的原型”“知识的泉源、我们信仰的明灯、法国乃至全世界的荣美与装饰”。33

1382年，十九岁的他升入神学部；一年后，他开始接受新任院长达伊 (D’Ailly) 的指导，在其门下七年。热尔松当时已崭露头角，1383年当选法国“同乡会”的代表，1387年又被选为代表团成员，前往克雷芒七世 (Clement VII.) 面前，陈辩控告蒙松的约翰 (John of Montson) 一案。这位多明我会士因否认马利亚无原罪始胎而被定罪，却拒绝撤回其说，理由是定他的罪，就等于定托马斯·阿奎那 (Thomas Aquinas) 的罪；他因而向教皇上诉。巴黎大学介入此案，达伊在教皇御前会议上两次发言，主张托马斯虽是圣徒，却并非无误。判决对蒙松不利；多明我会士不肯服从这一裁决，遂离开大学，直到1403年才返回。

热尔松主张马利亚既免于原罪，也免于本罪，并将她与基督加以区别：基督是按本性免于罪，马利亚——domina nostra，即我们的圣母——则是因上帝施恩的作为而免于罪。他说，这一教义不能直接从圣经推导出来，34但正如使徒所知的比先知更多，教会的教师也知道一些使徒所不知道的事。

1395年达伊升任主教时，他的学生继承了他的两个职位，即神学教授和大学校监。在大学居于主导地位的弥合教会分裂的讨论中，热尔松占据首要位置，并通过论著、讲道和公开陈情书，在这一迫切的问题上引导教会舆论。他的出发点是：教会的和平是其完成使命的必要条件。1404年，他在塔拉斯孔 (Tarascon)，当着本笃十三世 (Benedict XIII.) 和奥尔良公爵 (duke of Orleans) 的面，发表了一篇著名讲道，阐明这一观点。他宣告，君王和高级教士都有服从法律的义务。教会设立的目的，在于人类的和平与福祉。教会的一切权柄，都是为促进和平而设立的。和平是如此重大的福分，人人都应当甘愿为它放弃尊荣和地位。基督岂不是也曾忍受羞辱吗？与其让教会遵守教规却没有和平，不如暂时没有教皇，因为没有教皇的地方仍可有救恩。35应当召开大公会议；相信会议在处理教会分裂的问题上不会犯错，乃是敬虔的——pium est credere non erraret。正如施瓦布 (Schwab) 所说，从前从未有人这样向教皇讲道。这篇讲道引起了轰动。

热尔松虽未出席比萨会议 (council of Pisa)，却以其重要论著《论教会的合一》——De unitate ecclesiastica——和《论教会罢免教皇》——De auferbilitate papae ab ecclesia——参与了会议的讨论。他阐述的观点是：基督是教会的元首，教会的君主制组织不可改变。必须有一位教皇，而不能有数位，主教的权柄也不与他相等。既然教皇可以使自己与教会分离，教会也可以使自己与教皇分离。自卫的需要可能要求采取这样的行动。教皇职分出于上帝，然而，即使是未经教皇同意而召开的会议，也可以罢免教皇。教会的一切职分和任职者都是为教会的益处而存在，也就是为了借着实行爱而来的和平。假若教皇有权为自己辩护，例如反驳不贞的指控，教会为何没有同样的自卫权利呢？会议直接在基督及其律法的权威之下行事。会议可以凭借钥匙的权柄作出不利于教皇的裁决，因为这权柄不只是赐给一人，也是赐给整体——unitati。亚里士多德 (Aristotle) 宣称，整体在必要时有权罢黜其君王。会议也可以如此；凡拒绝教会会议的，就是拒绝那引导会议行动的上帝。教皇可以因异端和分裂而被罢免，例如不在圣餐前屈膝；即使他个人并无可归咎的罪责，也可能被罢免，正如前文提到的事例：一位教皇被撒拉森人 (Saracens) 俘虏，外间传说他已死去。

在康斯坦茨会议 (Council of Constance) 上，热尔松以法国国王代表的身份发言，一再口头申述这些立场，例如1415年3月23日的演说，以及7月21日为会议第五次全体会议所通过的法令辩护的第二次演说。他重申，可以迫使教皇退位，大公会议高于教皇，而无误性只属于作为整体的教会，或其最高代表——大公会议。36

热尔松积极参与定约翰·胡斯 (John Huss) 的罪，这在他的名声上留下了污点。他没有超越自己的时代，沿用英诺森三世 (Innocent III.) 的措辞，把异端称为毒瘤。37他宣称，在针对胡斯和威克里夫 (Wyclif) 的诉讼中，他的热心不亚于任何人。38他判定从胡斯《论教会》中摘出的十九项谬误是“显然的异端”。他宣告，异端若顽固不化，就必须予以消灭，即使要将信奉者处死也在所不惜。39他否认胡斯的基本立场，即凡圣经中找不到的，就不可接受为神圣真理。热尔松也谴责诉诸良心的做法，明确采取旧有立场，以教会权威和教会法为最终准则。个人无论多么精通圣经，其意见在会议的裁决面前都毫无分量。40

在波希米亚异端所牵涉的停止向平信徒授杯的争论中，热尔松同样采取了极端立场；他用来辩护的论据，在我们看来全然不配称为真正的神学。他在一篇论及此题的文章中宣称，虽然圣经和教父著作中的某些段落赞成同时分授酒与饼，却没有明确命令；至于那些确有明令的地方，则必须理解为适用于司祭，他们有义务领受饼酒两种形态的圣餐，以充分表明基督的受苦与受死。但对平信徒并无此要求，因为他们领圣餐是为领受基督受死的功效，而不是为表明他的死。基督只吩咐使徒领受饼酒两种形态。41平信徒兼领饼酒的习俗从未普遍施行，《使徒行传》2:42、46足以证明这一点。身体与血的圣礼，其本质比外在的饼酒更重要，见《约翰福音》6:54。然而，完整的基督存在于任何一种圣餐物中；若有些教会博士持不同见解，也应遵循教会的教义，而不是他们的意见。自远古以来，教会就只以一种形态分授圣餐。康斯坦茨会议裁定，只用一种圣餐物，便足以使人领受圣礼而得救，这是正确的。只要不触及有关礼仪的本质，教会就可以改变外在的仪式。兼用饼酒这两种圣餐物，从前虽有益处，如今却无益，而且是异端。

除这些论述外，热尔松还提出一些反对向平信徒授杯的实际考虑，例如酒有泼洒的危险，平信徒的长须可能弄脏器皿，为病人保存的酒可能变成醋，从而不再是基督的血——et ita desineret esse sanguis Christi；此外，也不可能在一个器皿中祝圣足供一万至两万人领用的酒，而复活节时却可能有此需要。另一个危险是，这种做法会助长以下观念：司祭与平信徒是平等的，圣礼的主要价值在于领受，而不在于饼酒的祝圣。42这位著名教师认为，向平信徒授杯会造成的“绊倒”，其中就有这些。

多年来，热尔松在一个问题上耗费了大量精力：杀死暴君或背叛的封臣是否正当。他主张否定的一方，却未能在康斯坦茨会议上获胜。这个问题源于人们对待半疯癫的法国国王查理六世 (Charles VI.，1880–1422年) 的方式，以及不同派别企图控制政府的斗争。

1407年11月28日，国王的堂亲、奥尔良公爵路易 (Louis) 奉国王叔父勃艮第公爵约翰 (John, duke of Burgundy) 之命被杀。方济各会士、巴黎大学教授约翰·珀蒂 (John Petit)——Johannes Parvus——在1408年3月8日当着国王所作的一篇演说中，为公爵的行为辩护。热尔松早年似乎曾赞成杀死暴君，此时却发表公开演说反驳珀蒂，并呼吁国王取缔珀蒂的九项命题。43巴黎大学全力支持热尔松的主张。珀蒂于1411年去世，但争论仍在继续。珀蒂的理论是：凡密谋反对其领主的封臣，其灵魂与身体都当受死。他是暴君，按照自然法和上帝的律法，任何人都有权将他除掉。这样的人地位越高，杀死他的功德就越大。他以托马斯·阿奎那、索尔兹伯里的约翰 (John of Salisbury)、亚里士多德、西塞罗 (Cicero) 及其他作者为论据，并引述摩西、心利 (Zambri)、将路西弗 (Lucifer) 逐出天庭的圣米迦勒 (St. Michael)，以及其他事例。奥尔良公爵犯了背叛国王之罪，因此勃艮第公爵杀他是正当的。

巴黎主教在宗教裁判所一个委员会的支持下，奉国王之命，谴责了珀蒂及其观点。1414年2月，热尔松发表公开演说，为这一谴责辩护；两天后，从珀蒂著作中摘出的条文在巴黎圣母院 (Notre Dame) 前被焚毁。国王批准了主教的判决，勃艮第公爵则将此案上诉至罗马。44

此案随即移交大公会议。会议于1415年7月6日第十五次会议上通过一项折衷决议，谴责以下学说：未经司法判决，任何臣民都可以、也应当将暴君处死，即使使用背信手段、违背誓言，也不算犯伪誓之罪。决议并未指名提到珀蒂 (Petit)。正是这种消极而怯懦的举措，使热尔松 (Gerson) 说，胡斯 (Huss) 若有一位辩护者，就不会被判有罪。据传，受命提出报告的委员会以八十票中的六十一票，判定珀蒂的条文可以容许，并宣称彼得本想杀死大祭司的仆人；倘若他在最后的晚餐时知道犹大的心思，杀死犹大也是正当的。据说，勃艮第公爵的金钱在此事上被大量使用。45阿拉斯 (Arras) 主教所领导的一派辩称，动刀的暴君应当受刀的惩罚。热尔松在达伊 (D’Ailly) 的支持下回答说，若是如此，“不可杀人”这条诫命，就只有在附上一条出于默示的解释——“未经司法授权”——之后，才会禁止谋杀这样的行为。这条诫命的意思是：“不可杀害无辜，也不可为报复而杀人。”1417年1月17日，热尔松在大公会议前发表演说，最后一次力争此事，但会议拒绝采取超出第十五次会议法令的行动。

勃艮第公爵于1418年控制巴黎，热尔松发现法国的大门已向他关闭。他在巴伐利亚 (Bavaria) 公爵的保护下，先避居拉滕贝格 (Rattenberg)，后又避居奥地利。1419年9月10日，勃艮第公爵本人在王太子的默许下遭到暗杀，此后热尔松返回法国，却没有回巴黎。他前往其弟约翰 (John) 所在的里昂 (Lyons)，在那里退隐修道院，度过晚年。据说，王太子于1420年赐给他二百里弗尔，以表彰他为王室所作的贡献。

以下还须论述热尔松作为神学家、传道人和爱国者的表现。

在神学本身的领域中，热尔松在神秘主义者中占有一席之地。46他将神秘主义定义为“爱的艺术”，即“借着经验感知上帝”。这种经验，与其说是循思辨之路获得，不如说是借着谦卑和悔罪而达到。默观的生活最值得向往；但效法基督的榜样，默观必须与行动相结合。对上帝的默观在于认识，正如《约翰福音》17:3所教导的：“认识你，并且认识你所差来的耶稣基督，这就是永生。”这样的认识与爱相融。灵魂借着爱与上帝合一。他的神秘主义，一方面以研究圣经为基础，另一方面以研究波纳文图拉 (Bonaventura) 和圣维克多学派 (St. Victors) 为基础。他曾专门撰写一篇论著，赞扬波纳文图拉及其神秘主义著作。他非但没有自觉与德国神秘主义者心意相通，反而撰文反对勒伊斯布鲁克的约翰 (John of Ruysbroeck) 及其门徒舍恩霍芬的约翰 (John of Schönhofen)，指控他们持泛神论。

热尔松虽然强调宗教情感，却远非一个沉迷宗教异象的人；他曾撰写论著，反对梦境和启示所带来的迷惑危险。正如钱币必须按其重量、硬度、颜色、形状和印记加以检验，异象也必须按自称见过异象之人的谦卑、诚实，以及他们是否乐于教导人、也乐于受教，来加以检验。他赞许一位修士的回答：有人提议让这修士观看一个形似基督的人像，他说：“我不愿在地上看见基督。我甘愿等到在天上见他。”

康斯坦茨会议 (Council of Constance) 正在商议确认圣布里吉塔 (St. Brigitta) 封圣一事时，热尔松提出一项原则：如果异象所启示的，是圣经中已有的内容，47那么这些异象就是虚假的，因为上帝不重复自己，见《约伯记》33:14。人们耳朵发痒，渴求启示，是因为不研读圣经。后来，他又告诫人们48提防妇女的启示，因为妇女比男子更易受骗。

热尔松教导说，圣经是教会的准则和指引，直到世界的末了。若有一句话被证明虚假，那么全书便都是虚假的，因为全书的作者是圣灵。然而，经文的字面本身不足以确定其含义，瓦勒度派 (Waldenses)、贝格哈德派 (Beghards) 及其他宗派人士的译本即为明证。49经文需要教会的权威，正如奥古斯丁 (Augustine) 所表明的：“若不是教会的权威催逼我，我就不会相信福音。”

热尔松在其他领域的贡献固然伟大，但按照那位同情他而又博学的传记作者施瓦布 (Schwab) 的看法，他对同时代人所施加的最大影响，来自讲坛。50他既用法语，也用拉丁语讲道；他的讲章大多具有实际目的，所论的是伦理主题，如骄傲、懒惰、愤怒、十诫的各条诫命以及婚姻生活。他认为，普通司祭应当只对十诫、各项大罪和信仰条款作简单的解释。

他生命的最后十年在里昂隐居度过，其间仍继续著述，尤其致力于神秘神学方面的写作。他最后一部著作论述的是《雅歌》。

据传，这位伟大的教师晚年曾在里昂圣保罗堂 (St. Paul’s) 主持一所儿童教理学校，并教孩子们每日为他献上祷告：“上帝，我的创造主，求你怜悯你贫苦的仆人让·热尔松”——Mon Dieu, mon Createur, ayez pitié de vostre pauvre serviteur, Jean Gerson。51他为少年人，也可能为刚入大学头几年的男学生，撰写了题为《引领儿童归向基督》 (Leading Children to Christ) 的短论。52该书开篇解释“让小孩子到我这里来”这句话，继而指出，将青春时最美好的献给上帝，远比把病弱衰老的残渣献给他更为合宜。作者论述应当劝诫儿童避免的罪，尤其是不贞洁；他严斥这样一种原则：恶行只要秘而不宣，便是可宽恕的。这原则用一句话表达就是：si non caste tamen caute——即使不能贞洁，也须谨慎隐瞒。

热尔松在一部由三部分组成的著作中，简要解释十诫，阐述七宗死罪，并就死亡及如何面对死亡写下若干简短默想，由此提供了一种教理问答，尽管并未采用问答形式。作者说，这部著作是为以下诸等人之益处而作：教养不足、却要听取告解的本堂司铎，有子女需要教导的父母，对公共礼拜不感兴趣的人，以及在医院照顾病人的人。53

授予让·热尔松的“最具基督徒精神的博士”——doctor christianissimus——这一称号，意在强调其教导所具有的福音精神。他既有清晰的理智，也有热烈的宗教热忱。他对教会的爱，几乎无人能够超越；他尊崇全体基督徒，认为他们具有基督的心意，并受基督直接引导。他全力倡导以下原则：基督教世界通过大公会议所表达的判断，乃是地上宗教事务的最终权威。

他反对从旧时代承袭下来的一些迷信，强调圣经文本的权威。在这些以及其他见解上，他与当代进步精神相契合。但他没有达到宗教改革者的原则。他对于个人自主和良心权利的原则一无所知。他的思想始终沿着教会的轨道运行。他没有威克里夫 (Wyclif) 那种大胆而独创的思想，也少有那种奋起反对我们所处时代流行谬误的精神。他投票赞成将胡斯烧死，已足以证明新时代的光尚未照进他的心灵。他不像他们，并不是十六世纪运动的先驱。

热尔松所力争的主要原则，即大公会议的至上权，在这位伟大的大学校务长去世后不久便遭挫败，先被教皇们废置，后来又被一次大公会议的裁决所否定。然而，他那些屡次刊行的著作，仍是十四世纪上半叶神学领域最主要的文献丰碑。54他在精神与方法上均有别于经院学者，几乎自成一类，是他那个世纪最杰出的神学家。这一评价，是对十五世纪末德国著名修院院长兼作家特里特米乌斯 (Trithemius) 之评价的引申：“他远为当代神学家之首。”55Theologorum sui temporis longe princeps.

§ 24. 道德论者克拉芒日的尼古拉

这一时期使巴黎大学声名远扬的三大名家中，第三位是尼古拉·普瓦勒维兰·德·克拉芒日 (Nicolas Poillevillain de Clamanges)。他约于1367年生于香槟 (Champagne) 的克拉芒日 (Clamengis)，56约于1437年卒于巴黎。他生性腼腆，在解决当代重大问题方面，不如同时代的达伊和热尔松那样引人注目。他与他们一样，参与了大分裂所引起的争论，并以高度重视研读圣经和尖锐揭露教士腐败而著称。他十二岁进入纳瓦尔学院 (College of Navarre)，师从达伊和热尔松。他在神学方面只取得了学士学位。他可能于1393年被选为大学校长。他与蒙斯特罗利奥的彼得 (Peter of Monsterolio) 同为该大学首屈一指的古典学者；他能够写道，在巴黎，维吉尔 (Virgil)、泰伦斯 (Terence) 和西塞罗 (Cicero) 的著作，无论公开还是私下，都常有人诵读。57

1394年，克拉芒日在拟订一份陈述大学关于弥合大分裂之结论的文件时，发挥了重要作用。58文件的呈文对象是“最热心维护宗教正统的至虔诚国王查理六世 (Charles VI.)”，呈文者则自称“他的女儿——大学”。这份著名文件提出三种弥合大分裂的途径——退位、仲裁和召开大公会议——其特点是坚定而温和，这也是克拉芒日性格中的两项突出要素。文件宣称大分裂是一场瘟疫；对于谁将赋予大公会议权威这一问题，文件回答说：“全体信徒的共融将赋予它权威；基督将赋予它权威，因为他说过：‘无论在哪里，有两三个人奉我的名聚会，那里就有我在他们中间。’”

这位巴黎教授，是目光敏锐的彼得·德·卢纳 (Peter de Luna) 所选中的人才之一。卢纳当选教皇后，克拉芒日支持他，并致函恳请他为整个基督教世界的利益行事，因为他已不再是众舟子中的一员，而是掌握船舵的人。他受召赴阿维尼翁 (Avignon) 教廷担任秘书，却渐渐厌倦宫廷与城中的喧扰和恶行。591406年，他似乎在热那亚 (Genoa) 离开了本笃 (Benedict)，退居朗格勒 (Langres)，在那里担任座堂教士。然而，他并未与教皇决裂；1408年，本笃颁布诏书，以绝罚威胁法国宫廷时，克拉芒日被指为诏书的执笔者。他否认这一指控，但关于他缺乏爱国心的指责已造成强烈影响，于是他退居瓦尔普龙 (Valprofonds) 的加尔都西会修道院 (Carthusian convent)，后来又迁往枫丹迪博斯克 (Fontaine du Bosc)。隐居期间，他撰写书信和论著，并研读圣经；此时他表示懊悔，以往竟因钻研古典学问而忽略了圣经。

他写给达伊 (D’Ailly) 的《论隐居的益处》——De fructu eremi，论述了隐居生活的好处。在另一篇论著《论逆境的益处》——De fructu rerum adversarum——中，他阐明了逆境的益处。另有一篇更重要的论著，谴责滥用主日和增设节庆的现象，因为这些事使工人离开工作，却无助于敬虔的增进。还有一篇《论神学研究》——De studio theologico——是写给巴黎一位神学家的；此人曾询问，自己究竟应当留在原处，还是退而担任牧职。克拉芒日强调，照管灵魂既重要又须审慎，但劝这位询问者留在大学，以研读圣经为主要事务。他将教会的衰败归咎于对圣经的忽视，并断言，除非人心因信得洁净，否则弥撒、游行仪式和节庆都毫无价值。

康斯坦茨大公会议 (Council of Constance) 召开期间，克拉芒日虽未出席，却致函会议，敦促与会者在思想和行动上保持一致。他怀疑大公会议是否总由圣灵引导。他认定教会不会有误，但教会只存在于圣灵所在之处；至于教会究竟在何处，唯有上帝自己知道。1425年，他返回巴黎，讲授修辞学和神学。

克拉芒日的声誉，主要建立在他对教士道德腐败的尖锐批评之上。他在阿维尼翁的居留，使他有充分机会加以观察。他论买卖圣职的高级教士的著作《论买卖圣职的教长》——De praesulibus simoniacis——是对《马太福音》21:13“你们倒使它成为贼窝了”这句话的阐释。另一篇论教会衰败的著作《论教会的倾覆》——De ruina ecclesiae——是当时最著名的作品之一。书中揭露了阿维尼翁的圣职买卖和私生活中的恶行；在那里，一切神圣之物都被出卖，以换取金钱和奢华享受。书中也描写了从教皇到最低阶司铎的教士腐败。作者在第一世纪找到了理想的状况，那时教士的心思全然专注于天上的事。随着财产和权势而来的，是贪婪与野心、骄傲与奢侈。教皇们高举自己的权威，凌驾于帝国之上，又声称自己有权任命一切高级教士，甚至有权授予整个基督教世界的一切圣俸职位，因此他们自己也犯了骄傲的罪。首年圣俸税 (annates) 和预授圣职权 (expectances) 所造成的弊害，实在难以言尽。枢机们效法教皇的贪婪与骄傲，竟有单个枢机占有多达500个圣俸职位。为使自己的“暴政”长存，教皇和教廷与君主们结成同盟，克拉芒日斥之为可憎的淫乱。许多主教从自己的教区领取丰厚收入，却委托他人管理，自己从不巡视。座堂教士和代牧也如法炮制，把时间消磨在懒散和声色享乐之中。托钵修士如同犹太会堂中的法利赛人。一千名教士中，几乎找不到一人履行其圣职所要求的职责。他们沉溺于无知，惯于争吵、酗酒、掷骰赌博和淫乱。司铎花钱购买蓄养情妇的特许。至于修女，克拉芒日说，他不敢谈论她们。女修道院不是上帝的圣所，而是维纳斯 (Venus) 的可耻妓院，是放荡纵欲的青年满足情欲的场所；女子戴上修女头纱，实际上就是使自己沦为娼妓。60教会沉醉于权势、荣耀和享乐的欲望之中。审判必然临到，人应当谦卑地俯伏在上帝面前，因为唯有他能够纠正这些弊恶，终止分裂。这样的描写必须审慎辨析，不可据此断定整个教士阶层都已腐败。然而，能使克拉芒日这样身份的人发出如此哀叹，可见这些病症必已根深蒂固。

作者并未像后来那位德国改教家一样，号召公开争战，而是提出以祈祷、游行和禁食作为补救之道。他的口号是：教会必须先自卑，才能得以重建。61然而，这仍是一番大胆的言论，也是那一批文献的重要组成部分；在改革大公会议时期，这批文献曾有力地塑造了舆论。

阿维尼翁时期，人们已高声抨击教廷内外的道德状况；到了大分裂时期，这些抨击若还有加强的余地，便更为强烈了。康斯坦茨大公会议于1417年10月30日公布的应予纠正的弊端清单，列出了教廷在职务上的种种过失，例如保留任命权 (reservations)、首年圣俸税、赎罪券的售卖，以及不受限制地向教廷上诉的权利。至于贞洁问题，则仍须由个别作者竭力申说。彼特拉克 (Petrarch) 描写第三个巴比伦时，其措辞甚至比克拉芒日更为严厉；后者所写的是将近一个世纪之后的状况，并指控教皇家臣犯有奸淫、强奸和各样淫乱之罪。62ois，《据当时若干道德论者所述的中世纪法国生活》(La vie en France au moyen âge d’après quelques moralistes du temps)，巴黎，1908年，第320、336页等。克拉芒日宣称，许多堂区坚持要求司铎蓄养情妇，以此作为保护自己家庭的预防措施。约翰二十三世 (John XXIII.) 违反一切教会法规，特许英王亨利四世 (Henry IV.) 年仅十岁的私生子领受圣职。63约翰二十三世的情形固然是一个极端的例子，但必须记得，据他的传记作者尼海姆的迪特里希 (Dietrich of Nieheim) 所说，当他作为枢机使节被派往博洛尼亚 (Bologna) 时，有二百名已婚妇女和少女，包括一些修女，成了这位未来教皇猎艳的牺牲品。迪特里希·弗里 (Dietrich Vrie) 在其《康斯坦茨大公会议史》(History of the Council of Constance) 中说：“据我所知，最高教皇的当选，靠的是贪婪和圣职买卖，其他主教也同样因金钱而获祝圣。‘你们白白地得来，也要白白地舍去’这句古训，如今被极其卑劣地歪曲为：‘我所得的既不是白白得来，我也不肯白白地舍去，因为我花了重价买得我的主教职位，必须以不敬虔的手段补偿自己的支出。’……倘若行邪术的西门 (Simon Magus) 如今还活着，他不但可以买到圣灵，也可以买到父上帝，以及我——子上帝。”64然而，大分裂时期圣统阶层和教皇制度的道德状况虽然恶劣，却还不及中世纪最后半个世纪那样败坏。改革大公会议虽绝非唯一的证据，却是最好的证据，表明教会中仍有深厚的道德生命力。会议的召集与举行，本身就是对教士“在头与肢体上”——从教皇直到最卑微的司铎——所存在的腐败和伪善的抗议，同时也是预示未来改善的极有希望的征兆。

§ 25. 学者与教会人士库萨的尼古拉

在热尔松 (Gerson) 和达伊之后一代的神学家中，没有谁的地位比德国人库萨的尼古拉 (Nicolas of Cusa，1401—1464年) 更为显著。他在巴塞尔大公会议 (Basel council) 初期发挥了重要作用，随后转而效力于尤金四世 (Eugenius IV.)，并以推动教会改革的实际努力，以及在神学和其他学术领域的著述而闻名。

他出生于特里尔 (Treves) 附近的库斯 (Cues)，是一个船夫的儿子，因受到苛待而离开父母的家。他得到曼德沙伊德伯爵 (count of Manderscheid) 的庇护，前往代芬特尔 (Deventer)，在共同生活弟兄会 (Brothers of the Common Life) 所办的学校接受教育。他在帕多瓦 (Padua) 研习法律，取得博士学位，后来却放弃法律，转攻神学；若依他的对手海姆堡的格奥尔格 (George of Heimburg) 的说法，这是因为他在承办的第一个案件中败诉。在帕多瓦，他的一位老师是切萨里尼 (Cesarini)；此人后来成为枢机，也是巴塞尔大公会议中的重要人物。

1432年，他作为特里尔候任大主教、曼德沙伊德的乌尔里希 (Ulrich of Manderscheid) 的代表出现在巴塞尔，为乌尔里希申辩，对抗其竞争者、施派尔 (Spires) 主教赫尔姆施塔特的拉巴努斯 (Rabanus of Helmstatt)；教皇已任命拉巴努斯为特里尔教区的大主教。尼古拉与会议紧密合作，在同胡斯派 (Hussites) 交涉的事务中发挥了主要作用，并与多数派一道主张大公会议的权威高于教皇。他于1433年献给会议的《论大公教会的和谐》——De concordantia catholica——体现了他对这一问题的看法，承袭了朗根施泰因 (Langenstein)、尼海姆 (Nieheim) 和热尔松先前的论述。大公会议既受圣灵感动，所说的话便真实无误。教会是信徒的团体——unitas fidelium——并由大公会议代表。教皇的权威来自教会的同意；会议有权因异端或其他原因罢黜教皇，且未经会议自身同意，不得延期或休会。彼得从基督所得的权柄，并不比其他使徒更多。凡对彼得所说的，也同样是对其他使徒说的。所有主教的权威和尊严一律平等，不论他们所行使的是主教、大主教、牧首还是教皇的管辖权，正如所有长老都是平等的一样。65

尽管持有这些观点，当与希腊人会晤的地点问题提出时，尼古拉却站在少数派一边，赞成选择意大利的一座城市；他也是少数派所委任的代表团成员，赴博洛尼亚拜见尤金四世，并前往君士坦丁堡 (Constantinople)。这是1437年的事，自此以后，他便甘心效力于尤金及其后两任教皇。后来成为庇护二世 (Pius II.) 的埃涅阿斯·西尔维乌斯 (Aeneas Sylvius)，称他为尤金派的赫拉克勒斯 (Hercules)。埃涅阿斯还称赞他在各门学问上造诣卓著，且以敬虔生活著称。66

尤金任用这位新支持者为使节，与德国教会和诸侯商订和解条件；1447年《维也纳协约》 (concordat of Vienna) 签订时，他终于看到这一目标实现。他获擢升进入枢机团，作为酬报，并于1452年被任命为蒂罗尔 (Tyrol) 的布里克森 (Brixen) 主教。他力图在此推行教会改革，又以教皇使节的身份，为同一目的巡行德国大部分地区。

这位主教试图主张其教区在中世纪所享有的一切封建权利，因而与奥地利公爵西格蒙德 (Siegmund) 发生激烈冲突。即使两位教皇先后宣布禁令，也未能使公爵屈服。公爵向主教宣战，将他俘获，迫使他承诺放弃那些旧有权利；他的前任们已多年未再主张这些权利。主教获释后，便以誓言出于强迫为由，视之为无效，并得到庇护二世的支持。1460年，他前往罗马，数年后卒于翁布里亚 (Umbria) 的托迪 (Todi)。

库萨的尼古拉通晓希腊文和希伯来文，或许可以称为大阿尔伯特 (Albertus Magnus) 以来直至他那个时代德国学识最为广博的学者。他对天文学、数学和植物学都感兴趣，并像先前的达伊一样，在巴塞尔大公会议上敦促修正历法。他倾注心力最多的著作，是讨论神学问题的《论有学识的无知》——De docta ignorantia。在这部书中，他抨击经院学派的方法，也显露出神秘主义对其思想的影响；他在代芬特尔时，便生活在这种氛围之中。他强调人心智的局限，以及理性无法穷尽对上帝的认识。他将信心界定为灵魂因上帝恩典而获赐的一种状态；信心能够认识理智无法企及的真理。67他的观点影响了法贝尔·斯塔普伦西斯 (Faber Stapulensis)；后者编订了这位库萨人的著作，本身也是在因信称义教义上先于路德的一位法国先驱。

他晚年的工作与庇护二世推动的讨伐土耳其人的十字军运动有关；这使他研究《古兰经》，并撰写《筛析古兰经》——De cribatione Alcoran。他在书中宣称，虚假的宗教都以真正的宗教为基础。

这位枢机在历史上的主要地位，在于他是教会事务的调解者，也是教士与修院弊端的改革者。他用本国语言讲道。在班贝格 (Bamberg)，他促成了禁止成立新的兄弟会的规定；在马格德堡 (Magdeburg)，他促成了对以金钱出售赎罪券的谴责。在萨尔茨堡 (Salzburg) 等地，他推行修院改革，又与家族中的其他成员共同在库斯 (Cues) 创办医院，设有33张病床。他资助20名男孩在代芬特尔 (Deventer) 受教育，表明了他对学术的关切。他论述地球绕轴自转，比哥白尼 (Copernicus) 早了将近一个世纪。他提出理由，认为《君士坦丁赠礼》是伪造的，也质疑《伊西多尔教令集》其他部分的真实性。

另一方面，这位枢机又是一位彻底忠于教会的人，是教会顺服的儿子。他作为尼古拉五世 (Nicolas V.) 的代理人，巡行德国，宣告禧年的赎罪。据说，经他之手，为修缮圣彼得大教堂而售出的赎罪券，价值达200,000古尔登 (gulden)。

扬森 (Janssen) 是一位为中世纪末期天主教会辩护的能干而博学的学者；他将这位高尚而多才多艺的人与古腾堡 (Gutenberg) 并提，称前者为教士纪律与教会纪律的捍卫者，后者为印刷机的发明者。若说尼古拉并未领受在思想和宗教领域充当新时代先驱的使命，不像古腾堡受命以其发明促进文化与文明，这并不是贬低尼古拉的志向和工作。68他并不具备道德与教义上的坚定信念和先见之明；正是这些禀赋，使那位维滕贝格 (Wittenberg) 的修士成为一场彻底的宗教改革的代言人和先驱，而基督教世界的很大一部分都为这场改革的持久裨益作证。

§ 26. 民众传道者

在截至1450年的一个半世纪中，既有地方性的传道者群体，也有独立的讲坛雄辩家，对会众产生了深刻影响。以埃克哈特 (Eckart) 和约翰·陶勒 (John Tauler) 为首的德国神秘主义者，在斯特拉斯堡 (Strassburg)、科隆 (Cologne) 和莱茵河沿岸讲道。达伊 (D’Ailly) 和热尔松 (Gerson) 面向经过挑选的听众讲道，为法国讲坛增添了光彩。威克里夫 (Wyclif) 在牛津，约翰·胡斯 (John Huss) 在波希米亚，都因讲道而引起极大关注，也因此招致教会的谴责。胡斯只是波希米亚许多杰出传道者中的一位。威克里夫则派出一类特殊的人，即他的“贫穷传道者” ("pore preachers")，力求推动传道工作。

民众传道者构成另一群体，不过这一时期并没有谁能与雷根斯堡的贝特霍尔德 (Berthold of Regensburg) 相比；这位露天传道者是他那个世纪的怀特腓 (Whitefield)，卒于1272年。当时最著名的民众传道者，是贝尔纳迪诺 (Bernardino) 和卡皮斯特拉诺的约翰 (John of Capistrano)，两人都是意大利人，同属方济各会的严守派 (Observants)；另有西班牙多明我会士文森特·费雷尔 (Vincent Ferrer)。佛罗伦萨的萨伏纳罗拉 (Savonarola) 和斯特拉斯堡的盖勒 (Geiler)，这两位光耀讲坛的人物，则属于稍后的时代。

锡耶纳的贝尔纳迪诺 (Bernardino of Siena)，1380—1444年，被庇护二世誉为第二位保罗。他给意大利听众留下了显著印象，也深得教皇马丁五世 (Martin V.) 的喜爱。他的声音起初微弱而含混，据说因他向马利亚求助，蒙她施恩，才变得洪亮而清晰。他是严守派的首任总会副会长；他加入时，这一派在意大利只有少数几个会团，但在他的管理下大有发展。1424年，他在罗马；据罗马日记作者因费苏拉 (Infessura) 记载，69他对民众的影响极大，以致他们把自己的游戏用具和装饰物品带到卡皮托尔 (Capitol)，堆在一起焚烧。无论他到哪里讲道，前面总有人举着一面旗帜，上面写着基督名号的字母组合 IHS，十二道光芒以这些字母为中心向外放射。他敦促神父们将这个字母组合置于教堂和公共建筑的墙上；锡耶纳市政厅上至今仍可见到这样的字母组合。70奥古斯丁会士、多明我会士以及波焦 (Poggio)，都因这一做法而攻击他。1427年，他到罗马答辩。马丁五世判他无罪，准许他在各地讲道，并命他就在教皇之城举行八十天的布道。1419年，他曾到伦巴第 (Lombard) 各城，民众为他劝人悔改的讲论所倾倒，常在公共广场焚烧自己的小饰物和游戏用具。他的遗体安葬在阿奎拉 (Aquila)，1450年由尼古拉五世封为圣徒。

卡皮斯特拉诺的约翰，1386—1456年，原是一名律师，年轻时便受托管理佩鲁贾 (Perugia)；1416年加入严守派，成为贝尔纳迪诺的学生。他在意大利北部担任宗教裁判官，使异端者和犹太人归信，又将他们处以火刑，因而成名。他反对异端的狂热之凶猛，无人能出其右。他于1426年首次获任宗教裁判官，23年后，即1449年，第四次获任此职。71

作为修会的领袖，他于1427年为贝尔纳迪诺辩护，并于1443年被任命为总会副会长。他把传道工作扩展到维也纳 (Vienna)，又深入德国，从纽伦堡 (Nürnberg) 到德累斯顿 (Dresden)、莱比锡 (Leipzig)、马格德堡和布雷斯劳 (Breslau)，所到之处都引起巨大轰动。他使用拉丁语或意大利语，必须由人翻译给听众。据报，听众曾多达三万人。72他随身携带贝尔纳迪诺的遗物，据说借着这些遗物和他本人的施行，出现了许多神迹。他的随从当场记录这些奇事。73人们焚烧尖头鞋、纸牌、骰子及其他供娱乐或炫耀之用的物品，足见他讲道的感召力。据报，另有数以千计的异端者接受了他的劝导。庇护二世召他讲道反对胡斯派 (Hussites)，后来又反对土耳其人。他亲历贝尔格莱德 (Belgrade) 之围，为成功守城、击败穆罕默德二世 (Mohammed II.) 作出了贡献。他于1690年被封为圣徒。

西班牙最伟大的传道者文森特·费雷尔，卒于1419年，其一生正值教皇分裂时期，并与这场分裂所引发的争论密切相关。他的名字也与说方言的恩赐及鞭笞派 (Flagellants) 联系在一起。这位献身传道的人生于瓦伦西亚 (Valencia)，加入多明我会，先后在巴塞罗那 (Barcelona) 和莱里达 (Lerida) 的大学求学。他凭借《论当今教会的分裂》——De moderno ecclesiae schismate——这篇论著，获得神学博士学位。回到瓦伦西亚后，他以讲道闻名，并被任命为阿拉贡 (Aragon) 王后约兰特 (Iolanthe) 的告解神父，以及她丈夫约翰一世 (John I.) 的顾问。1395年，本笃十三世 (Benedict XIII.) 召他到阿维尼翁 (Avignon)，担任首席赦罪司铎及教皇宫廷神学长。两年后，他以教皇使节的头衔返回瓦伦西亚。起初他十分热烈地维护对阿维尼翁教皇的效忠；但后来，他确信本笃声称要弥合分裂并非出于诚意，便撤回了对他的支持，转而支持康斯坦茨大公会议 (Council of Constance)。

费雷尔的使徒式劳作始于1399年。他巡行西班牙、意大利北部和法国，每日讲道两三次，以悔改和审判临近为重大主题。他享有在犹太人和穆罕默德教徒中最成功的传教士之誉。据说有两万五千名犹太人和八千名穆罕默德教徒接受了他的劝导。他只会说西班牙语，但据报，他的讲道虽无人翻译，在法国和意大利却都能为人听懂。他的同时代人认为他既有说方言的恩赐，也有行神迹的恩赐。神父和歌唱者随他巡行，所唱的圣诗中，有些是文森特自己所作。据称，他的听众多达70,000人，这个数目难以置信；又据说他讲过两万次道。他也曾向山谷中的瓦勒度派 (Waldenses) 及残存的卡塔里派 (Cathari) 讲道，据说使许多人归信。他本人也未能免于异端之嫌；艾默里希 (Eymerich) 控告他说，加略人犹大之所以上吊，是因为众人不容他活下去，而他已获得上帝的赦免。741455年，加里斯都三世 (Calixtus III.) 将他封为圣徒。传说，这位博尔贾 (Borgia) 家族的成员在瓦伦西亚还是年轻神父时，费雷尔就注意到他，并预言他有朝一日将登上凡人所能企及的最高职位。75

费雷尔巡行时，也有成群的鞭笞派信徒随行。他自己也参与鞭笞；据说，他每日用来鞭打自己的鞭子由六条皮带组成，至今仍保存在加泰罗尼亚 (Catalonia) 的加尔都西会修院“天梯”——scala coeli——之中。热尔松和达伊都曾攻击费雷尔，指责他接受了鞭笞派的迷妄。在康斯坦茨大公会议召开期间，热尔松写信给这位西班牙传道者，主张《旧约》和《新约》都禁止伤害身体，并引《申命记》14:1为证：“不可为自己用刀划身。”他邀请费雷尔前来康斯坦茨，但对方没有接受邀请。76

§ 20. Ockam and the Decay of Scholasticism.

Scholasticism had its last great representative in Duns Scotus, d. 1308. After him the scholastic method gradually passed into disrepute. New problems were thrust upon the mind of Western Europe, and new interests were engaging its attention. The theologian of the school and the convent gave way to the practical theological disputant setting forth his views in tracts and on the floor of the councils. Free discussion broke up the hegemony of dogmatic assertion. The authority of the Fathers and of the papacy lost its exclusive hold, and thinkers sought another basis of authority in the general judgment of contemporary Christendom, in the Scriptures alone or in reason. The new interest in letters and the natural world drew attention away from labored theological systems which were more adapted to display the ingenuity of the theologian than to be of practical value to society. The use of the spoken languages of Europe in literature was fitted to force thought into the mould of current exigencies. The discussions of Roger Bacon show that at the beginning of the fourteenth century men’s minds, sated with abstruse metaphysical solutions of theological questions, great and trivial, were turning to a world more real and capable of proof.

The chief survivors of the dialectical Schoolmen were Durandus and William Ockam. Gabriel Biel of Tübingen, who died just before the close of the fifteenth century, is usually called the last of the Schoolmen.(1) Such men as D’Ailly, Gerson and Wyclif, sometimes included under the head of mediaeval scholastics, evidently belong to another class.

A characteristic feature of the scholasticism of Durandus and Ockam is the sharper distinction they made between reason and revelation. Following Duns Scotus, they declared that doctrines peculiar to revealed theology are not susceptible of proof by pure reason. The body of dogmatic truth, as accepted by the Church, they did not question.

A second characteristic is the absence of originality. They elaborated what they received. The Schoolmen of former periods had exhausted the list of theological questions and discussed them from every standpoint.

The third characteristic is the revival and ascendency of nominalism, the principle Roscellinus advocated more than two hundred years before. The Nominalists were also called Terminists, because they represent words as terms which do not necessarily have ideas and realities to correspond to them. A universal is simply a symbol or term for a number of things or for that which is common to a number of things.(2) Universality is nothing more than a mode of mental conception. The University of Paris resisted the spread of nominalism, and in 1839 the four nations forbade the promulgation of Ockam’s doctrine or listening to its being expounded in private or public.(3) In 1473, Louis XI. issued a mandate forbidding the doctors at Paris teaching it, and prohibiting the use of the writings of Ockam, Marsiglius and other writers. In 1481 the law was rescinded.

Durandus, known as doctor resolutissimus, the resolute doctor, d. 1334, was born at Pourçain, in the diocese of Clermont, entered the Dominican order, was appointed by Fohn XXII. bishop of Limoux, 1317, and was later elevated to the sees of Puy and Meaux. He attacked some of the rules of the Franciscans and John XXII.’s theory of the beatific vision, and in 1333 was declared by a commission guilty of eleven errors. His theological views are found in his commentary on the Lombard, begun when he was a young man and finished in his old age. He showed independence by assailing some of the views of Thomas Aquinas. He went beyond his predecessors in exalting the Scriptures above tradition and pronouncing their statements more authoritative than the dicta of Aristotle and other philosophers.(4) All real existence is in the individual. The universal is not an entity which can be divided as a chunk of wood is cut into pieces. The universal, the unity by which objects are grouped together as a class, is deduced from individuals by an act of the mind. That which is common to a class has, apart from the individuals of the class, no real existence.

On the doctrine of the eucharist Durandus seems not to have been fully satisfied with the view held by the Church, and suggested that the words "this is my body," may mean "contained under"—contentum sub hoc. This marks an approach to Luther’s view of consubstantiation. This theologian was held in such high esteem by Gerson that he recommended him, together with Thomas Aquinas, Bradwardine and Henry of Ghent, to the students of the college of Navarre.(5)

The most profound scholastic thinker of the fourteenth century was the Englishman, William Ockam, d. 1349, called doctor invincibilis, the invincible doctor, or, with reference to his advocacy of nominalism, venerabilis inceptor, the venerable inaugurator. His writings, which were more voluminous than lucid, were much published at the close of the fifteenth century, but have not been put into print for several hundred years. There is no complete edition of them. Ockam’s views combined elements which were strictly mediaeval, and elements which were adopted by the Reformers and modern philosophy. His identification with the cause of the Spiritual Franciscans involved him in controversy with two popes, John XXII. and Benedict XII. His denial of papal infallibility has the appearance not 80 much of a doctrine proceeding from theological conviction as the chance weapon laid hold of in time of conflict to protect the cause of the Spirituals.

Of the earlier period of Ockam’s life, little is known. He was born in Surrey, studied at Oxford, where he probably was a student of Duns Scotus, entered the Franciscan order, and was probably master in Paris, 1315–1320. For his advocacy of the doctrine of Christ’s absolute poverty he was, by order of John XXII., tried and found guilty and thrown into confinement.(6) With the aid of Lewis the Bavarian, he and his companions, Michael of Cesena and Bonagratia, escaped in 1328 to Pisa. from that time on, the emperor and the Schoolman, as already stated, defended one another. Ockam accompanied the emperor to Munich and was excommunicated. At Cesena’s death the Franciscan seal passed into his hands, but whatever authority he possessed he resigned the next year into the hands of the acknowledged Franciscan general, Farinerius. Clement VI. offered him absolution on condition of his abjuring his errors. Whether he accepted the offer or not is unknown. He died at Munich and is buried there. The distinguished Englishman owes his reputation to his revival of nominalism, his political theories and his definition of the final seat of religious authority.

His theory of nominalism was explicit, and offered no toleration to the realism of the great Schoolmen from Anselm on. Individual things alone have factual existence. The universals are mere terms or symbols, fictions of the mind—fictiones, signa mentalia, nomina, signa verbalia. They are like images in a mirror. A universal stands for an intellectual act—actus intelligenda — and nothing more. Did ideas exist in God’s mind as distinct entities, then the visible world would have been created out of them and not out of nothing.(7)

Following Duns Scotus, Ockam taught determinism. God’s absolute will makes things what they are. Christ might have become wood or stone if God had so chosen. In spite of Aristotle, a body might have different kinds of motion at the same time. In the department of morals, what is now bad might have been good, if God had so willed it.

In the department of civil government, Ockam, advocating the position taken by the electors at Rense, 1338, declared the emperor did not need the confirmation of the pope. The imperial office is derived immediately from God.(8) The Church is a priestly institution, administers the sacraments and shows men the way of salvation, but has no civil jurisdiction,(9) potestas coactiva.

The final seat of authority, this thinker found in the Scriptures. Truths such as the Trinity and the incarnation cannot be deduced by argument. The being of God cannot be proven from the so-called idea of God. A plurality of gods may be proven by the reason as well as the existence of the one God. Popes and councils may err. The Bible alone is inerrant. A Christian cannot be held to believe anything not in the Scriptures.(10)

The Church is the community of the faithful—communitas, or congregatio fidelium.(11) The Roman Church is not identical with it, and this body of Christians may exist independently of the Roman Church. If the pope had plenary power, the law of the Gospel would be more galling than the law of Moses. All would then be the pope’s slaves.(12) The papacy is not a necessary institution.

In the doctrine of the eucharist, Ockam represents the traditional view as less probable than the view that Christ’s body is at the side of the bread. This theory of impanation, which Rupert of Deutz taught, approached Luther’s theory of consubstantiation. However, Ockam accepted the Church’s view, because it was the less intelligible and because the power of God is unlimited. John of Paris, d. 1308, had compared the presence of Christ in the elements to the co-existence of two natures in the incarnation and was deposed from his chair at the University of Paris, 1304. Gabriel Biel took a similar view.(13)

Ockam’s views on the authority of the civil power, papal errancy, the infallibility of the Scriptures and the eucharist are often compared with the views of Luther.(14) The German reformer spoke of the English Schoolman as "without doubt the leader and most ingenious of the Schoolmen"—scholasticorum doctorum sine dubio princeps et ingeniosissimus. He called him his "dear teacher," and declared himself to be of Ockam’s party—sum Occamicae factionis.(15) The two men were, however, utterly unlike. Ockam was a theorist, not a reformer, and in spite of his bold sayings, remained a child of the mediaeval age. He started no party or school in theological matters. Luther exalted personal faith in the living Christ. He discovered new principles in the Scriptures, and made them the active forces of individual and national belief and practice. We might think of Luther as an Ockam if he had lived in the fourteenth century. We cannot think of Ockam as a reformer in the sixteenth century. He would scarcely have renounced monkery. Ockam’s merit consists in this that, in common with Marsiglius and other leaders of thought, he imbibed the new spirit of free discussion, and was bold enough to assail the traditional dogmas of his time. In this way he contributed to the unsettlement of the pernicious mediaeval theory of the seat of authority.

§ 21. Catherine of Siena, the Saint.

Next to Francis d’Assisi, the most celebrated of the Italian saints is Catherine of Siena—Caterina da Siena—1347–1380. With Elizabeth of Thuringia, who lived more than a century before her, she is the most eminent of the holy women of the Middle Ages whom the Church has canonized. Her fame depends upon her single-hearted piety and her efforts to advance the interests of the Church and her nation. She left no order to encourage the reverence for her name. She was the most public of all the women of the Middle Ages in Italy, and yet she passed unscathed and without a taint through streets and in courts. Now, as the daughter of an humble citizen of Siena, she ministers to the poor and the sick: now, as the prophetess of heaven, she appeals to the conscience of popes and of commonwealths. Her native Sienese have sanctified her with the fragrant name la beata poplana, the blessed daughter of the people. Although much in her career, as it has been handed down by her confessor and biographer, may seem to be legendary, and although the hysterical element may not be altogether wanting from her piety, she yet deserves and will have the admiration of all men who are moved by the sight of a noble enthusiasm. It would require a fanatical severity to read the account of her unwearied efforts and the letters, into which she equally poured the fire of her soul, without feeling that the Sienese saint was a very remarkable woman, the Florence Nightingale of her time or more, "one of the most wonderful women that have ever lived," as her most recent English biographer has pronounced her. Or, shall we join Gregorovius, the thorough student of mediaeval Rome, in saying, "Catherine’s figure flits like that of an angel: through the darkness of her time, over which her gracious genius sheds a soft radiance. Her life is more worthy and assuredly a more human subject for history than the lives of the popes of her age."(16)

Catherine Benincasa was the twenty-third of a family of twenty-five children. Her twin sister, Giovanna, died in infancy. Her father was a dyer in prosperous circumstances. Her mother, Monna Lapa, survived the daughter. Catherine treated her with filial respect, wrote her letters, several of which are extant, and had her with her on journeys and in Rome during her last days there. Catherine had no school training, and her knowledge of reading and writing she acquired after she was grown up.

As a child she was susceptible to religious impressions, and frequented the Dominican church near her father’s home. The miracles of her earlier childhood were reported by her confessor and biographer, Raymund of Capua. At twelve her parents arranged for her a marriage, but to avoid it Catherine cut off her beautiful hair. She joined the tertiary order of the Dominicans, the women adherents being called the mantellate from their black mantles. Raymund declares "that nature had not given her a face over-fair," and her personal appearance was marred by the marks of the smallpox. And yet she had a winning expression, a fund of good spirits, and sang and laughed heartily. Once devoted to a religious life, she practised great austerities, flagellating herself three times a day,—once for herself, once for the living and once for the dead. She wore a hair undergarment and an iron chain. During one Lenten season she lived on the bread taken in communion. These asceticisms were performed in a chamber in her father’s house. She was never an inmate of a convent. Such extreme asceticisms as she practised upon herself she disparaged at a later period.

At an early age Catherine became the subject of visions and revelations. On one of these occasions and after hours of dire temptation, when she was tempted to live like other girls, the Saviour appeared to her stretched on the cross and said: "My own daughter, Catherine, seest thou how much I have suffered for thee? Let it not be hard for thee to suffer for me." Thrilled with the address, she asked: "Where wert thou, Lord, when I was tempted with such impurity?" and He replied, "In thy heart." In 1367, according to her own statement, the Saviour betrothed himself to her, putting a ring on her finger. The ring was ever afterwards visible to herself though unseen by others. Five years before her death, she received the stigmata directly from Christ. Their impression gave sharp pain, and Catherine insisted that, though they likewise were invisible to others, they were real to her.

In obedience to a revelation, Catherine renounced the retired life she had been living, and at the age of twenty began to appear in public and perform the active offices of charity. This was in 1367. She visited the poor and sick, and soon became known as the ministering angel of the whole city. During the plague of 1374, she was indefatigable by day and night, healed those of whom the physicians despaired, and she even raised the dead. The lepers outside the city walls she did not neglect.

One of the remarkable incidents in her career which she vouches for in one of her letters to Raymund was her treatment of Niccolo Tuldo, a young nobleman condemned to die for having uttered words disrespectful of the city government. The young man was in despair, but under Catherine’s influence he not only regained composure, but became joyful in the prospect of death. Catherine was with him at the block and held his head. She writes, "I have just received a head into my hands which was to me of such sweetness as no heart can think, or tongue describe." Before the execution she accompanied the unfortunate man to the mass, where he received the communion for the first time. His last words were "naught but Jesus and Catherine. And, so saying," wrote his benefactress, "I received his head in my hands." She then saw him received of Christ, and as she further wrote, "When he was at rest, my soul rested in peace, in so great fragrance of blood that I could not bear to remove the blood which had fallen on me from him."

The fame of such a woman could not be held within the walls of her native city. Neighboring cities and even the pope in Avignon heard of her deeds of charity and her revelations. The guide of minds seeking the consolations of religion, the minister to the sick and dying, Catherine now entered into the wider sphere of the political life of Italy and the welfare of the Church. Her concern was divided between efforts to support the papacy and to secure the amelioration of the clergy and establish peace. With the zeal of a prophet, she urged upon Gregory XI. to return to Rome. She sought to prevent the rising of the Tuscan cities against the Avignon popes and to remove the interdict which was launched against Florence, and she supported Urban VI. against the anti-pope, Clement VII. With equal fervor she urged Gregory to institute a reformation of the clergy, to allow no weight to considerations of simony and flattery in choosing cardinals and pastors and "to drive out of the sheep-fold those wolves, those demons incarnate, who think only of good cheer, splendid feasts and superb liveries." She also was zealous in striving to stir up the flames of a new crusade. To Sir John Hawkwood, the freelance and terror of the peninsula, she wrote, calling upon him that, as he took such pleasure in fighting, he should thenceforth no longer direct his arms against Christians, but against the infidels. She communicated to the Queen of Cyprus on the subject. Again and again she urged it upon Gregory XI., and chiefly on the grounds that he "might minister the blood of the Lamb to the wretched infidels," and that converted, they might aid in driving pride and other vices out of the Christian world.(17)

Commissioned by Gregory, she journeyed to Pisa to influence the city in his favor. She was received with honors by the archbishop and the head of the republic, and won over two professors who visited her with the purpose of showing her she was self-deceived or worse. She told them that it was not important for her to know how God had created the world, but that "it was essential to know that the Son of God had taken our human nature and lived and died for our salvation." One of the professors, removing his crimson velvet cap, knelt before her and asked for forgiveness. Catherine’s cures of the sick won the confidence of the people. On this visit she was accompanied by her mother and a group of like-minded women.

A large chapter in Catherine’s life is interwoven with the history of Florence. The spirit of revolt against the Avignon regime was rising in upper Italy and, when the papal legate in Bologna, in a year of dearth, forbade the transportation of provisions to Florence, it broke out into war. At the invitation of the Florentines, Catherine visited the city, 1375 and, a year later, was sent as a delegate to Avignon to negotiate terms of peace. She was received with honor by the pope, but not without hesitancy. The other members of the delegation, when they arrived, refused to recognize her powers and approve her methods. The cardinals treated her coolly or with contempt, and women laid snares at her devotions to bring ridicule upon her. Such an attempt was made by the pope’s niece, Madame de Beaufort Turenne, who knelt at her side and ran a sharp knife into her foot so that she limped from the wound.

The dyer’s daughter now turned her attention to the task of confirming the supreme pontiff in his purpose to return to Rome and counteract the machinations of the cardinals against its execution. Seeing her desire realized, she started back for Italy and, met by her mother at Leghorn, went on to Florence, carrying a commission from the pope. Her effort to induce the city to bow to the sentence of interdict, which had been laid upon it, was in a measure successful. Her reverence for the papal office demanded passive obedience. Gregory’s successor, Urban VI., lifted the ban. Catherine then returned to Siena where she dictated the Dialogue, a mystical treatise inculcating prayer, obedience, discretion and other virtues. Catherine declared that God alone had been her guide in its composition.

In the difficulties, which arose soon after Urban’s election, that pontiff looked to Siena and called its distinguished daughter to Rome. They had met in Avignon. Accompanied by her mother and other companions, she reached the holy city in the Autumn of 1378. They occupied a house by themselves and lived upon alms.(18) Her summons to Urban "to battle only with the weapons of repentance, prayer, virtue and love" were not heeded. Her presence, however, had a beneficent influence, and on one occasion, when the mob raged and poured into the Vatican, she appeared as a peacemaker, and the sight of her face and her words quieted the tumult.

She died lying on boards, April 29, 1380. To her companions standing at her side, she said: "Dear children, let not my death sadden you, rather rejoice to think that I am leaving a place of many sufferings to go to rest in the quiet sea, the eternal God, and to be united forever with my most sweet and loving Bridegroom. And I promise to be with you more and to be more useful to you, since I leave darkness to pass into the true and everlasting light." Again and again she whispered, "I have sinned, O Lord; be merciful to me." She prayed for Urban, for the whole Church and for her companions, and then she departed, repeating the words, "Into thy hands I commit my spirit."

At the time of her death Catherine of Siena was not yet thirty-three years old. A magnificent funeral was ordered by Urban. A year after, her head, enclosed in a reliquary, was sent to her native Siena, and in 1461 she was canonized by the city’s famous son, pope Pius II., who uttered the high praise "that none ever approached her without going away better." In 1865 when Santa Maria sopra Minerva in Rome was reopened, her ashes were carried through the streets, the silver urn containing them being borne by four bishops. Lamps are kept ever burning at the altar dedicated to her in the church. In 1866 Pius IX. elevated the dyer’s daughter to the dignity of patron saint and protectress of Rome, a dignity she shares with the prince of the Apostles. With Petrarch she had been the most ardent advocate of its claims as the papal residence, and her zeal was exclusively religious.

In her correspondence and Dialogue we have the biography of Catherine’s soul. Nearly four hundred of her letters are extant.(19) Not only have they a place of eminence as the revelations of a saintly woman’s thoughts and inner life, but are, next to the letters written by Petrarch, the chief specimens of epistolary literature of the fourteenth century. She wrote to persons of all classes, to her mother, the recluse in the cloister, her confessor, Raymund of Capua, to men and women addicted to the pleasures of the world, to the magistrates of cities, queens and kings, to cardinals, and to the popes, Gregory XI. and Urban VI., gave words of counsel, set forth at length measures and motives of action, used the terms of entreaty and admonition, and did not hesitate to employ threats of divine judgment, as in writing to the Queen of Naples. They abound in wise counsels.

The correspondence shows that Catherine had some acquaintance with the New Testament from which she quotes the greater precepts and draws descriptions from the miracle of the water changed into wine and the expulsion of the moneychangers from the temple and such parables as the ten virgins and the marriage-feast. One of her most frequent expressions is the blood of Christ, and in truly mystical or conventual manner she bids her correspondents, even the pope and the cardinals, bathe and drown and inebriate themselves in it, yea, to clothe and fill themselves with it, "for Christ did not buy us with gold or silver or pearls or other precious stones, but with his own precious blood."(20)

To Catherine the religious life was a subjection of the will to the will of God and the outgoing of the soul in exercises of prayer and the practice of love. "I want you to wholly destroy your own will that it may cling to Christ crucified." So she wrote to a mother bereft of her children. Writing to the recluse, Bartolomea della Seta, she represented the Saviour as saying, "Sin and virtue consist in the consent of the will, there is no sin or virtue unless voluntarily wrought."

To another she wrote, "I have already seen many penitents who have been neither patient nor obedient because they have studied to kill their bodies but not their wills."(21)

Her sound religious philosophy showed itself in insisting again and again that outward discipline is not the only or always the best way to secure the victory of the spirit. If the body is weak or fallen into illness, the rule of discretion sets aside the exercises of bodily discipline. She wrote, "Not only should fasting be abandoned but flesh be eaten and, if once a day is not enough, then four times a day." Again and again she treats of penance as an instrument. "The little good of penance may hinder the greater good of inward piety. Penance cuts off," so she wrote in a remarkable letter to Sister Daniella of Orvieto, "yet thou wilt always find the root in thee, ready to sprout again, but virtue pulls up by the root."

Monastic as Catherine was, yet no evangelical guide-book could write more truly than she did in most particulars. And at no point does this noble woman rise higher than when she declined to make her own states the standard for others, and condemned those "who, indiscreetly, want to measure all bodies by one and the same measure, the measure by which they measure themselves." Writing to her niece, Nanna Benincasa, she compared the heart to a lamp, wide above and narrow below. A bride of Christ must have lamp and oil and light. The heart should be wide above, filled with holy thoughts and prayer, bearing in memory the blessings of God, especially the blessing of the blood by which we are bought. And like a lamp, it should be narrow below, "not loving or desiring earthly things in excess nor hungering for more than God wills to give us."

To the Christian virtues of prayer and love she continually returns. Christian love is compared to the sea, peaceful and profound as God Himself, for "God is love." This passage throws light upon the unsearchable mystery of the Incarnate Word who, constrained by love, gave Himself up in all humility. We love because we are loved. He loves of grace, and we love Him of duty because we are bound to do so; and to show our love to Him we ought to serve and love every rational creature and extend our love to good and bad, to all kinds of people, as much to one who does us ill as to one who serves us, for God is no respecter of persons, and His charity extends to just men and sinners. Peter’s love before Pentecost was sweet but not strong. After Pentecost he loved as a son, bearing all tribulations with patience. So we, too, if we remain in vigil and continual prayer and tarry ten days, shall receive the plenitude of the Spirit. More than once in her letters to Gregory, she bursts out into a eulogy of love as the remedy for all evils. "The soul cannot live without love," she wrote in the Dialogue, "but must always love something, for it was created through love. Affection moves the understanding, as it were, saying, ’I want to love, for the food wherewith I am fed is love.’ "(22)

Such directions as these render Catherine’s letters a valuable manual of religious devotion, especially to those who are on their guard against being carried away by the underlying quietistic tone. Not only do they have a high place as the revelation of a pious woman’s soul. They deal with unconcealed boldness and candor with the low conditions into which the Church was fallen. Popes are called upon to institute reforms in the appointment of clergymen and to correct abuses in other directions. As for the pacification of the Tuscan cities, a cause which lay so close to Catherine’s heart, she urged the pontiff to use the measures of peace and not of war, to deal as a father would deal with a rebellious son,—to put into practice clemency, not the pride of authority. Then the very wolves would nestle in his bosom like lambs.(23)

As for the pope’s return to Rome, she urged it as a duty he owed to God who had made him His vicar. In view of the opposition on the Rhone, almost holding him as by physical force, she called upon him to "play the man," "to be a manly man, free from fear and fleshly love towards himself or towards any creature related to him by kin," "to be stable in his resolution and to believe and trust in Christ in spite of all predictions of the evil to follow his return to Rome."(24) To this impassioned Tuscan woman, the appointment of unworthy shepherds and bad rectors was responsible for the rebellion against papal authority, shepherds who, consumed by self-love, far from dragging Christ’s sheep away from the wolves, devoured the very sheep themselves. It was because they did not follow the true Shepherd who has given His life for the sheep. Likening the Church to a garden, she invoked the pope to uproot the malodorous plants full of avarice, impurity and pride, to throw them away that the bad priests and rulers who poison the garden might no longer have rule. To Urban VI. she addressed burning words of condemnation. "Your sons nourish themselves on the wealth they receive by ministering the blood of Christ, and are not ashamed of being money-changers. In their great avarice they commit simonies, buying benefices with gifts or flatteries or gold." And to the papal legate of Bologna, Cardinal d’Estaing, she wrote, "make the holy father consider the loss of souls more than the loss of cities, for God demands souls."

The stress Catherine laid upon the pope’s responsibility to God and her passionate reproof of an unworthy and hireling ministry, inclined some to give her a place among the heralds of the Protestant Reformation. Flacius Illyricus included her in the list of his witnesses for the truth—Catalogus testium veritatis.(25) With burning warmth she spoke of a thorough-going reformation which was to come upon the Church. "The bride, now all deformed and clothed in rags," she exclaimed, "will then gleam with beauty and jewels, and be crowned with the diadem of all virtues. All believing nations will rejoice to have excellent shepherds, and the unbelieving world, attracted by her glory, will be converted unto her." Infidel peoples would be brought into the Catholic fold,—ovile catholicum,—and be converted unto the true pastor and bishop of souls. But Catherine, admirable as these sentiments were, moved within the limits of the mediaeval Church. She placed piety back of penitential exercises in love and prayer and patience, but she never passed beyond the ascetic and conventual conception of the Christian life into the open air of liberty through faith. She had the spirit of Savonarola, the spirit of fiery self-sacrifice for the well-being of her people and the regeneration of Christendom, but she did not see beyond the tradition of the past. Living a hundred years and more before the Florentine prophet, she was excelled by none in her own age and approached by none of her own nation in the century between her and Savonarola, in passionate effort to save her people and help spread righteousness. Hers was the voice of the prophet, crying in the wilderness, "Prepare ye the way of the Lord."

In recalling the women of the century from 1350 to 1450, the mind easily associates together Catherine of Siena and Joan of Arc, 1411–1431, one the passionate advocate of the Church, the other of the national honor of France. The Maid of Orleans, born of peasant parentage, was only twenty when she was burnt at the stake on the streets of Rouen, 1431. Differing from her Italian sister by comeliness of form and robustness of constitution, she also, as she thought, was the subject of angelic communications and divine guidance. Her unselfish devotion to her country at first brought it victory, but, at last, to her capture and death. Her trial by the English on the charges of heresy and sorcery and her execution are a dark sheet among the pages of her century’s history. Twenty-five years after her death, the pope revoked the sentence, and the French heroine, whose standard was embroidered with lilies and adorned with pictures of the creation and the annunciation, was beatified, 1909, and now awaits the crown of canonization from Rome. The exalted passion of these two women, widely as they differ in methods and ideals and in the close of their careers, diffuses a bright light over the selfish pursuits of their time, and makes the aims of many of its courts look low and grovelling.

§ 22. Peter d’Ailly, Ecclesiastical Statesman.

One of the most prominent figures in the negotiations for the healing of the papal schism, as well as one of the foremost personages of his age, was Peter d’Ailly, born in Compiegne 1350, died in Avignon 1420. His eloquence, which reminds us of Bossuet and other French orators of the court of Louis XIV., won for him the title of the Eagle of France—aquila Francia.(26)

In 1372 he entered the College of Navarre as a theological student, prepared a commentary on the Sentences of the Lombard three years later, and in 1380 reached the theological doctorate. He at once became involved in the measures for the healing of the schism, and in 1381 delivered a celebrated address in the name of the university before the French regent, the duke of Anjou, to win the court for the policy of settling the papal controversy through a general council. His appeal not meeting with favor, he retired to Noyon, from which he wrote a letter purporting to come from the devil, a satire based on the continuance of the schism, in which the prince of darkness called upon his friends and vassals, the prelates, to follow his example in promoting division in the Church. He warned them as their overlord that the holding of a council might result in establishing peace and so bring eternal shame upon them. He urged them to continue to make the Church a house of merchandise and to be careful to tithe anise and cummin, to make broad the borders of their garments and in every other way to do as he had given them an example.(27)

In 1384 D’Ailly was made head of the College of Navarre, where he had Gerson for a pupil, and in 1389 chancellor of the university.

When Benedict XIII. was chosen successor to Clement VII., he was sent by the French king on a confidential mission to Avignon. Benedict won his allegiance and appointed him successively bishop of Puy, 1395, and bishop of Cambray, 1397. D’Ailly was with Benedict at Genoa, 1405, and Savona, 1407, but by that time seems to have come to the conclusion that Benedict was not sincere in his profession of readiness to resign, and returned to Cambray. In his absence Cambray had decided for the subtraction of its allegiance from Avignon. D’Ailly was seized and taken to Paris, but protected by the king, who was his friend. Thenceforth he favored the assemblage of a general council.

At Pisa and at Constance, D’Ailly took the position that a general council is superior to the pope and may depose him. Made a cardinal by John XXIII., 1411, he attended the council held at Rome the following year and in vain tried to have a reform of the calendar put through. At Constance, he took the position that the Pisan council? though it was called by the Spirit and represented the Church universal, might have erred, as did other councils reputed to be general councils. He declared that the three synods of Pisa, Rome and Constance, though not one body, yet were virtually one, even as the stream of the Rhine at different points is one and the same. It was not necessary, so he held, for the Council of Constance to pass acts confirming the Council of Pisa, for the two were on a par.(28)

In the proceedings against John XXIII., the cardinal took sides against him. He was the head of the commission which tried Huss in matters of faith, June 7, 8, 1415, and was present when the sentence of death was passed upon that Reformer. At the close of the council he appears as one of the three candidates for the office of pope, and his defeat was a disappointment to the French.(29) He was appointed legate by Martin V., with his residence at Avignon, and spent his last days there.

D’Ailly followed Ockam as a nominalist. To his writings in the departments of philosophy, theology and Church government he added works on astronomy and geography and a much-read commentary on Aristotle’s meteorology.(30) His work on geography, The Picture of the World,—imago mundi,—written 1410, was a favorite book with Columbus. A printed copy of it containing marginal notes in the navigator’s own hand is preserved in the biblioteca Colombina, Seville. This copy he probably had with him on his third journey to America, for, in writing from Hayti, 1498, he quoted at length the eighth chapter. Leaning chiefly upon Roger Bacon, the author represented the coast of India or Cathay as stretching far in the direction of Europe, so that, in a favorable wind, a ship sailing westwards would reach it in a few days. This idea was in the air, but it is possible that it was first impressed upon the mind of the discoverer of the New World by the reading of D’Ailly’s work. Humboldt was the first to show its value for the history of discovery.(31)

§ 23. John Gerson, Theologian and Church Leader.

In John Gerson, 1363–1429, we have the most attractive and the most influential theological leader of the first half of the fifteenth century. He was intimately identified with the University of Paris as professor and as its chancellor in the period of its most extensive influence in Europe. His voice carried great weight in the settlement of the questions rising out of the papal schism.

Jean Charlier Gerson, born Dec. 14, 1363, in the village of Gerson, in the diocese of Rheims, was the oldest of twelve children. In a letter to him still extant,(32) his mother, a godly woman, pours out her heart in the prayer that her children may live in unity with each other and with God. Two of John’s brothers became ecclesiastics. In 1377 Gerson went to Paris, entering the College of Navarre. This college was founded by Johanna, queen of Navarre, 1304, who provided for 3 departments, the arts with 20 students, philosophy with 30 and theology with 20 students. Provision was made also for their support, 4 Paris sous weekly for the artists, 6 for the logicians and 8 for the theologians. These allowances were to continue until the graduates held benefices of the value respectively of 30, 40 and 60 pounds. The regulations allowed the theological students a fire, daily, from November to March after dinner and supper for one half-hour. The luxury of benches was forbidden by a commission appointed by Urban V. in 1366. On the festival days, the theologians were expected to deliver a collation to their fellow-students of the three classes. The rector at the head of the college, originally appointed by the faculty of the university, was now appointed by the king’s confessor. The students wore a special dress and the tonsure, spoke Latin amongst themselves and ate in common.

Gerson, perhaps the most distinguished name the University of Paris has on its list of students, was a faithful and enthusiastic son of his alma mater, calling her "his mother," "the mother of the light of the holy Church," "the nurse of all that is wise and good in Christendom," "a prototype of the heavenly Jerusalem," "the fountain of knowledge, the lamp of our faith, the beauty and ornament of France, yea, of the whole world."(33)

In 1382, at the age of nineteen, he passed into the theological department, and a year later came under the guidance of D’Ailly, the newly appointed rector, remaining under him for seven years. Gerson was already a marked man, and was chosen in 1383 procurator of the French "nation," and in 1387 one of the delegation to appear before Clement VII. and argue the case against John of Montson. This Dominican, who had been condemned for denying the immaculate conception of Mary, refused to recant on the plea that in being condemned Thomas Aquinas was condemned, and he appealed to the pope. The University of Paris took up the case, and D’Ailly in two addresses before the papal consistory took the ground that Thomas, though a saint, was not infallible. The case went against De Montson; and the Dominicans, who refused to bow to the decision, left the university and did not return till 1403.

Gerson advocated Mary’s exemption from original as well as actual sin, and made a distinction between her and Christ, Christ being exempt by nature, and Mary—domina nostra — by an act of divine grace. This doctrine, he said, cannot be immediately derived from the Scriptures,(34) but, as the Apostles knew more than the prophets, so the Church teachers know some things the Apostles did not know.

At D’Ailly’s promotion to the episcopate, 1395, his pupil fell heir to both his offices, the offices of professor of theology and chancellor of the university. In the discussion over the healing of the schism in which the university took the leading part, he occupied a place of first prominence, and by tracts, sermons and public memorials directed the opinion of the Church in this pressing matter. The premise from which he started out was that the peace of the Church is an essential condition to the fulfilment of its mission. This view he set forth in a famous sermon, preached in 1404 at Tarascon before Benedict XIII. and the duke of Orleans. Princes and prelates, he declared, both owe obedience to law. The end for which the Church was constituted is the peace and well-being of men. All Church authority is established to subserve the interests of peace. Peace is so great a boon that all should be ready to renounce dignities and position for it. Did not Christ suffer shame? Better for a while to be without a pope than that the Church should observe the canons and not have peace, for there can be salvation where there is no pope.(35) A general council should be convened, and it was pious to believe that in the treatment of the schism it would not err—pium est credere non erraret. As Schwab has said, no one had ever preached in the same way to a pope before. The sermon caused a sensation.

Gerson, though not present at the council of Pisa, contributed to its discussions by his important tracts on the Unity of the Church—De unitate ecclesiastica — and the Removal of a Pope—De auferbilitate papae ab ecclesia. The views set forth were that Christ is the head of the Church, and its monarchical constitution is unchangeable. There must be one pope, not several, and the bishops are not equal in authority with him. As the pope may separate himself from the Church, so the Church may separate itself from the pope. Such action might be required by considerations of self-defence. The papal office is of God, and yet the pope may be deposed even by a council called without his consent. All Church offices and officials exist for the good of the Church, that is, for the sake of peace which comes through the exercise of love. If a pope has a right to defend himself against, say, the charge of unchastity, why should not the Church have a like right to defend itself? A council acts under the immediate authority of Christ and His laws. The council may pronounce against a pope by virtue of the power of the keys which is given not only to one but to the body—unitati. Aristotle declared that the body has the right, if necessary, to depose its prince. So may the council, and whoso rejects a council of the Church rejects God who directs its action. A pope may be deposed for heresy and schism, as, for example, if he did not bend the knee before the sacrament, and he might be deposed when no personal guilt was chargeable against him, as in the case already referred to, when he was a captive of the Saracens and was reported dead.

At the Council of Constance, where Gerson spoke as the delegate of the French king, he advocated these positions again and again with his voice, as in his address March 23, 1415, and in a second address July 21, when he defended the decree which the synod had passed at its fifth session. He reasserted that the pope may be forced to abdicate, that general councils are above the popes and that infallibility only belongs to the Church as a body or its highest representative, a general council.(36)

A blot rests upon Gerson’s name for the active part he took in the condemnation of John Huss. He was not above his age, and using the language of Innocent III. called heresy a cancer.(37) He declares that he was as zealous in the proceedings against Huss and Wyclif as any one could be.(38) He pronounced the nineteen errors drawn from Huss’ work on the Church "notoriously heretical." Heresy, he declared, if it is obstinate, must be destroyed even by the death of its professors.(39) He denied Huss’ fundamental position that nothing is to be accepted as divine truth which is not found in Scripture. Gerson also condemned the appeal to conscience, explicitly assuming the old position of Church authority and canon law as final. The opinions of an individual, however learned he may be in the Scriptures, have no weight before the judgment of a council.(40)

In the controversy over the withdrawal of the cup from the laity, involved in the Bohemian heresy, Gerson also took an extreme position, defending it by arguments which seem to us altogether unworthy of a genuine theology. In a tract on the subject he declared that, though some passages of Scripture and of the Fathers favored the distribution of both wine and bread, they do not contain a definite command, and in the cases where an explicit command is given it must be understood as applying to the priests who are obliged to commune under both kinds so as to fully represent Christ’s sufferings and death. But this is not required of the laity who commune for the sake of the effect of Christ’s death and not to set it forth. Christ commanded only the Apostles to partake of both kinds.(41) The custom of lay communion was never universal, as is proved by Acts 2:42, 46. The essence of the sacrament of the body and blood is more important than the elements, John 6:54. But the whole Christ is in either element, and, if some of the doctors take a different view, the Church’s doctrine is to be followed, and not they. From time immemorial the Church has given the communion only in one form. The Council of Constance was right in deciding that only a single element is necessary to a saving participation in the sacrament. The Church may make changes in the outward observance when the change does not touch the essence of the right in question. The use of the two elements, once profitable, is now unprofitable and heretical.

To these statements Gerson added practical considerations against the distribution of the cup to laymen, such as the danger of spilling the wine, of soiling the vessels from the long beards of laymen, of having the wine turn to vinegar, if it be preserved for the sick and so it cease to be the blood of Christ—et ita desineret esse sanguis Christi — and from the impossibility of consecrating in one vessel enough for 10,000 to 20,000 communicants, as at Easter time may be necessary. Another danger was the encouragement such a practice would give to the notions that priest and layman are equal, and that the chief value of the sacrament lies in the participation and not in the consecration of the elements.(42) Such are some of the "scandals" which this renowned teacher ascribed to the distribution of the cup to the laity.

A subject on which Gerson devoted a great deal of energy for many years was whether the murder of tyrants or of a traitorous vassal is justifiable or not. He advocated the negative side of the case, which he failed to win before the Council of Constance. The question grew out of the treatment of the half-insane French king, Charles VI. (1880–1422), and the attempt of different factions to get control of the government.

On Nov. 28, 1407, the king’s cousin, Louis, duke of Orleans, was murdered at the command of the king’s uncle, John, duke of Burgundy. The duke’s act was defended by the Franciscan and Paris professor, John Petit,—Johannes Parvus,—in an address delivered before the king March 8, 1408. Gerson, who at an earlier time seems to have advocated the murder of tyrants, answered Petit in a public address, and called upon the king to suppress Petit’s nine propositions.(43) The University of Paris made Gerson’s cause its own. Petit died in 1411, but the controversy went on. Petit’s theory was this, that every vassal plotting against his lord is deserving of death in soul and body. He is a tyrant, and according to the laws of nature and God any one has the right to put him out of the way. The higher such a person is in rank, the more meritorious is the deed. He based his argument upon Thomas Aquinas, John of Salisbury, Aristotle, Cicero and other writers, and referred to Moses, Zambri and St. Michael who cast Lucifer out of heaven, and other examples. The duke of Orleans was guilty of treason against the king, and the duke of Burgundy was justified in killing him.

The bishop of Paris, supported by a commission of the Inquisition and at the king’s direction, condemned Petit and his views. In February, 1414, Gerson made a public address defending the condemnation, and two days later articles taken from Petit’s work were burnt in front of Notre Dame. The king ratified the bishop’s judgment, and the duke of Burgundy appealed the case to Rome.(44)

The case was now transferred to the council, which at its fifteenth session, July 6, 1415, passed a compromise measure condemning the doctrine that a tyrant, in the absence of a judicial sentence, may and ought to be put to death by any subject whatever, even by the use of treacherous means, and in the face of an oath without committing perjury. Petit was not mentioned by name. It was this negative and timid action, which led Gerson to say that if Huss had had a defender, he would not have been found guilty. It was rumored that the commission which was appointed to bring in a report, by sixty-one out of eighty votes, decided for the permissibility of Petit’s articles declaring that Peter meant to kill the high priest’s servant, and that, if he had known Judas’ thoughts at the Last Supper, he would have been justified in killing him. The duke of Burgundy’s gold is said to have been freely used.(45) The party led by the bishop of Arras argued that the tyrant who takes the sword is to be punished with the sword. Gerson, who was supported by D’Ailly replied that then the command "thou shalt not kill" would only forbid such an act as murder, if there was coupled with it an inspired gloss, "without judicial authority." The command means, "thou shalt not kill the innocent, or kill out of revenge." Gerson pressed the matter for the last time in an address delivered before the council, Jan. 17, 1417, but the council refused to go beyond the decree of the fifteenth session.

The duke of Burgundy got possession of Paris in 1418, and Gerson found the doors of France closed to him. Under the protection of the duke of Bavaria he found refuge at Rattenberg and later in Austria. On the assassination of the duke of Burgundy himself, with the connivance of the dauphin, Sept. 10, 1419, he returned to France, but not to Paris. He went to Lyons, where his brother John was, and spent his last years there in monastic seclusion. The dauphin is said to have granted him 200 livres in 1420 in recognition of his services to the crown.

It remains to speak of Gerson as a theologian, a preacher and a patriot.

In the department of theology proper Gerson has a place among the mystics.(46) Mysticism he defines as "the art of love," the "perception of God through experience." Such experience is reached by humility and penance more than through the path of speculation. The contemplative life is most desirable, but, following Christ’s example, contemplation must be combined with action. The contemplation of God consists of knowledge as taught in John 17:3, "This is life eternal, to know Thee and Jesus Christ whom Thou hast sent." Such knowledge is mingled with love. The soul is one with God through love. His mysticism was based, on the one hand, on the study of the Scriptures and, on the other, on the study of Bonaventura and the St. Victors. He wrote a special treatise in praise of Bonaventura and his mystical writings. Far from having any conscious affinity with the German mystics, he wrote against John of Ruysbroeck and Ruysbroeck’s pupil, John of Schönhofen, charging them with pantheism.

While Gerson emphasized the religious feelings, he was far from being a religious visionary and wrote treatises against the dangers of delusion from dreams and revelations. As coins must be tested by their weight, hardness, color, shape and stamp, so visions are to be tested by the humility and honesty of those who profess to have them and their readiness to teach and be taught. He commended the monk who, when some one offered to show him a figure like Christ, replied, "I do not want to see Christ on the earth. I am contented to wait till I see him in heaven."

When the negotiations were going on at the Council of Constance for the confirmation of the canonization of St. Brigitta, Gerson laid down the principle that, if visions reveal what is already in the Scriptures,(47) then they are false, for God does not repeat Himself, Job 33:14. People have itching ears for revelations because they do not study the Bible. Later he warned(48) against the revelations of women, as women are more open to deception than men.

The Scriptures, Gerson taught, are the Church’s rule and guide to the end of the world. If a single statement should be proved false, then the whole volume is false, for the Holy Spirit is author of the whole. The letter of the text, however, is not sufficient to determine their meaning, as is proved from the translations of the Waldenses, Beghards and other secretaries.(49) The text needs the authority of the Church, as Augustine indicated when he said, "I would not believe the Gospel if the authority of the Church did not compel me."

Great as Gerson’s services were in other departments, it was, to follow his sympathetic and scholarly biographer, Schwab, from the pulpit that he exercised most influence on his generation.(50) He preached in French as well as Latin, and his sermons had, for the most part, a practical intent, being occupied with ethical themes such as pride, idleness, anger, the commandments of the Decalogue, the marital state. He held that the ordinary priest should confine himself to a simple explanation of the Decalogue, the greater sins and the articles of faith.

During the last ten years of his life, spent in seclusion at Lyons, he continued his literary activity, writing more particularly in the vein of mystical theology. His last work was on the Canticles.

The tradition runs that the great teacher in his last years conducted a catechetical school for children in St. Paul’s at Lyons, and that he taught them to offer for himself the daily prayer, "God, my creator, have pity upon Thy poor servant, Jean Gerson"—Mon Dieu, mon Createur, ayez pitié de vostre pauvre serviteur, Jean Gerson.(51) It was for young boys and perhaps for boys spending their first years in the university that he wrote his tractate entitled Leading Children to Christ.(52) It opens with an exposition of the words, "Suffer little children to come unto me" and proceeds to show how much more seemly it is to offer to God our best in youth than the dregs of sickly old age. The author takes up the sins children should be admonished to avoid, especially unchastity, and holds up to reprobation the principle that vice is venial if it is kept secret, the principle expressed in the words si non caste tamen caute.

In a threefold work, giving a brief exposition of the Ten Commandments, a statement of the seven mortal sins and some short meditations on death and the way to meet it, Gerson gives a sort of catechism, although it is not thrown into the form of questions and answers. As the author states, it was intended for the benefit of poorly instructed curates who heard confessions, for parents who had children to instruct, for persons not interested in the public services of worship and for those who had the care of the sick in hospitals.(53)

The title, most Christian doctor—doctor christianissimus — given to John Gerson is intended to emphasize the evangelical temper of his teaching. To a clear intellect, he added warm religious fervor. With a love for the Church, which it would be hard to find excelled, he magnified the body of Christian people as possessing the mind and immediate guidance of Christ and threw himself into the advocacy of the principle that the judgment of Christendom, as expressed in a general council, is the final authority of religious matters on the earth.

He opposed some of the superstitions inherited from another time. He emphasized the authority of the sacred text. In these views as in others he was in sympathy with the progressive spirit of his age. But he stopped short of the principles of the Reformers. He knew nothing of the principles of individual sovereignty and the rights of conscience. His thinking moved along churchly lines. He had none of the bold original thought of Wyclif and little of that spirit which sets itself against the current errors of the times in which we live. His vote for Huss’ burning proves sufficiently that the light of the new age had not dawned upon his mind. He was not, like them, a forerunner of the movement of the sixteenth century.

The chief principle for which Gerson contended, the supremacy of general councils, met with defeat soon after the great chancellor’s death, and was set aside by popes and later by the judgment of a general council. His writings, however, which were frequently published remain the chief literary monuments in the department of theology of the first half of the fourteenth century.(54) Separated from the Schoolmen in spirit and method, he stands almost in a class by himself, the most eminent theologian of his century. This judgment is an extension of the judgment of the eminent German abbot and writer, Trithemius, at the close of the fifteenth century: "He was by far the chief divine of his age"(55) Theologorum sui temporis longe princeps.

§ 24. Nicolas of Clamanges, the Moralist.

The third of the great luminaries who gave fame to the University of Paris in this period, Nicolas Poillevillain de Clamanges, was born at Clamengis,(56) Champagne, about 1367 and died in Paris about 1437. Shy by nature, he took a less prominent part in the settlement of the great questions of the age than his contemporaries, D’Ailly and Gerson. Like them, he was identified with the discussions called forth by the schism, and is distinguished for the high value he put on the study of the Scriptures and his sharp exposition of the corruption of the clergy. He entered the College of Navarre at twelve, and had D’Ailly and Gerson for his teachers. In theology he did not go beyond the baccalaureate. It is probable he was chosen rector of the university 1393. With Peter of Monsterolio, he was the chief classical scholar of the university and was able to write that in Paris, Virgil, Terence and Cicero were often read in public and in private.(57)

In 1394, Clamanges took a prominent part in preparing the paper, setting forth the conclusions of the university in regard to the healing of the schism.(58) It was addressed to the "most Christian king, Charles VI., most zealous of religious orthodoxy by his daughter, the university." This, the famous document suggesting the three ways of healing the schism,—by abdication, arbitration and by a general council,—is characterized by firmness and moderation, two of the elements prominent in Clamanges’ character. It pronounced the schism pestiferous, and in answer to the question who would give the council its authority, it answered: "The communion of all the faithful will give it; Christ will give it, who said: ’Where two or three are gathered together in my name there am I in the midst of them.’ "

The Paris professor was one of the men whom the keen-eyed Peter de Luna picked out, and when he was elected pope, Clamanges supported him and wrote appealing to him, as the one who no longer occupied the position of one boatman among others, but stood at the rudder of the ship, to act in the interest of all Christendom. He was called as secretary to the Avignon court, but became weary of the commotion and the vices of the palace and the town.(59) In 1406, he seems to have withdrawn from Benedict at Genoa and retired to Langres, where he held a canon’s stall. He did not, however, break with the pope, and, when Benedict in 1408 issued the bull threatening the French court with excommunication, Clamanges was charged with being its author. He denied the charge, but the accusation of want of patriotism had made a strong impression, and he withdrew to the Carthusian convent, Valprofonds, and later to Fontaine du Bosc. His seclusion he employed in writing letters and treatises and in the study of the Bible which he now expressed regret for having neglected in former years for classical studies.

To D’Ailly he wrote on the advantages of a secluded life.—De fructu eremi. In another tract—De fructu rerum adversarum — he presented the advantages of adversity. One of more importance complained of the abuse of the Lord’s Day and of the multiplication of festivals as taking the workman from his work while the interests of piety were not advanced. In still another tract—De studio theologico — addressed to a theologian at Paris who had inquired whether it was better for him to continue where he was or to retire to a pastorate, he emphasized the importance and delicacy of caring for souls, but advised the inquirer to remain at the university and to concern himself chiefly with the study of the Scriptures. He ascribed the Church’s decline to their neglect, and pronounced the mass, processionals and festivals as of no account unless the heart be purified by faith.

During the sessions of the Council of Constance, which he did not attend, Clamanges sent a letter to that body urging unity of thought and action. He expressed doubt whether general councils were always led by the Holy Spirit. The Church, which he defined as infallible, is only there where the Holy Spirit is, and where the Church is, can be only known to God Himself. In 1425 he returned to Paris and lectured on rhetoric and theology.

Clamanges’ reputation rests chiefly upon his sharp criticism of the corrupt morals of the clergy. His residence in Avignon gave him a good opportunity for observation. His tract on the prelates who were practising simony—De praesulibus simoniacis — is a commentary on the words, "But ye have made it a den of thieves," Matt. 21:13. A second tract on the downfall of the Church—De ruina ecclesiae — is one of the most noted writings of the age. Here are set forth the simony and private vices practised at Avignon where all things holy were prostituted for gold and luxury. Here is described the corruption of the clergy from the pope down to the lowest class of priests. The author found ideal conditions in the first century, when the minds of the clergy were wholly set on heavenly things. With possessions and power came avarice and ambition, pride and luxury. The popes themselves were guilty of pride in exalting their authority above that of the empire and by asserting for themselves the right of appointing all prelates, yea of filling all the benefices of Christendom. The evils arising from annates and expectances surpass the power of statement. The cardinals followed the popes in their greed and pride, single cardinals having as many as 500 livings. In order to perpetuate their "tyranny," pope and curia had entered into league with princes, which Clamanges pronounces an abominable fornication. Many of the bishops drew large incomes from their sees which they administered through others, never visiting them themselves. Canons and vicars followed the same course and divided their time between idleness and sensual pleasure. The mendicant monks corresponded to the Pharisees of the synagogue. Scarcely one cleric out of a thousand did what his profession demanded. They were steeped in ignorance and given to brawling, drinking, playing with dice and fornication. Priests bought the privilege of keeping concubines. As for the nuns, Clamanges said, he dared not speak of them. Nunneries were not the sanctuaries of God, but shameful brothels of Venus, resorts of unchaste and wanton youth for the sating of their passions, and for a girl to put on the veil was virtually to submit herself to prostitution.(60) The Church was drunken with the lust of power, glory and pleasures. Judgment was sure to come, and men should bow humbly before God who alone could rectify the evils and put an end to the schism. Descriptions such as these must be used with discrimination, and it would be wrong to deduce from them that the entire clerical body was corrupt. The diseases, however, must have been deep-seated to call forth such a lament from a man of Clamanges’ position.

The author did not call to open battle like the German Reformer at a later time, but suggested as a remedy prayers, processions and fasts. His watchword was that the Church must humble itself before it can be rebuilt.(61) It was, however, a bold utterance and forms an important part of that body of literature which so powerfully moulded opinion at the time of the Reformatory councils.

The loud complaints against the state of morals at the papal court and beyond during the Avignon period increased, if possible, in strength during the time of the schism. The list of abuses to be corrected which the Council of Constance issued, Oct. 30, 1417, includes the official offences of the curia, such as reservations, annates, the sale of indulgences and the unrestricted right of appeals to the papal court. The subject of chastity it remained for individual writers to press. In describing the third Babylon, Petrarch was even more severe than Clamanges who wrote of conditions as they existed nearly a century later and accused the papal household of practising adultery, rape and all manners of fornication.(62)ois, La vie en France au moyen âge d’après quelques moralistes du temps, Paris, 1908, pp. 320, 336, etc. Clamanges declared that many parishes insisted upon the priests keeping concubines as a precaution in defence of their own families. Against all canonical rules John XXIII. gave a dispensation to the illegitimate son of Henry IV. of England, who was only ten years old, to enter orders.(63) The case of John XXIII. was an extreme one, but it must be remembered, that in Bologna where he was sent as cardinal-legate, his biographer, Dietrich of Nieheim, says that two hundred matrons and maidens, including some nuns, fell victims to the future pontiff’s amours. Dietrich Vrie in his History of the Council of Constance said: "The supreme pontiffs, as I know, are elected through avarice and simony and likewise the other bishops are ordained for gold. The old proverb; ’Freely give, for freely ye have received’ is now most vilely perverted and runs ’Freely I have not received and freely I will not give, for I have bought my bishopric with a great price and must indemnify myself impiously for my outlay.’ ... If Simon Magus were now alive he might buy with money not only the Holy Ghost but God the Father and Me, God the Son."(64) But bad as was the moral condition of the hierarchy and papacy at the time of the schism, it was not so bad as during the last half century of the Middle Ages. The Reformatory councils are the best, though by no means the only, proof that a deep moral vitality existed in the Church. Their very summons and assembling were a protest against clerical corruption and hypocrisy "in head and members,"—from the pope down to the most obscure priest,—and at the same time a most hopeful sign of future betterment.

§ 25. Nicolas of Cusa, Scholar and Churchman.

Of the theologians of the generation following Gerson and D’Ailly none occupies a more conspicuous place than the German Nicolas of Cusa, 1401–1464. After taking a prominent part in the Basel council in its earlier history, he went into the service of Eugenius IV. and distinguished himself by practical efforts at Church reform and by writings in theology and other departments of human learning.

Born at Cues near Treves, the son of a boatman, he left the parental home on account of harsh treatment. Coming under the patronage of the count of Manderscheid, he went to Deventer, where he received training in the school conducted by the Brothers of the Common Life. He studied law in Padua, and reached the doctorate, but exchanged law for theology because, to follow the statement of his opponent, George of Heimburg, he had failed in his first case. At Padua he had for one of his teachers Cesarini, afterwards cardinal and a prominent figure in the Council of Basel.

In 1432 he appeared in Basel as the representative of Ulrich of Manderscheid, archbishop-elect of Treves, to advocate Ulrich’s cause against his rivals Rabanus of Helmstatt, bishop of Spires, whom the pope had appointed archbishop of the Treves diocese. Identifying himself closely with the conciliar body, Nicolas had a leading part in the proceedings with the Hussites and went with the majority in advocating the superiority of the council over the pope. His work on Catholic Unity,—De concordantia catholica,—embodying his views on this question and dedicated to the council 1433, followed the earlier treatments of Langenstein, Nieheim and Gerson. A general council, being inspired by the Holy Spirit, speaks truly and infallibly. The Church is the body of the faithful—unitas fidelium — and is represented in a general council. The pope derives his authority from the consent of the Church, a council has power to dethrone him for heresy and other causes and may not be prorogued or adjourned without its own consent. Peter received no more authority from Christ than the other Apostles. Whatever was said to Peter was likewise said to the others. All bishops are of equal authority and dignity, whether their jurisdiction be episcopal, archiepiscopal, patriarchal or papal, just as all presbyters are equal.(65)

In spite of these views, when the question arose as to the place of meeting the Greeks, Nicolas sided with the minority in favor of an Italian city, and was a member of the delegations appointed by the minority which visited Eugenius IV. at Bologna and went to Constantinople. This was in 1437 and from that time forward he was a ready servant of Eugenius and his two successors. Aeneas Sylvius, afterwards Pius II., called him the Hercules of the Eugenians. Aeneas also pronounced him a man notable for learning in all branches of knowledge and on account of his godly life.(66)

Eugenius employed his new supporter as legate to arrange terms of peace with the German Church and princes, an end he saw accomplished in the concordat of Vienna, 1447. He was rewarded by promotion to the college of cardinals, and in 1452 was made bishop of Brixen in the Tyrol. Here he sought to introduce Church reforms, and he travelled as the papal legate in the same interest throughout the larger part of Germany.

By attempting to assert all the mediaeval feoffal rights of his diocese, the bishop came into sharp conflict with Siegmund, duke of Austria. Even the interdict pronounced by two popes did not bring the duke to terms. He declared war against the bishop and, taking him prisoner, forced from him a promise to renounce the old rights which his predecessors for many years had not asserted. Once released, the bishop treated his oath as null, on the ground that it had been forced from him, and in this he was supported by Pius II. In 1460 he went to Rome and died at Todi, Umbria, a few years later.

Nicolas of Cusa knew Greek and Hebrew, and perhaps has claim to being the most universal scholar of Germany up to his day since Albertus Magnus. He was interested in astronomy, mathematics and botany, and, as D’Ailly had done before, he urged, at the Council of Basel, the correction of the calendar. The literary production on which he spent most labor was a discussion of the problems of theology—De docta ignorantia. Here he attacked the scholastic method and showed the influence upon his mind of mysticism, the atmosphere of which he breathed at Deventer. He laid stress upon the limitations of the human mind and the inability of the reason to find out God exhaustively. Faith, which he defined as a state of the soul given of God’s grace, finds out truths the intellect cannot attain to.(67) His views had an influence upon Faber Stapulensis who edited the Cusan’s works and was himself a French forerunner of Luther in the doctrine of justification by faith.

His last labors, in connection with the crusade against the Turks pushed by Pius II., led him to studies in the Koran and the preparation of a tract,—De cribatione Alcoran,—in which he declared that false religions have the true religion as their basis.

It is as an ecclesiastical mediator, and as a reformer of clerical and conventual abuses that the cardinal has his chief place in history. He preached in the vernacular. In Bamberg he secured the prohibition of new brotherhoods, in Magdeburg the condemnation of the sale of indulgences for money. In Salzburg and other places he introduced reforms in convents, and in connection with other members of his family he founded the hospital at Cues with beds for 33 patients. He showed his interest in studies by providing for the training of 20 boys in Deventer. He dwelt upon the rotation of the earth on its axis nearly a century before Copernicus. He gave reasons for regarding the donation of Constantine spurious, and he also called in question the genuineness of other parts of the Isidorian Decretals.

On the other hand, the cardinal was a thorough churchman and obedient child of the Church. As the agent of Nicolas V. he travelled in Germany announcing the indulgence of the Jubilee Year, and through him, it is said, indulgences to the value of 200,000 gulden were sold for the repair of St. Peter’s.

This noble and many-sided man has been coupled together with Gutenberg by Janssen,—the able and learned apologist of the Catholic Church in the closing years of the Middle Ages,—the one as the champion of clerical and Church discipline, the other the inventor of the printing-press. It is no disparagement of the impulses and work of Nicolas to say that he had not the mission of the herald of a new age in thought and religion as it was given to Gutenberg to promote culture and civilization by his invention.(68) He did not possess the gift of moral and doctrinal conviction and foresight which made the monk of Wittenberg the exponent and the herald of a radical, religious reformation whose permanent benefits are borne witness to by a large section of Christendom.

§ 26. Popular Preachers.

During the century and a half closing with 1450, there were local groups of preachers as well as isolated pulpit orators who exercised a deep influence upon congregations. The German mystics with Eckart and John Tauler at their head preached in Strassburg, Cologne and along the Rhine. D’Ailly and Gerson stood before select audiences, and give lustre to the French pulpit. Wyclif, at Oxford, and John Huss in Bohemia, attracted great attention by their sermons and brought down upon themselves ecclesiastical condemnation. Huss was one of a number of Bohemian preachers of eminence. Wyclif sought to promote preaching by sending out a special class of men, his "pore preachers."

The popular preachers constitute another group, though the period does not furnish one who can be brought into comparison with the field-preacher, Berthold of Regensburg, the Whitefield of his century, d. 1272. Among the popular preachers of the time the most famous were Bernardino and John of Capistrano, both Italians, and members of the Observant wing of the Franciscan order, and the Spanish Dominican, Vincent Ferrer. To a later age belong those bright pulpit luminaries, Savonarola of Florence and Geiler of Strassburg.

Bernardino of Siena, 1380–1444, was praised by Pius II. as a second Paul. He made a marked impression upon Italian audiences and was a favorite with pope Martin V. His voice, weak and indistinct at first, was said to have been made strong and clear through the grace of Mary, to whom he turned for help. He was the first vicar-general of the Observants, who numbered only a few congregations in Italy when he joined them, but increased greatly under his administration. In 1424 he was in Rome and, as Infessura the Roman diarist reports,(69) so influenced the people that they brought their games and articles of adornment to the Capitol and made a bonfire of them. Wherever he went to preach, a banner was carried before him containing the monogram of Christ, IHS, with twelve rays centring in the letters. He urged priests to put the monogram on the walls of churches and public buildings, and such a monogram may still be seen on the city building of Siena.(70) The Augustinians and Dominicans and also Poggio attacked him for this practice. In 1427, he appeared in Rome to answer the charges. He was acquitted by Martin V., who gave him permission to preach everywhere, and instructed him to hold an eighty-days’ mission in the papal city itself. In 1419, he appeared in the Lombard cities, where the people were carried away by his exhortations to repentance, and often burned their trinkets and games in the public squares. His body lies in Aquila, and he was canonized by Nicolas V., 1450.

John of Capistrano, 1386–1456, a lawyer, and at an early age intrusted with the administration of Perugia, joined the Observants in 1416 and became a pupil of Bernardino. He made a reputation as an inquisitor in Northern Italy, converting and burning heretics and Jews. No one could have excelled him in the ferocity of his zeal against heresy. His first appointment as inquisitor was made in 1426, and his fourth appointment 23 years later in 1449.(71)

As a leader of his order, he defended Bernardino in 1427, and was made vicar-general in 1443. He extended his preaching to Vienna and far up into Germany, from Nürnberg to Dresden, Leipzig, Magdeburg and Breslau, making everywhere a tremendous sensation. He used the Latin or Italian, which had to be interpreted to his audiences. These are reported to have numbered as many as thirty thousand.(72) He carried relics of Bernardino with him, and through them and his own instrumentality many miracles were said to have been performed. His attendants made a note of the wonderful works on the spot.(73) The spell of his preaching was shown by the burning of pointed shoes, games of cards, dice and other articles of pleasure or vanity. Thousands of heretics are also reported to have yielded to his persuasions. He was called by Pius II. to preach against the Hussites, and later against the Turks. He was present at the siege of Belgrade, and contributed to the successful defence of the city and the defeat of Mohammed II. He was canonized in 1690.

The life of Vincent Ferrer, d. 1419, the greatest of Spanish preachers, fell during the period of the papal schism, and he was intimately identified with the controversies it called forth. His name is also associated with the gift of tongues and with the sect of the Flagellants. This devoted missionary, born in Valencia, joined the Dominican order, and pursued his studies in the universities of Barcelona and Lerida. He won the doctorate of theology by his tract on the Modern Schism in the Church—De moderno ecclesiae schismate. Returning to Valencia, he gained fame as a preacher, and was appointed confessor to the queen of Aragon, Iolanthe, and counsellor to her husband, John I. In 1395, Benedict XIII. called him to be chief penitentiary in Avignon and master of the papal palace. Two years later he returned to Valencia with the title of papal legate. He at first defended the Avignon obedience with great warmth, but later, persuaded that Benedict was not sincere in his professions looking to the healing of the schism, withdrew from him his support and supported the Council of Constance.

Ferrer’s apostolic labors began in 1399. He itinerated through Spain, Northern Italy and France, preaching two and three times a day on the great themes of repentance and the nearness of the judgment. He has the reputation of being the most successful of missionaries among the Jews and Mohammedans. Twenty-five thousand Jews and eight thousand Mohammedans are said to have yielded to his persuasions. Able to speak only Spanish, his sermons, though they were not interpreted, are reported to have been understood in France and Italy. The gift of tongues was ascribed to him by his contemporaries as well as the gift of miracles. Priests and singers accompanied him on his tours, and some of the hymns sung were Vincent’s own compositions. His audiences are given as high as 70,000, an incredible number, and he is said to have preached twenty thousand times. He also preached to the Waldenses in their valleys and to the remnant of the Cathari, and is said to have made numerous converts. He himself was not above the suspicion of heresy, and Eymerich made the charge against him of declaring that Judas Iscariot hanged himself because the people would not permit him to live, and that he found pardon with God.(74) He was canonized by Calixtus III., 1455. The tale is that Ferrer noticed this member of the Borgia family as a young priest in Valencia, and made the prediction that one day he would reach the highest office open to mortal man.(75)

On his itineraries Ferrer was also accompanied by bands of Flagellants. He himself joined in the flagellations, and the scourge with which he scourged himself daily, consisting of six thongs, is said still to be preserved in the Carthusian convent of Catalonia, scala coeli. Both Gerson and D’Ailly attacked Ferrer for his adoption of the Flagellant delusion. In a letter addressed to the Spanish preacher, written during the sessions of the Council of Constance, Gerson took the ground that both the Old Testament and the New Testament forbid violence done to the body, quoting in proof Deut. 14:1, "Ye shall not cut yourselves." He invited him to come to Constance, but the invitation was not accepted.(76)
