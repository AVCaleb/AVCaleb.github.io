教育与真正宗教的进展密不可分。这一时期的学术文化史，以十一世纪后期西欧开始的显著觉醒，以及十二世纪大学的兴起为标志。后者是人类智力发展进程中最重大的事件之一。十一世纪的复兴，表现为人们对学校的兴趣显著恢复、杰出教师相继出现、古典研究重新开展，以及人类心智视野的拓展。

罗马帝国的市立学校被四、五世纪蛮族的入侵一扫而空，几乎未留下什么遗迹。自特土良 (Tertullian) 和耶柔米 (Jerome) 时代起，教会中的主流意见便敌视异教学问，至查士丁尼 (Justinian) 关闭雅典大学之举而达到顶点。但即使教会教师对古典文学抱持友善态度，旧有的罗马学校能否经受哥特人 (Goth)、汪达尔人 (Vandal) 和匈人 (Hun) 进攻所带来的冲击，仍属可疑。

中世纪早期的学校附属于修道院和主教座堂，直到十三世纪，市立学校才重新出现，而且是在遥远的北方，即德国和低地地区 (Lowlands)。新教育史上首先应提及的人物是卡西安 (Cassian)，他于404年在马赛 (Marseilles) 创立了圣维克多 (St. Victor) 修道院学校。但西欧办学的持久动力，则应归功于努尔西亚的本笃 (Benedict of Nursia)。《本笃会规》将教育列为宗教的辅助事业，规定由修会成员教养儿童，并抄写手稿。我们的图书馆能够保存古典作家及教父的著作，实有赖于本笃会士，尤其是熙笃会士 (Cistercians)。

查理大帝 (Charlemagne) 设立宫廷学校——一种为德意志帝国服务的师范学校——并颁布有关教育的敕令，这一明智政策，与英格兰阿尔弗雷德 (Alfred) 的政策一道，借王室的扶持给学术带来了新的动力。查理大帝宫廷中的阿尔昆 (Alcuin)、英格兰的阿瑟 (Asser)，以及大胆查理 (Charles the Bold) 宫廷中的约翰·司各脱·爱留根纳 (John Scotus Erigena)，都是较为杰出的教师。教育可能并不限于教士，因为修道院有两类学校：内校招收预备入修道院的献身儿童，外校则似乎具有较为普遍的性质。主教座堂学校的首要目的，即使不是唯一目的，也是训练青年担任座堂职务——canonici puri。教育的主要目的，即使不是排他性的目的，也是培养人进入司祭职分和修道院。1 十一世纪，德国所有修道院和主教座堂都设有学校，2 其中威悉河畔的科尔韦 (Corvey) 与希尔德斯海姆 (Hildesheim) 尤为著名；在意大利，米兰 (Milan) 和帕尔马 (Parma) 的学校也颇负盛名。

但在那个世纪，教育中心转移到了法国。贝克 (Bec)、兰斯 (Rheims)、奥尔良 (Orleans)、拉昂 (Laon) 和巴黎的学校无可匹敌，其声誉吸引了来自英格兰和德国的学生，甚至包括修士、司祭和主教。3 热尔贝 (Gerbert)，即后来的西尔维斯特二世 (Sylvester II.，卒于1003年)，曾在兰斯赢得“学术复兴者”的称号；然而，兰斯的声望后来让位于兰弗朗克 (Lanfranc) 和安瑟伦 (Anselm) 主持下的贝克更盛大的声望。学生自远方慕名而来；在热情颂扬他们的奥德里克·维塔利斯 (Ordericus Vitalis) 看来，即便是最繁盛时期的雅典，也会在每一门学问上向兰弗朗克致敬。4 继这两位院长之后，教师代有其人，奥德里克称他们为“谨慎的舵手和熟练的御者”。一位功成名就的人向自己的教师致以如此隆重的敬意，实属罕见：兰弗朗克出任坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 大主教后访问罗马时，亚历山大二世 (Alexander II.) 就曾如此待他。5 教皇起身张开双臂迎接他，对旁观者说，他接待兰弗朗克，与其说是接待一位大主教，不如说是接待自己曾在其脚前受教的老师。约卒于1120年的诺让的吉贝尔记载，在他幼年时，法国教师极为罕见；但到他写作之时，法国每一个较大的城镇都有教师。6 母亲们热切希望儿子受教育，从吉贝尔讲述自己母亲的事例便可看出。

与中世纪早期一样，在这中期阶段，人们也尚未想到普及教育的观念。当时也没有我们所谓的美文学和一般文学。7 一切文学都直接关涉宗教题材。8 沃尔特·马普 (Walter Map) 和索尔兹伯里的约翰等最接近现代文人观念的人，也都是教士。修道院的创办者，如贝克的创办者埃尔卢安 (Herlouin)，往往既不会读，也不会写。奥德里克说，在兰弗朗克前往贝克之前，历经六位公爵的统治，诺曼人 (Norman) 中几乎没有一个人致力于学问。不过，阿基坦公爵威廉 (Duke William of Aquitaine，卒于1030年) 自幼受过教育；据说他常在夜间读书，直到困倦不支，并且藏有一批书籍。9

这一时代最卓越的教师，是拉昂的安瑟伦 (Anselm of Laon)、尚波的威廉 (William of Champeaux)、沙特尔的伯纳德 (Bernard of Chartres)、孔什的威廉 (William of Conches)，而其中尤以阿伯拉尔 (Abaelard) 为首。他们都属于法国。在他们身上，学校随教师而定；学生前往求学，与其说是奔赴某个地方，不如说是投奔某位教师。然而，人们的兴趣日益集中于巴黎，那里有好几所学校——主教座堂学校、圣热纳维耶芙 (St. Genevieve)、圣维克多和圣但尼 (St. Denis) 学校。10 我们对这些人的认识，主要来自阿伯拉尔和索尔兹伯里的约翰。约翰于1137–1149年间在法国求学十二年，曾在上述诸人门下受教。他对当时学业、教师的方法与彼此竞争的描述，见于《逻辑学辩护》(Metalogicus)。

尚波的威廉，卒于1121年，是拉昂的安瑟伦的学生。他在巴黎主教座堂学校赢得声誉，却在与才华横溢的阿伯拉尔交锋时失去了自己的地位。他退居圣维克多，生命的最后八年则用于治理沙隆 (Chalons) 教区。他是一位极端实在论者。

拉昂的安瑟伦及其兄弟拉尔夫 (Ralph) 的教学，吸引了南至米兰、北至不来梅 (Bremen) 的学生。索尔兹伯里的约翰称这兄弟二人为“高卢灿烂的明灯”，11 而“博士之师”则是安瑟伦公认的称号。这位卒于1117年的教师，或许曾是贝克的安瑟伦的学生；阿伯拉尔曾在他的听众之列，却对他出言轻侮。但我们对这个人的判断，应当以索尔兹伯里的约翰的赞誉，而非阿伯拉尔的蔑视为依据。他的《行间注释》(glossa interlinearis)，是一部以敷衍释义方式解说《武加大译本》(Vulgate) 的注释书，数百年间一直备受推崇。12

约在1140年，沙特尔的伯纳德被索尔兹伯里的约翰誉为“近世高卢最为丰沛的学问之泉”，又称为“我们时代最完美的柏拉图主义者”。13 他用以下的话承认自己受惠于古代作家：“我们如同站在巨人肩上的侏儒，因此能够比他们看得更多、更远；但这不是因为我们的眼光更敏锐，或身材更高，而是因为我们被举到了那些巨人的身上。我们的时代享有前代的馈赠；我们知道得更多，不是因为才智更优，而是因为我们利用了前人的成果。”14

孔什的威廉，卒于1152年（？），其名来自他出生的诺曼底小村庄。他和老师沙特尔的伯纳德一样，强调应当透彻掌握文法，作为一切学问的基础。索尔兹伯里的约翰撰写《逻辑学辩护》，似乎正是为了维护老师们的主张：相对于辩证法，文法研究具有根本的重要性。但他所倡导的是一项注定失败的事业。经院哲学正在扼杀人文主义初萌的新芽。15 孔什的威廉敢于不受公认见解的约束，否认夏娃确实是由亚当的一根肋骨造成的。普尔认为，威廉教导的根本，可见于他自己的话：“我们借着认识受造物，达到对造物主的认识。”16

学业至少在理论上仍沿用旧有的三艺 (trivium) 和四艺 (quadrivium) 体系：三艺包括文法、修辞和辩证法，四艺包括算术、几何、天文和音乐。这些学科的范围，比我们从其中某些名称所联想到的要广。例如，在沙特尔的伯纳德那里，文法远不止于技术性的规则和词语的基本区分。它还包括转义和修辞格，分析作者的整体思想，并阐明其中涉及自然、科学和伦理问题的隐喻典故。教学的内容远远超出了查理大帝敕令所规定的范围。然而，这一切学问都只是神学的前厅，其价值仅在于为神学作入门准备。维特里的雅各 (Jacob of Vitry，卒于1244年) 将七艺与神学比较时，17 说道：“逻辑是好的，因为它教导我们辨别真伪；文法是好的，因为它教导我们正确地说话和写作；修辞是好的，因为它教导我们优雅地言说，并说服他人。几何也是好的，它教导我们测量大地；算术，即计算之术，使我们能够估量自己年日的短暂；音乐使我们想起蒙福者甜美的歌唱；天文引导我们思考在神面前灿烂发光的天体。但神学远为美好，唯有它配称为自由之艺，因为唯有它能使人的灵魂脱离苦难。”

英诺森三世 (Innocent III.) 借第四次拉特兰会议 (Fourth Lateran) 的教规，命令所有主教座堂都设立文法教师和神学讲师，并且只将高位作为奖赏，给予那些额上流汗、刻苦治学的人。18 他所想到的，仅仅是攻读神学的人。

沿用数百年的教科书仍然盛行，例如卡西奥多鲁斯 (Cassiodorus) 的著作、波菲利 (Porphyry) 的《导论》(Isagoge)、亚里士多德 (Aristotle) 的《范畴篇》(Categories) 和《解释篇》(De interpretatione)、波爱修斯 (Boethius) 的《音乐》(Music) 和《哲学的慰藉》(Consolations of Philosophy)、马尔提安努斯·卡佩拉 (Martianus Capella) 的著作，以及普里西安 (Priscian) 和多纳图斯 (Donatus) 的文法书。19 不过，一股新的潮流已清晰可见；一些主要教育家在讲授中公开采用古典作品，以及当时著述对古典作品的运用，正是这一潮流最确凿的证据。

耶柔米 (Jerome) 对古代经典所作的谴责，为卡西安 (Cassian) 所接受，并传给了后世。在数百年间，除极少数例外，蒙昧主义者几乎独占了这一领域。阿尔昆 (Alcuin) 晚年背弃维吉尔 (Virgil)，把他的作品看作一堆“虚妄的寓言”，又在写给一位初学修士的信中，劝他不要让这位诗人繁茂而有害的辞藻玷污心灵；这实在无助于阿尔昆的声誉。20 利奥 (Leo) 在991年答复奥尔良的阿努尔夫 (Arnulf of Orleans) 时辩称，使徒彼得并不熟悉柏拉图 (Plato)、维吉尔、泰伦提乌斯 (Terence) 等作家，也不认识任何伪哲学家；上帝从起初所拣选作他器皿的，就不是演说家和哲学家，而是无知的乡野之人。21 可敬的彼得 (Peter the Venerable) 也扬声反对这些作家。然而，这些警告22 并不足以使所有人都远避拉丁作家的魅力。

热尔贝 (Gerbert) 讲授维吉尔、斯塔提乌斯 (Statius)、泰伦提乌斯、尤维纳利斯 (Juvenal)、佩尔修斯 (Persius)、贺拉斯 (Horace) 和卢坎 (Lucan) 的作品。23 他由这些作品进而讲授哲学。彼得·达米安 (Peter Damiani) 将研读诗人和哲学家的作品比作掠取埃及人的财物。这些作品有助于磨炼理解力；研读教会作家的著作则是为上帝建造会幕。贝克的安瑟伦 (Anselm of Bee) 提倡研读维吉尔及其他古典作家，同时劝人排除含有邪恶暗示的论著。24 索尔兹伯里的约翰 (John of Salisbury) 的老师们热心研读这类作品。约翰在篇幅不大的《逻辑学辩护》(Metalogicus) 中，引用了不下七位古典诗人：斯塔提乌斯、马尔提安 (Martian)、维吉尔、贺拉斯、奥维德 (Ovid)、卡图卢斯 (Catullus) 和佩尔修斯，其中有些还引用了多次。他说，无论你信奉何种哲学，只要到维吉尔和卢坎的作品中探寻，就必能找到哲学的精髓。25 他抱怨旧派人士把研读古典诗人与历史学家的人比作行动迟缓的驴子，讥笑他们比石头还愚钝。26 阿伯拉尔 (Abaelard) 给予维吉尔以先知应得的敬重。英国的会吏长布卢瓦的彼得 (Peter of Blois，卒于1204年) 引用西塞罗 (Cicero)、撒路斯提乌斯 (Sallust)、李维 (Livy)、库尔提乌斯 (Curtius)、塔西佗 (Tacitus)、苏埃托尼乌斯 (Suetonius)、塞涅卡 (Seneca) 的《书信》及其他作家的作品。格罗斯泰斯特 (Grosseteste) 熟悉奥维德、塞涅卡、贺拉斯和其他古典作家。然而，全面的文艺复兴尚未来临。巴黎大学最早的章程将古典作品排除在课程之外。经院学者的精微思辨虽然并未完全忽视古典作品，却能不借助这些作品而构建宏大的神学体系，尽管他们大量而自信地援用了亚里士多德。

学校的纪律十分严厉。狠狠鞭打一顿，被视为促进学业进步的有益手段，能驱逐智力迟钝和精神昏沉的邪灵。Degere sub virga，即在杖下度日，是接受教育的另一种说法。后来，教师的就职仪式包括授予一根杖，并要求他当众鞭打一个男孩，以展示自己的本领，至少在英国是如此。27 如果诺让的吉贝尔 (Guibert of Nogent) 的遭遇具有代表性，那么接受教育的过程，确实是一段肉体受苦的经历。

吉贝尔对自身经历的记述，是我们现有的关于中世纪学校生活最详尽的描述，也是文学作品中最有趣的学童经历记述之一。28 这孩子很早就被寡母送去上学，老师对他拳打杖击，毫不留情；这位老师本人是在年事已高时才学文法的。老师虽不是出色的文法学家，吉贝尔却见证了他道德志向的坚毅，以及他给学生带来的有益道德影响。鞭打每天都有，但这孩子求学的热忱并未冷却。一天傍晚，他回到家里，解开衬衣，母亲看见他肩上的鞭痕和瘀伤，因为他那天被打得青一块紫一块。29 她既愤慨又怜惜，提议儿子放弃为担任司铎而作的准备，并愿意为他置办从事骑士生涯所需的装备。但吉贝尔情绪激动，对这样的建议深感不满。

在克吕尼 (Cluny)，学生睡在教师近旁，夜里若必须起身，也须先得到教师的许可。他们若在唱诗篇或其他圣歌、就寝或任何别的事情上犯了过错，便要只穿着衬衣，由院长或其他教师用预先备好的细枝鞭打。30

然而，也不乏教师反对这种方法。安瑟伦提倡以爱心和信任施教，并强调，熟练的工匠绝不会单靠敲打，就把金片制成所要的形象；他会用灵巧而轻柔的手将它压制成形。不停地责打，只会使人变得粗野。有位修道院长说：“我们昼夜不停地惩戒托付给我们照管的孩子，他们却越来越坏。”安瑟伦回答说：“当真如此！那么，他们长大后会成为什么呢？愚笨的蠢人。把人变成禽兽，真是好教育！……倘若你在园中栽一棵树，将它四面围住，使它不能伸展枝条，那么，几年以后，当你解除它的束缚时，会看见什么呢？一棵枝条弯曲而瘦弱的树；你如此无理地束缚它，难道不正是你的过错吗？”31

当时通行的原则是，凡境况不允许付费的人，都可免费受教育；其余的人则自行缴费。沙特尔的富尔贝 (Fulbert of Chartres) 向人数迅速增加的学生收取费用，认为哲学值得人付出这样的代价。但这种做法被视为例外，并遭到反对。32 阿尔昆的话：“你若愿意求学，不花钱就能得到所寻求的”，被铭刻在萨尔茨堡 (Salzburg) 的圣彼得修道院上。33 克吕尼引以为荣的是，它对最卑微学生的照料，也像对宫廷中孩子的照料一样周到。34

书籍与学校相伴相生，两者对教会思想的进步都不可或缺。穆里 (Muri) 修道院的中世纪藏书目录，强有力地申明了思想生活与宗教生活的密切联系。其中说，我们理当不断抄录、装饰、修订并注释书籍，因为属灵之人的生活若没有书籍，就毫无意义。35

拥有几卷书的修道院是有福的。36 修道院和主教座堂几乎是仅有的藏书之所。在这里，书籍最能免遭入侵者的肆意毁坏和中世纪频繁发生的火灾；在这里，会读书的人也能接触到它们。当时流传着一句格言，其源头最早可追溯到圣巴尔布昂奥日 (St. Barbe-en-Auge) 的教士戈特弗里德 (Gottfried)：没有图书馆的修道院，就如没有兵器的堡垒。37 中世纪早期，约克 (York)、富尔达 (Fulda)、卡西诺山 (Monte Cassino) 以及其他修道院都有小规模的藏书。这些书受到极大珍视，教士们不惜远行求取；威尔茅斯 (Wearmouth) 修道院长比斯科普 (Biscop) 就曾为此五赴意大利。在格列高利七世 (Gregory VII.) 之后的两个多世纪中，书籍的使用和数量都有增加；但直到十四世纪，彼特拉克 (Petrarch) 的热忱才为图书馆史开辟了一个新时代。随后的文艺复兴时期，人们对古代手稿的渴求空前强烈，而学者从君士坦丁堡的迁出，使这种渴求有了得到满足的可能。

文学与宗教长久受惠于西欧修道院，不仅因为它们保存书籍，也因为它们增抄书籍。本笃会 (St. Benedict) 修士作为图书馆的创建者以及教父文献和古典文学的守护者，居于首位。他们的会规要求每天作一定数量的阅读；大斋期开始时，每人从修道院藏书中领取一本，须将它“从头到尾读完”。这项规定犹如一道光，照耀着修道制度的历程，尽管许多修道院可能根本没有书籍，有些也不大懂得书籍的价值。

藏书数百部，在当时相对而言，就如今日藏书数十万卷的图书馆一样庞大。十二世纪，弗勒里 (Fleury) 藏书238卷，圣里基耶 (St. Riquier) 藏书258卷。38 十一世纪，英国克罗伊兰 (Croyland) 修道院的毁灭，使“300卷原本和400多卷较小的书籍”付之一炬。修道院建筑在夜间被大火烧毁。院长英古尔夫 (Ingulph) 叙述这场灾难的信十分引人入胜，其中提及那些以图画装饰、并饰有金十字架的精美手稿。这位善良的院长描述了小教堂、病舍及其他建筑部分的损失之后，接着说：“我们的地窖，连同那些盛满啤酒的酒桶，也都烧毁了。”39

这一时期的一些藏书目录保存至今。爱德华兹列举了三十三份英国图书馆的中世纪目录。40 萨尔茨堡普吕费宁 (Prüfening) 的1158年目录，出自“一位天生的图书馆员”之手，41 将藏书分为三类：圣经抄本、教父著作和近世作家的作品。最常见的书籍是圣经或其中的一部分、礼仪书，以及奥古斯丁 (Augustine)、大格列高利 (Gregory the Great)、耶柔米和安布罗斯 (Ambrose) 的著作；加洛林时代的作家中，则以比德 (Bede) 和阿尔昆为多。皮卡第 (Picardy) 的科尔比 (Corbie) 修道院十二世纪的目录列有奥古斯丁著作39部、耶柔米16部、比德13部、波爱修斯 (Boethius) 15部、西塞罗5部，以及泰伦提乌斯、李维、普林尼 (Pliny) 和塞涅卡作品的抄本。42 在中世纪较晚的作家中，最常见的是安瑟伦、伯纳德 (Bernard)、雨果 (Hugo) 和阿伯拉尔的著作，不过许多藏书中连一位近世作家的作品也没有。班贝格 (Bamberg) 圣米歇尔斯贝格 (St. Michelsberg) 的藏书在其他方面颇为丰富，却只有一部近世著作，即安瑟伦的《默想录》(Meditations)。普吕费宁图书馆各有安瑟伦、雨果、阿伯拉尔、伦巴德 (the Lombard) 和格拉提安 (Gratian) 的一部著作。古典作家的作品也很常见。达勒姆 (Durham) 图书馆藏有西塞罗、泰伦提乌斯、维吉尔、贺拉斯、克劳狄安 (Claudian)、斯塔提乌斯、撒路斯提乌斯、苏埃托尼乌斯、昆体良 (Quintilian) 和其他拉丁作家作品的抄本。43 有时古典作品单独编目，诺伊明斯特 (Neumünster) 就是如此。

捐赠书籍被视为可贵的善举。巴黎主教彼得 (Peter) 在启程前往圣地之前，将300部著作交给圣维克托 (St. Victor) 修道院保管。44 格罗斯泰斯特 (Grosseteste) 遗赠自己的藏书给牛津的方济各会士。45 热尔贝 (Gerbert)，即后来的西尔维斯特二世 (Sylvester II.)，说他因朋友们慷慨相助，得以在罗马、意大利和佛兰德斯 (Flanders) 购得一批书籍。46 那位满怀钦佩的编年史家认为，狄奥多里克 (Theodoric) 为自己所辖的圣埃夫鲁 (St. Evroult) 修道院购得旧新约各卷及大格列高利的全套著作，足以使他名垂后世。其他人效法他的善举，购得耶柔米、奥古斯丁、安布罗斯及其他教父的著作。47 可敬者彼得 (Peter the Venerable) 宣称，在克吕尼 (Cluny)，书籍，尤其是奥古斯丁的著作，被看得比黄金更宝贵。48

捐赠藏书有时附有条件，规定这些书必须外借。卡尔纳里乌斯的雅各 (Jacob of Carnarius) 就是如此，他于1234年以此为条件，将藏书赠给韦尔切利 (Vercelli) 的多明我会士。1270年，曾任坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 会吏长的司提反 (Stephen) 将自己的书籍赠给巴黎圣母院，条件是将它们借给贫困的神学生；1297年，茹瓦尼的彼得 (Peter of Joigny) 则将藏书直接遗赠给贫困学生。49 到了下一个世纪，彼特拉克将书籍遗赠给威尼斯的圣马可教堂 (St. Marks)，薄伽丘 (Boccaccio) 则将自己的这类财产遗赠给佛罗伦萨的奥古斯丁会修士。

手抄本有时被献在祭坛或圣徒的圣龛前，作为求献书者灵魂得医治的祭物——pro remedio animae suae。50 另一方面，在紧急情况下，书籍也会被典当或出售。1190年，伊利 (Ely) 主教朗尚的威廉 (William of Longchamps) 为赎回理查一世，典当了13部福音书抄本。51 1139年，洛尔施 (Lorsch) 修道院长迪耶莫 (Diemo) 因需要钱购置军事装备，卖掉了三部以黄金和宝石装饰的书。52 有些地方征税以资助图书馆，1215年的伊夫舍姆 (Evesham) 就是如此；同年，里昂会议也采用了类似办法。53 1240年，罗斯托克 (Rostock) 的博尔温亲王 (Prince Borwin) 赠给达尔贡 (Dargun) 修道院一海德土地，其收益须用于图书馆的需要。54

在所有书籍中，圣经抄本最受珍视。它们往往装有镶金嵌银的封面，有时还饰以宝石，并配有华美的彩饰。圣奥尔本斯 (St. Albans) 修道院长保罗 (Paul) 在院内图书馆安放了八部《诗篇》和两部福音书，均以黄金和宝石精美装饰，此外还有一部祝文集、一部使徒书信集及其他28部书。1295年，圣保罗座堂主任牧师在堂内发现12部镶有珠宝的福音书抄本，第十三部则与圣物一同存放在匣内。55

书籍起初存放在称为 armaria 的横式柜中，图书管理员则称为 armarius。约在十四世纪，回廊墙边开始设置书架。56 早在十三世纪，人们就用链子拴住书籍，以防热切的读者将它们偷走。57 1350年剑桥三一学院 (Trinity College, Cambridge) 的章程规定，某些书籍须始终留在图书馆中，用链子拴在原处，供院士使用。十六世纪，这一习惯在英国仍然盛行，当时教堂里的英文圣经便用链子拴在读经台上。分发书籍仍遵行古老的本笃会规。兰弗兰克 (Lanfranc) 于1070年为英国本笃会制定的章程，要求修士在大斋期第一个主日归还书籍。随后将书铺在地上，分配给各人供下一年阅读，每位修士一部。凡未读完自己所领书籍的人，必须俯伏在地，承认自己的疏忽。58 书籍外借并不罕见。伯纳德和可敬者彼得都既向人借书，也将书借给别人。59 熙笃会 (Cistercians) 为向会外人士出借书籍作了规定；1212年的巴黎会议坚持要求各修道院不得废弃这一良好惯例，并称之为一项怜悯之工。

书室，即 scriptorium，是完整修道院建筑的一部分，用来写作和抄写手稿。有时，修士有自己的小书室，称为 scriptoriolum，或将书籍存放在自己的房间里。伯纳德的秘书尼古拉 (Nicholas) 描述他的小房间紧邻病舍，“满是精选的神圣书籍”。60 继索尔兹伯里的约翰 (John of Salisbury) 出任沙特尔 (Chartres) 主教的塞勒的彼得 (Peter of Celle)，谈到自己的 scriptoriolum，说那里满是书籍，使他得以远离尘世的虚空与烦扰。他说，这地方分配给他，是供他读书、写作、默想、祈祷和敬拜主的。61

修道院长自己也在其他劳作之外从事抄写。1050至1057年任职的圣埃夫鲁修道院长狄奥多里克就是如此。他是一位娴熟的抄写员，据奥德里库斯·维塔利斯 (Ordericus Vitalis) 记述，62 他所抄写的祝文集、升阶经集 (Graduale) 和对经集 (Antiphonary)，收藏在修道院中，留下了“彰显其书法技艺的辉煌丰碑”。狄奥多里克还雇用他人抄写注释书和圣经前七卷。63 各修道院都用心延揽熟练的抄写员。院长保罗在1077至1093年间，使抄写成为圣奥尔本斯修道院的一项特色。他筹款设立抄写室 (scriptorium)，并从远方招来抄写员。十一世纪后期，德国南部的希尔绍 (Hirschau) 因院长威廉 (William) 而以这类工作闻名；他确保院内培养出十二名优秀的抄写员。这些人制作了许多抄本，据说威廉向他所改革的每一所修道院都赠送过书籍。同一世纪，埃默拉姆的抄写员奥特洛 (Othlo of Emmeram) 给我们留下了一份他所赠书籍的清单。64

修士在面对来世的实境时，生前勤于抄写有时竟能使他们大受裨益。奥德里库斯提到过这样一个人。65 这位修士亲手抄过一大卷圣经，但他在道德上犯有许多罪过。当恶灵前来索取他的灵魂时，天使拿出了他所抄写的圣书，将其中每一个字母逐一数点，抵偿一项罪。最后发现，字母比罪数多出一个。魔鬼试图再搜罗出一项罪来，却徒劳无功，于是主准许这位幸运的修士重返肉身，作应有的补赎。

抄写有时被规定为对违反修院纪律者的惩罚；加尔都西会 (Carthusian) 的会规规定，凡有能力抄写却不愿运用这门技艺的修士，不得饮酒。这项工作有时似乎极为拘束而劳累。巴伐利亚韦索布伦 (Wessobrunn) 的修士路易 (Lewis) 就有过这样的感受；他在所抄耶柔米《但以理书注释》之后附上以下诗句，并求读者为他祈祷：——

Dum scripsit friguit, et quod cum lumine solis

他抄写时饱受严寒，凡在日光下

Scribere non potuit, perfecit lumine noctis.

未能写完的，都在夜间灯光下完成。

“他抄写时冻得发僵；白日光下未能完成的，便借夜间灯光写完。”66

直到印刷机发明以前，书价始终昂贵。一位安茹 (Anjou) 伯爵为购得哈尔伯施塔特的海莫 (Haimo of Halberstadt) 的讲道集抄本，付出了200只羊和大量食物。1274年，一部缮写精美的圣经售价50马克，约合170美元，当时工钱每天只有一先令。梅特兰 (Maitland) 估计，一名修士抄写整部圣经需要十个月，其劳动按今日价值应值60或70英镑。67 然而，价格往往也会大幅降低；伯里的理查 (Richard of Bury) 在他的《爱书录》(Philobiblion) 中说，他曾以50英镑从圣奥尔本斯修道院购得32卷书。

抄写员与主教座堂的建造者一样，通常隐去自己的姓名。他们习惯在工作结束时，附上一句虔敬的话，有时也写些诙谐之语。经常附写的一句是：finito libro, sit laus et gloria Christo. “书已告成，愿颂赞与荣耀归于基督。”作者完成著述时常有的喜悦，一位抄写员也曾感受到，他写道：libro completo, saltat scriptor pede leto. “书已写完，抄写员欢欣起舞。”另一位则虔诚地表达自己的心情，写道：dentur pro penna scriptori caelica regna. “愿抄写员因其笔耕之劳，得享天国的赏赐。”68

当其他修士外出消遣、自己独留院中时，一位中世纪神学家如此生动地描述了在图书馆的幽静中与书交谈的乐趣：——

“我们的院舍空无一人，只有我自己，还有那些在孤寂饥饿中啃噬的大小老鼠。厅中没有人声，楼梯上没有脚步声……我坐在这里，惟有书籍作伴，品尝文学中甘美的蜜房。世上文学中的一切心灵，都汇集在一座图书馆里。这是圣殿的顶端，从这里我们可以看见世上的万国及其荣华。我把埃及和圣地安放在窗旁的柜子里。在它们旁边，是雅典和罗马帝国。从未有过像我这里所集结的这样一支大军。没有一位将军拥有我这样的士兵。没有一个王国的臣民，能及我的臣民一半显赫，也没有臣民能及他们一半守纪律。我可以随心所欲地把最傲慢的臣民放到上层或下层……我呼唤柏拉图 (Plato)，他就应声‘在这里’——一位高贵而坚强的士兵；‘亚里士多德 (Aristotle)！’‘在这里’——他一人便是一支大军。德摩斯梯尼 (Demosthenes)、普林尼、西塞罗、塔西佗 (Tacitus)、凯撒。‘在这里，’他们回答，带着永不衰老的青春向我微笑。他们都很谦逊，若不向他们说话，他们就不发一言。他们都很慷慨，从不拒绝回答。他们又都和睦相处……全世界都环绕着我，凡曾激动人心、点燃想象的一切，都无害地安居于此。我的书柜是时间的通衢。历代曾经劳作，世世代代曾经成长，他们所开的花全都散落在这里。这是一座结有不朽果实的园子，没有狗，也没有龙。”

十二世纪，大学作为一种确立的制度出现在欧洲。它迅速成为思想与文学生活中活跃不息的中心，成为学问与科学进步的工场。其体制具有民主性质，接纳各个阶层的人，使他们带着新思想走向社会，并具备成为时代领袖的才干。

起源。——大学是中世纪心智的产物，古代世界中没有任何事物可以与之充分对应。它们生长于主教座堂和修道院学术研究的土壤之上，但与早期学校之间并无有机的延续关系。它们独立成长，应一种需求而产生；这种需求是由欧洲生活环境的改变和思想的复兴所唤起的。它们的到来并无喧嚣声响，而是从不完善的开端逐渐发展为组织完备的学术社团。

大学也不是教会直接创办的。教会权威并没有像发动十字军那样使大学产生。我们所能说的只是，为大学奠基并构筑其初步架构的人是教士，而教皇也颇有远见，很早便成为大学的赞助者，并且像巴黎的情形那样，把大学总体行政的缰绳掌握在自己手中。人类知识各门类——文科、法律、医学和神学——实行专门研究的时机已经到来；其中最后一门，按维特里的雅各 (Jacob of Vitry) 的说法，“唯独配称为自由之艺，因为唯独它能使人的灵魂脱离苦难。”

大学的兴起有赖于个别教师的热忱；69这些教师无论身在何处，都以其辩证的技巧和个人魅力吸引学生。博洛尼亚 (Bologna) 借着伊尔内里乌斯 (Irnerius) 和其他教师，巴黎则借着一群以阿伯拉尔 (Abaelard) 为最杰出人物的学者，成为大学理念最早且最充分实现的中心。这些教师既满足了教育专门化的需求，也创造了这种需求。

行会的功绩也不应抹杀，其组织为大学提供了范式，博洛尼亚的情形尤其如此。大学是学术的行会，代表一个志趣相投、从事学术工作的共同体。也有可能须将一部分功绩归于阿拉伯的影响，萨莱诺 (Salerno) 医学校便是如此。

最初的大学兴起于意大利，其中最早的是萨莱诺和博洛尼亚。随后出现的是巴黎及法国其他大学。接下来是英国，然后是西班牙。布拉格 (Prague) 最先在中欧实现了这一理念。具有鲜明德意志特色的大学，其历史均不早于十四世纪下半叶：维也纳 (Vienna)，1365年；埃尔福特 (Erfurt)，1379年；海德堡 (Heidelberg)，1385年；科隆 (Cologne)，1388年。苏格兰的三所大学——圣安德鲁斯 (St. Andrews)、格拉斯哥 (Glasgow) 和阿伯丁 (Aberdeen)——创办于十五世纪。这一世纪还见证了遥远北方的哥本哈根 (Copenhagen) 和乌普萨拉 (Upsala) 两所大学的诞生。至十五世纪末，这类学术机构已有近八十所。其中有些后来消失，有些则始终只有地方性的声望。

萨莱诺、博洛尼亚、巴黎、帕多瓦 (Padua)、牛津 (Oxford)、剑桥 (Cambridge) 及其他一些大学，其存在并不依赖任何教皇或王室的特许状。图卢兹 (Toulouse，1229年) 和罗马 (Rome，1244年) 是最早凭教皇诏书创办的大学。那不勒斯 (Naples) 大学于1224年由皇帝弗里德里希二世 (Frederick II.) 创办。西班牙的帕伦西亚 (Palencia，1212年)、萨拉曼卡 (Salamanca，1230年) 和塞维利亚 (Seville，1254年) 三所大学，均由卡斯蒂利亚诸王创办。布拉格大学于1347年凭教皇与查理四世 (Charles IV.) 双方的特许状创办。有些大学源于原有大学内部的不满：帕多瓦大学起于一批学生脱离博洛尼亚；剑桥大学起于1209年一批学生脱离牛津；莱比锡 (Leipzig) 大学则于1409年因德意志“同乡团”不满在布拉格所受的待遇而兴起。海德堡是在教皇创办的学府中最早转向宗教改革的一所。70

组织。——大学最初所指的，并不是一套学科或从事研究的场所，而是教师与学生的集合——universitas magistrorum et scholarium。“大学”这个词曾用于任何一群人，universitas vestra 是表示“你们这个团体”或“你们全体”的常用语。71在十二、十三世纪，它常用于指称行会。学术行会，即大学，指的是从事学术研究的一群人。中世纪与我们如今所用“大学”一词相当的，是 studium 和 studium generale，即“研习场所”或“普遍研习场所”。因此，博洛尼亚大学被称为 studium Bononie 或 Bononiense——正如它在意大利至今仍被称为 studio Bolognese；巴黎称为 studium Parisiense，牛津称为 studium Oxoniense。附加的“普遍”一词针对的是学生，而不是知识门类的多样性，表示该 studium 向各地的学生开放。72到十五世纪，“大学”一词才有了今天的含义。用 alma 或 alma mater，即“慈爱的”或“慈母”，来称呼学府，始于十三世纪。

一所完整的大学至少须有四个学院：文学院——今在德国大学称为哲学院——以及法学院、医学院和神学院。最早创办的大学并未体现这一理念，有些大学在其存在的整个期间也始终不齐备。萨莱诺是一所医学校。博洛尼亚在一个多世纪中只是一所法学校。萨拉曼卡是西班牙现存最古老的教育机构，直到十四世纪末才有神学院。73巴黎最初是神学文化的中心，虽然1219年以前便已教授民法，却直到十七世纪才正式为民法研究作出安排。74将近一半的大学没有将神学列入课程。意大利的大学最初几乎无一例外，只限于研究法学和医学。其原因或许是有意避免与那些为培养司铎而设的主教学校和修道院学校发生冲突。文学院是层级最低的学院，其课程包括三艺 (trivium) 和四艺 (quadrivium) 所涵盖的七门学科；后来范围扩大，纳入了形而上学、语言、历史，以及法律、医学和神学研究所未涵盖的其他学科。神学被视为最高的、统领一切的学科。亚历山大四世 (Alexander IV.) 于1256年致函巴黎，说神学像女主人一样统辖其他学科，其他学科则如仆人般追随她。75

大学拥有自己的管理体制、捐赠财产和特权。这些特权，或称权利宪章，价值重大，使教师与学生团体免受市政当局通常施行的治安监督，其内容包括免税、除紧急情况外免服兵役，以及不必按照通常程序在市政当局面前受审。控告巴黎大学成员的案件，由巴黎主教审理。在博洛尼亚，此类诉讼由被告学生的教授或主教审理。根据腓力·奥古斯都1200年颁布的特权令，巴黎学生的动产不受民政官员扣押。大学是国中之国，是自由的学术共和国。76教师与学生仿佛形成了一个独立阶层。当他们认为自己的权利受到侵害时，便采取所谓停课，即 cessatio，暂停大学的活动，甚至迁往别处。1229年，由于布朗什王后 (Queen Blanche) 迟迟未对狂欢节期间两名学生横死一事给予补救，巴黎大学停课两年。许多教授离开巴黎，以致知名教授无一留存。巴黎主教向主要肇事者施以绝罚；但大学取得了胜利，国王为所造成的伤害致歉，教皇也撤销了教会惩戒。格列高利九世 (Gregory IX.) 于1231年确认了这项停课特权。77这一特色仍存于德国大学，它们坚持 Lehrfreiheit，即教授依良心施教、无须惧怕国家干预的自由。

典范大学。——各大学在管理事务时，以博洛尼亚和巴黎为典范。在博洛尼亚，学生掌握管理权；在巴黎，则由教师与学生共同管理。至于与教皇和教会权威的关系，博洛尼亚与它在法国那所较年轻的姊妹大学相比，始终是自由、反教皇而又反教权的。民主原则得到了广泛认可。首先应注意的，是各学院所起的作用。到十三世纪中叶，巴黎各学院的组织已十分完备。1281年，大学作为一个整体，承诺维护其每一个学院。78早在那以前，各学院便已自行核准学位、规定讲课事宜，并办理其他专门事务。

第二个要素，是所谓民族团体在管理中所起的作用。博洛尼亚有四个民族团体，即意大利人、英格兰人、79普罗旺斯人和德意志人。巴黎的学生也分为四组，分别代表法国、皮卡第（包括尼德兰）、诺曼底和英格兰；最后一组于1430年由德意志取代。巴黎这种独特的组织形式，可以追溯到十三世纪初。80这些民族团体最初是由民族与语言的亲缘关系聚集起来的流动群体，后来发展为各有自身规章的法人组织。各民族团体又划分为若干省区。整个团体由一位选举产生、称为校长的官员领导。在博洛尼亚，早在1194年，他便被称为“各社团的校长”，即 rector societatum。他与代表各省区的参议会共同处理大学事务。

现存文献中，最早称巴黎大学负责人为校长的记载，81见于亚历山大四世1259年的一份诏书；但这一职位无疑早已存在。他由四个民族团体的代理人或主席选出。校长必须是文学硕士，可以是平信徒，但必须独身。他在重大场合主持仪式，身穿醒目的服装。他须向自己所代理的团体负责。巴黎校长被尊称为“宏量阁下”，即 vestra amplitudo。

巴黎还设有教务长，这一职位的历史比校长更为久远。他领导巴黎圣母院 (Notre Dame) 的座堂参事会，既称座堂教务长，也称巴黎教务长。他享有颁发教学许可和授予学位的特权。教皇们一再承认他的权威，又以教皇敕令加以限制，以致他所失去的权力，便成为校长所得的权力。82在博洛尼亚，按照霍诺留三世 (Honorius III.) 1219年的敕令，学位由教区的总执事授予。83

学位。——最迟到1264年，巴黎各学院都已有自己的院长，84并行使颁发本学科教学许可的权利。这种许可，即 jus docendi 或 legendi，若由博洛尼亚或巴黎授予，便附有在各地任教的权利，即 jus ubique docendi。格列高利九世于1233年，以及其他教皇，也曾将同样的特权授予图卢兹 (Toulouse) 及其他大学的硕士；但他们的学位是否受到承认，似乎仍属可疑。即使持有牛津的学位，若不重新接受考试，也无权在巴黎讲学。亚历山大四世授予萨拉曼卡 (Salamanca) 的硕士在各地任教的权利时，明文将博洛尼亚和巴黎排除在外。85

中世纪学位的问题颇多困难。似乎分为三个阶段：学士，即 baccalaureus，获授教学许可者，以及博士或硕士。这与行会中的三个等级相对应：学徒、助手和师傅。学士经过考试而获接纳，并承担辅助性的讲授工作。学位不仅是已完成学业的证明，也是赋予持有人从事讲读或教学这一行业的证书。硕士（magister）、博士（dominus）和教授（scholasticus）这些称号，意义相同。“博士”是博洛尼亚通常使用的称号，“硕士”则通用于巴黎；但渐渐地，在巴黎，“博士”主要用来称教会法毕业生，“硕士”则用于神学毕业生。86腓特烈在1224年的特许状中提到“各学院的博士和硕士”，无疑是将两词作为同义词使用。学位考试称为“裁定”，即 determinance，其主要部分是提出一篇论题，并对所有前来质辩者作出答辩。

罗贝尔·德·库尔松 (Robert de Courcon) 于1215年规定，攻读神学博士的预备期为八年；但到十四世纪初，这一期限延长为十四年。法学须修习八年，医学则须修习六年。

教师与学业。——教学起初在修道院和私人住所进行。1253年，巴黎有十二位神学教授，其中九位在修道院授课，并属于修会。大学校舍的建设进展缓慢；像约翰斯·霍普金斯大学 (Johns Hopkins)、康奈尔大学 (Cornell) 和芝加哥大学 (University of Chicago) 这样，从创办伊始便拥有巨额捐赠和校舍、设备齐全的大学，在中世纪是闻所未闻的。教授和学生必须自谋出路，起初国王或市政当局都不提供薪俸。教授靠听课费和富有学生的馈赠维生。后来才有捐赠基金，城市也拨款支付薪俸。87学院最初是助学宿舍或寄宿所，学生共同居住，并获免费供养。88这类获捐助的机构中，最早而至今仍存的，是罗贝尔·德·索邦 (Robert of Sorbon) 于1257年在巴黎创办的索邦学院 (Sorbonne)，供十六名非修会学生居住，每个民族团体四名。“非修会”一词，是相对于修道院而言。另一所著名学院，是美男子腓力的王后、纳瓦拉的让娜 (Jeanne of Navarre) 于1304年在圣热纳维耶芙 (St. Genevieve) 创办的纳瓦拉学院。拉什达尔 (Rashdall)，I. 478–517，列出了1500年以前在巴黎创办的六十多所学院或助学宿舍。学院原是贫困学生的住所，后来也容纳教师，牛津和剑桥便是如此。在博洛尼亚，学院制度始终没有发展到巴黎和英格兰那样的程度。

除极少数例外，各学院的教师都是教士；即使是平信徒，也未曾婚娶。约翰二十二世 (John XXII.) 于1331年曾特别准许一名已婚男子在巴黎教授医学，但这只是例外。直到1452年，巴黎医学院才放宽独身要求；海德堡则到1479年才如此；巴黎和博洛尼亚的法学教授，直到更晚才获免受这一限制。宗教改革立即使受新教影响的大学发生了改变。89

讲课使用拉丁文，学生和教师交谈时也必须使用拉丁文。任何一种学问都被视为过于神圣，不宜用欧洲的通俗方言来传授。901215年，教皇使节罗贝尔·德·库尔松以权威规定了巴黎大学的学习课程。91格列高利九世也于1231年参与规定应当采用哪些教科书。古典文学在其中并无地位。亚里士多德的某些著作遭到禁止，后来贝纳的阿莫里 (Amauri of Bena)、迪南的大卫 (David of Dinant)，以及其他被认为是或确为异端者的著作，也遭到禁止。格列高利九世告诫神学生，不要醉心哲学，而应以成为“受神教导的人”（theodocts）为满足。92

学生人数与纪律。——中世纪大学的学生人数一直是争论颇多的问题。有些数字大得令人难以置信。93最早时期没有留下注册簿；直到十四世纪末，我们才有巴黎实际毕业人数的记录。十三世纪作家奥德弗里杜斯 (Odefridus) 说，在他以前两代，博洛尼亚有学生10,000人。据传，巴黎曾有学生25,000人，牛津多达30,000人；94若依威克里夫 (Wyclif) 所说，牛津一度甚至有60,000人。不过，威克里夫谈到自己所处的时代时，给出的数字较为合理，为3,000人。阿伯拉尔 (Abaelard) 在声名尚未蒙尘之时，曾向3,000名听众讲学；考虑到他个人的巨大吸引力，这一数字似乎并不夸张。作任何估计时都须记住，学生群体中既有少年，也有年岁颇长的人。拉什达尔认为，牛津学生人数的最高规模在1,500至3,000人之间。95

十三世纪并不存在我们所理解的大学纪律。各种见证一致表明，学生生活放纵。96他们中许多人不过是少年，在文学院就读。当时没有学生宿舍，而当时所能利用的通讯手段，也使父母无法像今天这样约束离家的儿子。卒于1614年的费利克斯·普拉特 (Felix Platter) 在自传中说，即使到了十六世纪中叶，从巴塞尔 (Basel) 到蒙彼利埃 (Montpellier) 的学校，仍需走二十天。在巴黎，学生因来自遥远之地而获免缴费用；路途常需数月，并有遭遇强盗的危险。971218年，巴黎有人控诉学生闯入民宅，掳走少女和妇女；1269年，一份公告谴责成群学生闯入民宅、强奸妇女、抢劫财物，并犯下“许多其他上帝所憎恶的暴行”。98巴黎的酒馆——tabernae——为数甚多。英格兰学生以酗酒著称，“饮酒如英格兰人，歌唱如诺曼人”成了谚语。99决斗是解决争端的常见办法；格列高利九世于1231年禁止学生携带武器穿行街道。100

腓特烈·巴巴罗萨 (Frederick Barbarossa) 于1158年颁给博洛尼亚的敕书，将学生描绘为“因热爱学问而自我放逐，在贫困中耗尽精力”的人。事实并不支持任何关于社会平等的美好图景，如我们对理想民主社会所期望的那样。因喜爱冒险和新奇而被吸引到大学的人，数目必定不少。贵族有自己的专用住处和仆人，贫困学生却要乞讨度日。牛津教务长惯常给贫困学生颁发乞讨许可。101在博洛尼亚，富人占据前排座位。102罗贝尔·德·库尔松称许将衣物及其他物品赠给贫困学子的善举。

中世纪大学是年轻一代理想与希望的中心。后世教会运动与思想运动的种子，以及那些被保守群体斥为学术新说和教义异端的革命，其种子都在那里播下。

一位中世纪作家宣称，维系大公信仰的三股主要力量，是司祭职、帝国和大学。但事实并非总是如此。对教皇专制理论最猛烈的一些攻击，就发自巴黎；一个世纪以后，改革者热尔松 (Gerson) 和达伊 (D’Ailly) 也从那里兴起。胡斯派运动孕育于布拉格 (Prague)。威克里夫的教导使牛津成为异端的据点。维滕贝格 (Wittenberg) 是最后一所开办的中世纪大学，它保护并追随路德。庇护二世 (Pius II.) 所创办的巴塞尔大学，以及海德堡、牛津、剑桥、圣安德鲁斯 (St. Andrews) 等大学，都成了新思想的堡垒。另一方面，索邦、鲁汶 (Louvaine) 和科隆 (Cologne) 则下令焚烧路德的著作。作为促进文化与人类不断进步的机构，中世纪对近代所作的贡献，没有一项能在效用上与大学相比。

博洛尼亚大学 (Bologna) 是欧洲最古老而可敬的大学。萨莱诺 (Salerno) 的创立时间比它更早，作为医学校曾享有盛名，以致彼特拉克 (Petrarch) 称赞它为医学的源泉——fons medicinae；但它的历史只延续了两个世纪。103萨莱诺的起源已湮没不明。似乎没有充分证据表明，这所学校起源于八十英里外的卡西诺山修道院 (Monte Cassino)。它是在意大利南部医学兴趣复苏的过程中产生的，希腊和阿拉伯的影响在其中也起了作用。

1888年，博洛尼亚庆祝了建校八百周年，至今仍是南欧最兴盛的学府之一。早在十三世纪，就流传着狄奥多西二世 (Theodosius II.) 于433年赐予它特许状的传说。然而，它的开端最早只能追溯到十一世纪后期或十二世纪初年。当时，约于1130年去世的伊尔内里乌斯 (Irnerius) 正在讲授查士丁尼法典；104稍后，卡马尔多利会 (Camaldulensian) 修士格拉提安 (Gratian) 在圣斐利克斯修道院 (St. Felix) 讲授教会法。这两位分别精通世俗法和教会法的法学大师，被视为这所大学的创始人。

博洛尼亚成为欧洲研究这两种法律的首要学府。1221年增设的文学院、1260年增设的医学院，以及1360年依英诺森六世 (Innocent VI.) 的诏书设立的神学院，其地位始终不及法学院。

1155年，腓特烈·巴巴罗萨 (Frederick Barbarossa) 到访该城时，承认了这所大学；1158年，又在龙卡利亚 (Roncaglia) 原野颁给它第一份特许状。105这是现存最古老的大学立法文书。从此，博洛尼亚成为第二个、且更为出色的贝里图斯 (Berytus)，是法学的养育者，legum nutrix，并采用了自豪的格言：Bononia docet——“博洛尼亚施教。”它很少、甚或完全不曾受惠于教皇的庇护；在这一点以及其他方面，它的历史与巴黎大学并不相同。106学生们从西欧各地成百上千地涌来。

掌握管理权的学生团体起初分为四个“大学”，即行会。德意志“同乡会”的章程保存至今，其中宣称其宗旨为：弟兄之爱、彼此交往、照顾病人、救济贫困者、办理丧事、平息争端，以及为即将参加博士学位考试的学生提供适当的陪送。107到十四世纪，四个“大学”已由两个团体取代，称为山外团体 (Ultramontanes) 和山内团体 (Cismontanes)；每个团体又分为若干较小的团体，由称为conciliarii的议事员主持。

各学部的学监经选举产生，任期两年，必须是不属修会的教士，未婚，并穿着教士服。就职仪式包括为他们戴上兜帽。十四世纪中叶以后，两个法科“大学”的两位学监改由一位学监取代。

教授们向学生团体宣誓，遵守其规章。如要离开职守，必须向学监请假。他们须随钟声准时开始和结束讲课，否则罚款；不得跳过教科书的任何部分，也不得把问题的解答推延到授课时段结束时。另一项规定要求他们在规定时间内讲完一定的内容。108学生团体以不满时便迁往别处相威胁，使教授们心存畏惧。这种抵制曾实行多次，例如博洛尼亚学生于1204年离去，迁往维琴察 (Vicenza)，1222年迁往帕多瓦 (Padua)，最后一次则于1321年迁往锡耶纳 (Siena)。

教授起初依靠学费维生，有时因学生未能缴清费用而停止授课。博洛尼亚的法学家奥德弗里杜斯 (Odefridus) 曾有一次宣布，下一学期下午不再讲课，因为“学生只想受益，却不肯付费”。教授的聘任权起初掌握在学生团体手中，后来则成为市政当局的特权。这一变化，部分是由于市政府承担了支付固定薪俸的义务。109说来奇怪，约在十三世纪中叶，博洛尼亚的教授职位大半成了世袭职位。

博洛尼亚一个引人注目、但并非独有的特点，是接纳有学问的女性担任教职。著名法学家乔瓦尼·丹德烈亚 (Giovanni d’Andrea) 的女儿诺韦拉·丹德烈亚 (Novella d’Andrea，1312–1366年) 讲授哲学和法律，却隔着帘幕授课，免得她的容貌分散学生对学业的注意力。其他女教授包括：1778年去世的劳拉·巴西 (Laura Bassi)，哲学和数学博士兼教授；1794–1817年讲解希腊古典著作的克洛蒂尔达·坦布罗尼 (Chlotilda Tambroni)；以及直到几年前仍在讲授病理学的朱塞皮娜·卡塔尼 (Giuseppina Cattani)。在萨莱诺，也有妇女行医和讲学，如约1059年的特罗图拉 (Trotula)，她曾撰写关于妇女疾病的著作。德尼夫勒提醒我们，十一世纪后期，巴黎有一位名叫曼戈尔德 (Mangold) 的人，他的女儿们也曾讲授神学。110

另一方面，人们也充分留意保护博洛尼亚的学生，免受妇女的诱惑。阿尔沃诺斯枢机 (Cardinal Albornoz) 于1367年为西班牙学生设立的学院，其章程禁止学生跳舞，因为“魔鬼容易通过这种娱乐引诱人作恶”；又禁止妇女“进入院内，因为女人是罪恶之首、魔鬼的右手，也是人类被逐出乐园的原因”。111

在博洛尼亚，世俗法毕业生须学习七年，教会法毕业生须学习六年。要取得两法博士，即utriusque juris学位，则规定须学习十年。1292年，尼古拉四世 (Nicholas IV.) 正式授予博洛尼亚博士在各处讲学的权利；这项权利，他们此前已在行使。晋授博士学位伴随着盛大的仪式，候选人须为馈赠和宴席花费巨款。112

博洛尼亚的教会法和世俗法课堂，成了传统见解的代名词。独创性得不到鼓励。教授解释完传承下来的教科书，其工作便告结束。但丁 (Dante) 抱怨博洛尼亚只研究教令集时，心中所指的也许正是这种保守主义。罗杰·培根 (Roger Bacon) 则疾呼：“四十年来，法学研究毁坏了智慧的研究［即哲学、各门科学及神学］，是的，甚至毁坏了教会本身及其所有部门。”113文艺复兴来临时，它并非发端于博洛尼亚或意大利其他任何大学，而是兴起于诸侯和教皇的宫廷，尤其是佛罗伦萨 (Florence) 城。大学没有培养出萨沃纳罗拉 (Savonarola)，也没有鼓励任何宗教或教义改革。

附注——关于1888年博洛尼亚八百周年盛大庆典的记述，见菲利普·沙弗 (Philip Schaff)：《大学》等，载《文学与诗歌》(Lit. and Poetry)，第265–278页。沙弗博士当时代表纽约大学出席。意大利国王翁贝托 (Humbert) 和王后的莅临，为庆典增添了光彩。命途多舛的德国皇帝腓特烈三世 (Frederick III.)，作为某种意义上腓特烈·巴巴罗萨的继承人，从病榻上发来了贺信。教士们的缺席在庆典中格外显眼，尽管来宾中有加瓦齐神父 (Father Gavazzi)。他原为巴拿巴会 (Barnabite) 修士，1848年曾为意大利的自由与统一事业，激起同胞博洛尼亚人的热情，后来又在国内外成为祖国新福音运动雄辩的倡导者。与此形成对照的是1886年海德堡大学五百周年纪念，沙弗博士也曾出席；那次庆典以庄严的宗教礼拜和讲道开幕。

早在十三世纪初年，巴黎大学的光辉便已遍及整个西欧。直到宗教改革时期，它一直是神学和一般学术的首要中心。1231年，贵格利九世 (Gregory IX.) 称巴黎为“诸学之母，另一个基列·西弗 (Kerieth Sepher)，一座学术之城；在那里，如同在智慧的工场中，智慧的宝石与黄金为基督的教会而被加工、琢磨”。1141256年，亚历山大四世 (Alexander IV.) 也以同样的语调颂扬这所大学，115称它为“最卓越的学术之邦，著名的文艺之城，杰出的博学之校，最高的智慧工场——officina sapientiae——以及最有成效的学问训练场。在那里，诸学的清泉涌流，万国之民都来饮用”。三百年后，即1518年，路德 (Luther) 在向卡耶坦 (Cajetan) 提出的抗议中，表示愿意将自己的案件交由巴黎大学审理，称它“为学问之母，自古以来始终是最具基督教精神的大学，尤其潜心于神学”。

法国人的自尊，使他们迟迟不肯放弃那种将大学起源追溯至查理曼 (Charlemagne) 的古老传统。杜布莱 (Du Boulay) 用了整整一卷，叙述这所大学在1000年以前的假定历史。甚至阿伯拉尔 (Abaelard) 也不是它的创办人。至多只能说，这位才华横溢的教师为这个新机构预备了道路；116它的开端应归于1150–1170年间。

这所大学自发展初期起，就得到王室的认可和教皇的眷顾；教皇们很快便看出了它未来的重要性。1200年，法国国王腓力·奥古斯都 (Philip Augustus) 授予它一项宝贵的特权，使学生和教师团体享有独立于市政当局的权利。其古老文献中，有英诺森三世 (Innocent III.)、他的使节库尔松的罗伯特 (Robert of Courçon)、洪诺留三世 (Honorius III.) 以及贵格利九世于1231年发出的函件。自那时起，档案中便有大量教皇书信，以及大学当局呈交教皇的函件。

如前所述，在巴黎，教师是掌握管理权的团体。“教师与学生的大学”这一用语，首次出现于1221年。117最早的章程见于英诺森三世约1209年颁布的一道诏书。118后来，英诺森准许该团体在罗马设立代表，并命令重新接纳一位被开除的教师，从而承认了它的法人团体权利。库尔松的罗伯特于1215年制定的章程，规定了教科书及其他规章。大学早在1221年就已使用印章。119大学与巴黎主教座堂的教务长及其他教会当局之间的争端，最早可追溯至1213年。

关于大学组织最初以何种形式为基础，向来意见纷纭。现代最权威的学者德尼夫勒 (Denifle) 反对杜布莱的说法，坚持认为其基础是四个学院，而不是“同乡会”；他认为，十三世纪最初几年，各学院就已形成。120早在1170年，已有某种教师团体存在；约在此时，后来于1195–1214年任圣奥尔本斯 (St. Albans) 修道院院长的塞勒的约翰 (John of Celle)，获准加入该团体。1211207年，英诺森三世提到“教师团体”；1213年，他又承认教师有权坚持要求向其所推荐的候选人颁发教学许可。在此事上，教务长没有选择的余地。122十三世纪中叶，一些教师迁往塞纳河 (Seine) 西岸的圣热纳维耶芙山 (St. Genevieve)，教务长的权力因而受到进一步削减。圣热纳维耶芙修道院院长自1255年起开始被称为“圣热纳维耶芙教务长”，并取得颁发教学许可或学位的权利；这一权利得到了教皇谕令的认可。123

四个同乡会似乎源于这样的需要：管理来自相近地区的学生，并在面对世俗当局时彼此保护。博洛尼亚 (Bologna) 的先例很可能对巴黎产生了某种影响。

贵格利九世于1231年颁布的《诸学之母》诏书——parens scientiarum，被德尼夫勒称为“大学的大宪章”——承认并确认了大学的自由权。这道诏书缘于一场已持续两年的停课。事端起于一家客店中的争吵，后来演变为学生与市民之间的斗殴。市警在布朗什王后 (Queen Blanche) 的同意下介入，杀死了几名学生。教授们下令“停课”，见公道未获伸张，便宣布大学停办六年。其中一些人移居英国，受聘于牛津和剑桥。124另一些人则在法国其他学校安顿下来。贵格利九世最终平息了这场风波；他确认教师有集体迁离的权利，要求布朗什惩处肇事官员，禁止教务长设立任何监狱，也禁止主教向学生处以罚金或将他们监禁。

校长一职可能早在1200年就已存在，当时有一位官员被称为“巴黎学人的首领”。125早在1245年，这一称号便已明确出现，而且校长已与同乡会会长有所区别。126后来，与大学通信时，向“校长与诸教师”致意便成为合宜的惯例。校长与巴黎主教、教务长等其他高级显贵之间的位次问题，引起了许多争执与倾轧。杜布莱本人曾任校长，他颇为自豪地列举了一些事例，说明在公共典礼上，校长的位次高于大主教、枢机、教皇使节、法国贵族，以及其他地位较低的名流。127

各学院后来由院长主持，各同乡会则由会长主持。在处理大学的一般事务时，按学院投票。

大学早期所享有的自由，在十五世纪后半叶受到路易十一 (Louis XI.) 及其继承者的大幅削减。大学因试图干涉不属于自身职权的事务而遭到严厉斥责。停课权被撤销，自由选举校长的权利也被剥夺。128市警获得了更大的管辖权，君主的意志成了具有支配作用的因素。

巴黎大学的声誉来自其文学院和神学院。索邦学院 (Sorbonne) 起初是资助贫困神学生的机构，后来神学部便以它命名。它由圣路易 (St. Louis) 的宫廷司铎索邦的罗伯特 (Robert of Sorbon) 创办，国王亲自赠予了部分建院用地。随着时间推移，它的厅堂开始用作辩论场所，教师团体的裁决也获得了全欧洲的声望。年满二十五岁、修业六年并通过考试的神学生，有资格获授学士许可。在最初三年，他们讲解圣经，随后讲解伦巴德 (Lombard) 的《箴言集》(Sentences)。这两类讲师分别称为 Biblici（圣经讲师）和 Sententiarii（《箴言集》讲师）。取得博士学位的年龄下限是三十五岁。

大学史上最引人入胜的篇章之一，是十三世纪中叶围绕接纳托钵修士而发生的斗争。教皇权为修士们赢得了胜利。大学虽不情愿，却不得不承认他们是其教学人员的一部分。

这场斗争最初爆发于1229年“停课”期间；当时，多明我会士似乎暗中支持世俗官员，反对大学当局，并在宫廷中挑拨对大学的敌意。多明我会于1217年在巴黎设立会院，方济各会则于1220年设立；两会都持有洪诺留三世的荐书，起初颇受欢迎，教师们自己在1254年的一份文件中就是这样说的。129然而，他们不久便开始显露傲慢，要求其学生享有取得学位的权利，却不肯承诺服从大学章程。最早在大学任教的两位多明我会教师中，有一位是英国人贾尔斯的约翰 (John of Giles)。约翰在圣雅各堂 (St. Jacques) 讲论贫穷之后，走下讲坛，穿上了多明我会的会服。

1251年“停课”时，获大学承认为教师的两名多明我会士和一名方济各会士，拒绝与其他主管人员采取一致行动；风波平息后，那两名多明我会士未获重新接纳。大学通过一项章程，禁止接纳拒绝宣誓遵守校规的人加入大学共同体——consortium。修士们拒绝服从这项章程，并从亚历山大四世那里取得一道命令，要求大学接纳他们，同时撤销一切针对他们的处分。130

摩擦仍在继续，世俗教师试图通过揭露弗洛雷的约阿希姆 (Joachim of Flore) 的异端来削弱方济各会士的影响。修士们则以攻击圣阿穆尔的威廉 (William of St. Amour) 作为反击；威廉的《末世的危险》(The Perils of the Last Times) 猛烈抨击托钵制度，认为它违反使徒的教训。威廉的书引出了托马斯·阿奎那 (Thomas Aquinas) 和波拿文都拉 (Bonaventura) 的反驳，随后被焚毁；作者因拒绝撤回主张而被停止教学，并逐出法国。131修士们在街头遭到嘘骂和殴打。据亚历山大四世向我们保证，到1257年，平静已经恢复。大学自古以来就乐于接受教皇权的指导，而教皇权对它的回报，竟是剥夺这所学府的自由。132

自十四世纪中叶起，巴黎大学在法国政治事务中发挥了举足轻重的作用。它不止一次在宫廷和王国贵族面前发言，也不止一次因其不请自来的热忱而受到斥责。133法国国王们自己称它为“国王的女儿”。它曾积极热衷于迫害贞德 (Joan of Arc)。134

作为欧洲宗教史上的一股力量，大学在西方教会大分裂期间——1378–1418年——表现得最为突出。它提出了弥合分裂的三种途径，并为实现这一结果，派遣代表遍访西欧，与国王及其他掌权者磋商。在其教务长热尔松 (Gerson) 和达伊 (D’Ailly) 的引导下，比萨 (Pisa) 和康斯坦茨 (Constance) 两次改革会议的讨论得以展开，并终结了教皇分裂。康斯坦茨会议按民族投票，是大学的一次胜利。

至于纯属教义问题的争论，大学曾反对约翰二十二世 (John XXII.)，以及他那否认人死时即可享有荣福直观的异端。1497年，大学要求所有学位候选人宣誓接受圣母无原罪始胎的教义。新教宗教改革兴起时，大学判定反对这一运动，并下令焚烧路德的著作。

作为哲学和神学的学府，牛津大学 (University of Oxford) 的历史和重要性仅次于巴黎大学；传统将它的创建追溯到阿尔弗雷德王 (King Alfred)，这是错误的。关于奥克森福德 (Oxenford)，即牛津，最早的历史记载见于912年。城中先后设立了三所宗教机构，学府可能起源于其中任何一所，也可能由三者共同孕育而成：圣弗里德斯维德修院 (St. Frideswyde)、奥斯尼修道院 (Osseney)，以及城堡内圣乔治律修会教士的教堂。通常接受的看法将其起源与第一所联系起来。不过，这所大学也可能真正始于1167年巴黎师生的迁入。这一看法的依据，是索尔兹伯里的约翰 (John of Salisbury) 所说法国驱逐了外籍学者，以及亨利二世 (Henry II.) 颁令禁止教士未经司法长官许可前往欧洲大陆或从那里返回。135然而，在此以前，牛津已有教学活动。

最早的一位教师是蒂博·德唐普 (Thibaut d’Estampes，Theobaldus Stampensis)，他从卡昂 (Caen) 的圣司提反修道院迁来，于1117至1121年间在牛津授课。他的学校有六十至一百名学生，他自称牛津教师，magister oxenfordiae. 一位修士嘲笑他为“微不足道的小教士”——tantillus clericellus，说他属于那些“留着尖胡须、卷着头发、衣着女性化的游荡随行教士，他们以合宜的教士服装和剃顶礼为耻”；又指责他“埋首于世俗文学”。

剑桥大学 (University of Cambridge) 首次明确见于记载是在1209年，136但直到十五世纪才取得相当的地位，在此以前也没有什么杰出的教师可称道。教廷最早的承认，见于格列高利九世 (Gregory IX.) 1233年的诏书，其中提到一位校监。137

宗教改革时期，剑桥的声望和影响与牛津不相上下。罗切斯特主教费舍尔 (Fisher) 是最慷慨的学术赞助者之一，后来遭亨利八世 (Henry VIII.) 杀害而殉道；他曾促成两所学院的创立，即1505年的基督学院和1511年的圣约翰学院。剑桥的教师中有伊拉斯谟 (Erasmus)，后来还有欧洲大陆的改革家布策 (Bucer) 和法吉乌斯 (Fagius)。第一部印行的英文《新约》译者丁道尔 (Tyndale)，以及托马斯·比尔尼 (Thomas Bilney)，都是这里的学生，两人后来均殉道。三位殉道者克兰麦 (Cranmer)、拉蒂默 (Latimer) 和里德利 (Ridley) 也曾在此求学，虽然他们是在牛津被烧死的。伊丽莎白时代，这所大学成为清教主义的堡垒，卡特赖特 (Cartwright) 和特拉弗斯 (Travers) 都在此担任教授。卡德沃思 (Cudworth) 和新柏拉图主义者也曾在这里盛极一时。近些年来，这里的教席则由历史学派和释经学派的代表人物担任，如莱特富特主教 (Bishop Lightfoot)、继他任达勒姆主教的韦斯科特 (Westcott)、格洛斯特与布里斯托尔主教埃利科特 (Ellicott)，以及约翰·安东尼·霍特 (John Anthony Hort)。

牛津和剑桥与欧洲大陆各大学的不同之处，在于它们重视本科教育，实行学院和学舍制度，而且与教会保持着更为紧密、关乎自身生命的联系。

倘若采信坎特伯雷的杰维斯 (Gervaise of Canterbury) 那不甚可靠的证言，意大利人瓦卡里乌斯 (Vacarius) 于1149年将民法研究引入牛津；不过，更可能的情况是，他在坎特伯雷大主教西奥博尔德 (Theobald) 的府中讲学。138据说，他编写了一部法律汇编，“足以解决学校中惯常讨论的一切法律问题”。

关于牛津作为求学之地的最早记载之一，见于威尔士旅行家兼历史学家吉拉尔杜斯·坎布伦西斯 (Giraldus Cambrensis) 的叙述。约在1185年，他到访该城，并“在各学部、博士和学生面前”宣读了自己的《爱尔兰地志》(Topography of Ireland)。139这所学府能吸引这样一位人物，显然已经颇具重要性。吉拉尔杜斯称牛津的会吏长沃尔特·马普 (Walter Map) 为“一位牛津教师”。已知最早授出的学位，是颁给后来成为坎特伯雷大主教的埃德蒙·里奇 (Edmund Rich) 的。从吉拉尔杜斯的记述可以清楚看出，教师们已按学部分组。早在1209年，由于市长绞死了三名学生，教师和学生遂大批迁出，据说共有三千人，剑桥大学即由此发端。140

牛津大学所受教会权威的约束，比巴黎大学要少。牛津所在教区的林肯主教曾试图确立对大学的监督权，却未能成功。英诺森四世 (Innocent IV.) 于1254年颁布的诏书，是最接近教皇特许状的文件，确认大学享有其“豁免权和古老惯例”。校监一职首次见于1201年的记载。这一职务似乎从一开始就是由大学选举产生的，起初任期为两年。如今，校监是一个荣誉性的显职，任职者并不打算常住牛津。

1395年，教皇诏书豁免大学，不再受主教或当然教皇使节（legati nati）的任何管辖。由于威克里夫 (Wyclif) 及其追随者引起的纷争，这项法令于1411年被撤销；但1490年，西克斯图斯四世 (Sixtus IV.) 又恢复了大学不受教会权威管辖的豁免权。

大学与市民及市政当局不断发生冲突。其中最著名的一次发生于1354年。照例，事端起于酒馆中的斗殴，店主不仅得到同城居民的支持，还有邻近乡间数千人前来助阵。141校监逃离了城镇。修士们取出圣饼，置于交战双方之间，却被打落在地；一名学生紧抱着手持圣饼的修士，仍遭杀害。流血甚多。市民一心要清算旧账，闯入二十所学院寄宿舍和学舍，大肆抢掠。他们甚至不尊重教堂的神圣性，躲入其中寻求庇护的学者也遭追捕杀害。学生们离开了城市。校监向国王申诉，借助王权和主教的属灵权威，迫使市政团体作出赔偿。该城被施以一年的禁令。官员受到惩罚，学生的财物也得到赔偿。解除禁令的条件是：市长、执达吏和六十名市民，须在骚乱爆发的周年纪念日，即圣斯科拉斯蒂卡日，到圣马利亚教堂，为被杀的学生行补赎礼；每名市民在主祭坛上献一便士，所得款项由贫困学生与堂区牧师平分。直到1825年，大学才同意取消这种已延续近五百年的年度补赎仪式。大学经过数年才恢复旧观。142学生之间也并非总是相安无事。1413年，议会通过法案，以滋事为由驱逐了爱尔兰学生群体。143

正如其他地方已经说过的，多明我会和方济各会的到来，是牛津一件极为引人注目的大事，但它们从未取得在巴黎所达到的独立权势。此后，加尔默罗会、奥古斯丁会及其他修会也相继而来。

下一件重要事件，是围绕威克里夫以及罗拉德派 (Lollards) 的教义和成员所展开的争论；这场争论贯穿十四世纪最后二十五年，并延续至其后。

学院制度在英国大学中得到了持久的发展。主教、国王及其他人士慷慨捐赠所设立的基金，为由教师和学生组成的法人团体及学舍提供了核心，各自都有多少独具特色的生活。这些学院团体及其建筑，至今仍赋予牛津和剑桥以中世纪的风貌，处处唤起对往昔世纪的庄严追忆。牛津现存此类学院二十一所，学舍五所。其中最古老的有：大学学院 (University College)，由达勒姆的威廉 (William of Durham) 于1249年去世时所留遗赠创立；默顿学院 (Merton)，1264年；贝利奥尔学院 (Balliol)，1266年，由那位苏格兰国王的父亲创立；埃克塞特学院 (Exeter)，1314年；奥里尔学院 (Oriel)，1324年；王后学院 (Queen’s College)，1340年；著名的新学院 (New College)，1379年，由温彻斯特主教威廉·威克姆 (William of Wykenham) 创立；万灵学院 (All Souls)，1438年；莫德林学院 (Magdalen)，1448年，沃尔西 (Wolsey) 曾任该院院士。早期在牛津任教的杰出人物包括埃德蒙·里奇、罗杰·培根 (Roger Bacon)、格罗斯泰斯特 (Grosseteste)、亚当·马什 (Adam Marsh)、邓斯·司各脱 (Duns Scotus)、奥卡姆 (Ockam)、布拉德沃丁 (Bradwardine)、阿马的理查德 (Richard of Armagh) 和威克里夫。

作为神学教育中心，牛津与英国宗教史上一些最重要的运动有着密切联系。威克里夫在这里宣讲他的教义和实际改革。人文主义者格罗辛 (Grocyn)、科莱特 (Colet) 和利纳克尔 (Linacre) 在这里授课。在宗教改革时期、共和国时期和王政复辟时期，这所学府都是重要的宗教中心。卫斯理兄弟 (Wesleys) 和怀特菲尔德 (Whitefield) 曾在这里求学，循道运动也诞生于此。上世纪上半叶，普西 (Pusey)、基布尔 (Keble) 和纽曼 (Newman) 在这里施展其摄人心魄的影响，《书册》运动 (Tractarian movement) 也在这里发起并得到培育。自1854年起，牛津和剑桥向不从国教者开放。1871年，一切宗教资格审查均被废除。1885年，清教徒的属灵后裔独立派 (Independents) 在牛津设立曼斯菲尔德学院 (Mansfield College)，以培养牧师。

中世纪的主教座堂是宗教赞美与虔敬的表现，完全是教会的产物。其建造没有掺入任何其他因素。它们是石头写成的赞美诗；在中世纪留给后世的贡献中，除大学以外，最为宏伟、最有裨益的便是这些建筑。十字军的军旅未能实现其征服的企图；留在本土的建筑者却造出了此后历代滋养人们虔敬、激起人们赞叹的建筑。它们的出现并非归功于教皇制度，而是出于城市、贵族和人民的虔诚奉献。

从早期基督徒聚集敬拜的 triclinia，即私人住宅中的房间，以及地下墓窟，到圣索菲亚主教座堂 (St. Sophia)，其间有了显著的进步；据说查士丁尼 (Justinian) 在该堂落成时曾高呼：“所罗门啊，我超过你了。”从中欧和北欧用于敬拜的茅屋和粗陋庙宇，到奉献给基督教敬拜的宏伟建筑，这又是何等的变化！正是坎特伯雷的奥古斯丁 (Augustine of Canterbury)、博尼法斯 (Boniface) 和圣安斯加尔 (St. Ansgar)，将这种敬拜传给了北方的蛮族。

中世纪的伟大建筑不是宫殿，也不是商业用房，这也是它的一个特点，尽管威尼斯 (Venice) 哥特式的总督宫，以及布鲁塞尔 (Brussels)、鲁汶 (Louvaine) 和比利时、荷兰其他城市的市政厅，也都规模宏大、气势非凡。这些伟大的建筑，无论是主教座堂还是修道院建筑，都是宗教建筑。它们往往像在法国那样，坐落在高地或城市中央，民居环绕其周围，仿佛在寻求庇护。

宏伟的主教座堂成了每日的讲道，见证上帝的临在和基督的复活。对人民而言，它们如同一部圣经，人们可以用眼睛看见其中的基本教训。望见它们的墙垣和高耸的尖塔，人们的思想便被提升，转向属灵的事。阳光透过彩色玻璃窗，使宽阔的内部空间或明亮、或幽暗，令他们想起来世生命的荣耀；这荣耀借着种种启示，向尘世孤独而神秘的生活显明自己。坚固的地基、粗大的柱子和扶壁，象征着上帝宝座的稳固，也表明祂以权能的话语创造了万有。

这些建筑的营造历经多年，有的甚至需要几个世纪才能完成。谁能估量，奠下第一块石头时唤起了多少祷告和虔诚奉献，而这些又一直倾注不息，直到最后一层石头砌上塔顶，或嵌入顶饰之中？它们的雕刻、彩色玻璃窗、壁画和绘画，展现圣经与教会历史中的场景。君王、王后、武士，以及当世公认为敬虔的人，都安葬在那里；这种习俗进入近代以后仍然延续，例如路德 (Luther) 和梅兰希通 (Melanchthon) 的遗骸，就安息在维滕贝格 (Wittenberg) 的城堡教堂。尽管火灾频繁，烧毁大教堂的局部乃至整座建筑，人们仍加以修复或重建，而且往往不止一次，如沙特尔 (Chartres)、坎特伯雷 (Canterbury) 和诺里奇 (Norwich) 的主教座堂便是如此。中央塔楼也曾倒塌，如温彻斯特 (Winchester)、彼得伯勒 (Peterborough)、林肯 (Lincoln) 以及英国其他主教座堂的塔楼，但后来都予以重建。诸侯与人民共同参与这些教堂的兴建，为推进这项事业，献出物料和劳力。乌尔姆 (Ulm) 的妇女为促进本城主教座堂的工程，献出了所有饰物；各地的德国人则为科隆 (Cologne) 主教座堂的营建捐献。

十一世纪开启了世界建筑史上最卓著的时期之一，延续近三个世纪。它有自身鲜明的特色，卓越的人才也献身为之效力。可以说，修士们以其建造坚固、宽敞、美丽修道院的热忱，走在了前面。这些建筑激起法国主教们超越它们的雄心。这一时期的建筑通常分为两种风格：罗马式 (Romanesque)，在英国也称诺曼式 (Norman)；以及哥特式 (Gothic)。建筑学作家又作了若干细分，有些人则把十二至十五世纪的所有建筑，都归入哥特式或基督教尖拱式 (Christian Pointed) 之名下。在这几个世纪中，从欧洲南部到遥远的北方苏格兰和瑞典，宏伟建筑星罗棋布；它们一方面足与君士坦丁堡的圣索菲亚大教堂媲美，另一方面，此后虽有人仿效，却无人能够企及。

在罗马，迟至十三世纪霍诺留三世 (Honorius III.) 开始建造圣洛伦佐教堂 (San Lorenzo) 时，古老的巴西利卡式 (basilica) 仍居主导地位。罗马式发源于意大利北部，十一世纪初越过阿尔卑斯山，在山北取得了最辉煌的成就。在意大利，比萨 (Pisa) 主教座堂体现了新旧两式的融合，兼具十字形平面与穹顶。在德国，施派尔 (Spires)、沃尔姆斯 (Worms) 和美因茨 (Mainz) 的主教座堂属于这一时期；在英国，早期的主教座堂或其中一部分也属于这一时期，例如约1070年动工的温彻斯特、约1084年动工的伍斯特 (Worcester)、约1120年动工的彼得伯勒、约1096年动工的诺里奇、约1083年动工的伊利 (Ely)，以及约1099年动工的达勒姆 (Durham)。

巴西利卡的基本平面被十字形所取代。唱诗班席所在的区域扩大，地面也被抬高。这是祭司阶层的时代；祭司主义体现在祭坛的扩大、圣职人员席位的增多及其富丽装饰，以及祭坛后部空间的设置上。这些特征在前一时期也已存在，如今却更为突出。十字形的长端，即中殿，尤其在英国的主教座堂中，大大延长，使人从远处就能看见祭坛及其陈设，因为正门便设在这朝西的一端。在英国，耳堂，即十字形的两臂，变得修长宽敞。塔楼成为显著特征，墙体上也增设扶壁。在意大利，塔楼采取钟楼 (campanile) 的形式，在穹顶之外另行建造，有时是一座独立建筑，而从来不是穹顶建筑不可缺少的部分。拱顶和交叉拱顶取代了平顶。

哥特式因其被认为具有蛮族特色而在意大利得此名称，却完全是在北方达到了最高的发展，其发端之地是法国北部。这是历来形成的最宏伟的教堂建筑风格。它体现在教堂墙壁的高度上，也体现在仿佛奋力要直达上帝宝座的尖塔上。亚眠 (Amiens) 主教座堂的拱顶距地面147英尺，博韦 (Beauvais) 为157英尺，科隆为155英尺。这种风格发展了尖拱、垂直线条和柳叶形窗。它具有伦巴第白杨树的某些特征：高耸、严峻、肃穆。它那如堡垒般坚固的扶壁、塔楼和巨柱，表现了北方森林的坚韧与力量。其尖顶适于承受北方常见的暴风雪。它的飞扶壁、内部精细的雕刻，以及富丽的入口，尤其是法国主教座堂的入口，象征着基督教应许与盼望的丰盛。

哥特式于十三世纪兴起于法国。著名的实例有1211年动工的兰斯 (Rheims) 主教座堂、亚眠、拉昂 (Laon) 的主教座堂，以及1163年动工的巴黎圣母院 (Notre Dame)。其拱形不如英国的尖锐，而门廊的规模却宏大得多，装饰也更为丰富。巴黎圣母院的飞扶壁，是最精美的范例之一。该堂以及法国哥特式建筑的大多数实例，都设有塔楼。巴黎、亚眠和兰斯主教座堂的塔楼尚未完成。巴黎圣礼拜堂 (Sainte Chapelle) 则是纯粹哥特式的辉煌杰作。

在德国，马尔堡 (Marburg) 献给圣伊丽莎白 (St. Elizabeth) 的教堂，以及纽伦堡 (Nürnberg)、班贝格 (Bamberg)、弗赖堡 (Freiburg)、斯特拉斯堡 (Strassburg) 等城的教堂，都是哥特式的优美实例。科隆主教座堂据称是现存最完美的哥特式建筑。其唱诗班席部分于1248年开工，霍赫施塔登的康拉德 (Konrad of Hochsteden) 在新当选的皇帝、荷兰的威廉 (William of Holland) 和众多诸侯在场时奠下基石。唱诗班席部分于1322年举行奉献礼。到1437年，其中一座塔楼已建至现今高度的三分之一。宗教改革时期，屋顶覆盖的是木板。十九世纪发现了原来的设计图，完成整座建筑、包括两座尖塔的工程，遂成为一项全国性的事业。工程于1880年竣工。

英国拥有丰富的中世纪建筑遗存，其兴起始于诺曼人的到来。国家的生活与这些建筑交织在一起，而威斯敏斯特教堂 (Westminster Abbey) 或许是全世界最庄严的安葬之地。除前面提及的主教座堂外，林肯、坎特伯雷、约克 (York)、索尔兹伯里 (Salisbury) 及其他大教堂，也都在这一时期动工。此后不断增建，英国这些庄严的教堂才获得了最终的形态。塔楼是英国主教座堂的显著特征之一；在具有尖塔的教堂中，利奇菲尔德 (Lichfield) 大概最为重要。索尔兹伯里和林肯大教堂的外观最为优美。这些主教座堂有许多是本笃会修士建造的，如坎特伯雷、达勒姆、伊利和诺里奇；另一些则由圣奥古斯丁律修会士建造，如卡莱尔 (Carlisle) 和布里斯托尔 (Bristol)。林肯、奇切斯特 (Chichester)、索尔兹伯里、约克、圣戴维斯 (St. David’s) 等教堂，则由在俗司铎主持。

苏格兰的建筑师似乎来自英国，并依照英国的样式建造。其最宏伟的中世纪教堂有格拉斯哥 (Glasgow)、圣安德鲁斯 (St. Andrews)、邓布兰 (Dumblane) 和埃尔金 (Elgin)；修道院中则有凯尔索 (Kelso)、德赖伯勒 (Dryburgh)、荷里路德 (Holyrood) 和梅尔罗斯 (Melrose)。

在西班牙，托莱多 (Toledo)、布尔戈斯 (Burgos) 等城市的大教堂于十三世纪以哥特式建造；塞维利亚 (Seville) 主教座堂也属同一类型，其地面面积为所有基督教教堂之最，于1401年动工，1520年竣工。

在意大利，哥特式始终未能完全适应当地的环境。米兰 (Milan)、佛罗伦萨 (Florence) 和锡耶纳 (Siena) 的主教座堂，被视为其中较为精美的实例。锡耶纳主教座堂于1243年动工，米兰大教堂则迟至1385年才开始建造。它是仅次于塞维利亚主教座堂和圣彼得大教堂的第三大基督教教堂。其西立面与建筑其余部分并不协调，后者是纯粹的哥特式。整座教堂以白色大理石建成，数百座尖塔直耸云霄。从米兰主教座堂望去，阿尔卑斯山尽收眼底，白雪冠顶，高高超越人间车马与人声的喧嚣；与上帝那些更为雄伟的大教堂相比，人的创造显得渺小，正如人在创造他的主面前显得渺小一样。

§ 88. Schools.

Education and the advance of true religion are inseparable. The history of literary culture in this period is marked by the remarkable awakening which started in Western Europe in the latter part of the eleventh century and the rise of the universities in the twelfth century. The latter was one of the most important events in the progress of the intellectual development of the race. The renaissance of the eleventh century showed itself in a notable revival of interest in schools, in the appearance of eminent teachers, in a renewed study of the classics, and in an enlarged sweep of the human mind.

The municipal schools of the Roman Empire were swept away by the barbarian invasions of the fourth and fifth centuries, and few vestiges of them were left. The weight of opinion in the Church had been hostile to Pagan learning from the time of Tertullian and Jerome and culminated in Justinian’s act, closing the university of Athens. But it is doubtful whether the old Roman schools would have withstood the shock from the assaults of Goth, Vandal, and Hun, even had Church teachers been friendly to classical literature.

The schools of the earlier Middle Ages were associated with the convents and cathedrals, and it was not till the thirteenth century that the municipal school appeared again, and then it was in the far North, in Germany, and the Lowlands. The first name in the history of the new education is Cassian who founded the convent school of St. Victor, Marseilles, 404. But it was to Benedict of Nursia that Western Europe owed the permanent impulse to maintain schools. The Benedictine Rule made education an adjunct of religion, provided for the training of children by members of the order, and for the transcription of manuscripts. To the Benedictines, especially to the Cistercians, are our libraries indebted for the preservation of the works of classical and patristic writers.

The wise policy of Charlemagne in establishing the Palace school, a sort of normal school for the German Empire, and in issuing his Capitularies bearing on education, and the policy of Alfred in England, gave a fresh impulse to learning by the patronage of royalty. Alcuin at the court of Charlemagne, Asser in England, and John Scotus Erigena at the court of Charles the Bold, were some of the more eminent teachers. It is possible the education was not confined to clerics, for convents had two kinds of schools, the one, the interior, for oblates intended for the monastery, and the exterior school which seems to have had a more general character. The cathedral schools had for their primary, if not for their sole purpose, the training of youth for cathedral positions—canonici puri. The main, if not the exclusive, purpose of education was to prepare men for the priesthood and the convent.(1) In the eleventh century all the convents and cathedrals in Germany had schools,(2) — Corvey on the Weser and Hildesheim being noted; and, in Italy, the schools of Milan and Parma were well known.

But in that century the centre of education shifted to France. The schools at Bec, Rheims, Orleans, Laon, and Paris had no rivals and their fame attracted students, even monks, priests, and bishops, from England and Germany.(3) The fame of Rheims, where Gerbert, afterwards Sylvester II., d. 1003, had won the title of "restorer of studies," gave way to the greater fame of Bec, under Lanfranc and Anselm. Students were drawn from afar and, in the judgment of the glowing panegyrist, Ordericus Vitalis, Athens, in its most flourishing period, would have honored Lanfranc in every branch of learning.(4) These two priors were followed by a succession of teachers whom Ordericus calls "careful pilots and skilful charioteers." Seldom has so splendid a compliment been paid a teacher by a man risen to eminence as was paid by Alexander II. to Lanfranc,(5) on Lanfranc’s visit to Rome, after he was made archbishop of Canterbury. Rising to welcome him with open arms, the pope remarked to the bystanders that he received Lanfranc as his teacher, at whose feet he had sat, rather than as archbishop. Guibert of Nogent, who died about 1120, is authority for the statement that teachers were very rare in France in his early years, but, at the time when he was writing, every considerable town in France had a teacher.(6) That mothers were anxious to have their sons educated is evident from the example of Guibert’s statement concerning his own mother.

As in the earlier period of the Middle Ages, so in this middle period, the idea of universal education was not thought of. Nor was there anything such as we call belles lettres and general literature.(7) All literature had an immediate bearing on religious subjects.(8) Such men as Walter Map and John of Salisbury, who approach nearest our modern idea of men of letters, were clerics. The founders of convents, like Herlouin, founder of Bec, were often men who could neither read nor write. Ordericus says that during the reigns of six dukes, before Lanfranc went to Bec, scarce a single Norman devoted himself to studies. Duke William of Aquitaine, d. 1030, however, was educated from childhood and was said to have spent his nights in reading till sleep overcame him, and to have had a collection of books.(9)

The most brilliant teachers of this era were Anselm of Laon, William of Champeaux, Bernard of Chartres, William of Conches, and, above all, Abaelard. They all belonged to France. In their cases, the school followed the teacher and students went not so much to a locality as to an educator. More and more, however, the interest centred in Paris, which had a number of schools,—the Cathedral school, St. Genevieve, St. Victor, St. Denis.(10) Our knowledge of these men is derived chiefly from Abaelard and John of Salisbury. John studied in France for twelve years, 1137–1149, and sat under them all. His descriptions of the studies of the age, and the methods and rivalries of teachers, are given in the Metalogicus.

William of Champeaux, d. 1121, the pupil of Anselm of Laon, won fame at the Cathedral school of Paris, but lost his position by clash with the brilliant abilities of Abaelard. He retired to St. Victor and spent the last eight years of his life in the administration of the see of Chalons. He was an extreme realist.

The teaching of Anselm of Laon and his brother Ralph drew students from as far south as Milan and from Bremen in the North. The brothers were called by John of Salisbury the "splendid luminaries of Gaul,"(11) and "doctor of doctors" was an accepted appellation of Anselm. This teacher, d. 1117, perhaps the pupil of Anselm of Bec, had Abaelard among his hearers and won his contumely. But John of Salisbury’s praise, and not Abaelard’s contempt, must determine our judgment of the man. His glossa interlinearis, a periphrastic commentary on the Vulgate, was held in high esteem for several centuries.(12)

Bernard of Chartres, about 1140, was celebrated by John of Salisbury as the "most overflowing spring of letters in Gaul in recent times" and, the most perfect Platonist of our age."(13) He acknowledged his indebtedness to the ancient writers in these words, "We are as dwarfs mounted on the shoulders of giants, so that we are able to see more and further than they; but this is not on account of any keenness of sight on our part or height of our bodies, but because we are lifted up upon those giant forms. Our age enjoys the gifts of preceding ages, and we know more, not because we excel in talent, but because we use the products of others who have gone before."(14)

William of Conches, d. 1152 (?), got his name from the Norman hamlet in which he was born. Like his teacher, Bernard of Chartres, he laid stress upon a thorough acquaintance with grammar as the foundation of all learning, and John of Salisbury seems to have written the Metalogicus to vindicate the claims his teachers made for the fundamental importance of this study as opposed to dialectics. But he was advocating a losing cause. Scholasticism was crushing out the fresh sprouts of humanism.(15) William of Conches took liberties with received opinions and denied that Eve was literally created from Adam’s rib. The root of his teachings Poole finds in William’s own words, "through knowledge of the creature we attain to the knowledge of the Creator."(16)

The studies continued, at least theoretically, to follow the scheme of the old trivium, including grammar, rhetoric, and dialectic; and the quadrivium, including arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, and music. These branches had a wider scope than we associate with some of the titles. Grammar, for example, with Bernard of Chartres, included much more than technical rules and the fundamental distinctions of words. It took in the tropes and figures of speech, analyzed the author’s body of thought, and brought out the allusions to nature, science, and ethical questions. The teaching extended far beyond the teaching of the Capitularies of Charlemagne. Nevertheless, all these studies were the vestibule of theology and valuable only as an introduction to it. Jacob of Vitry, d. 1244, comparing the seven liberal arts with theology,(17) said, "Logic is good for it teaches us to distinguish truth from falsehood, grammar is good for it teaches how to speak and write correctly; rhetoric is good for it teaches how to speak elegantly and to persuade. Good too are geometry which teaches us how to measure the earth, arithmetic or the art of computing which enables us to estimate the brevity of our days, music which reminds us of the sweet chant of the blessed, astronomy which leads us to consider the heavenly bodies shining resplendently before God. But far better is theology which alone can be called a liberal art, since it alone delivers the human soul from its woes."

Innocent III., through the canons of the Fourth Lateran, ordered all cathedrals to have teachers of grammar and lectors in theology, and offered the rewards of high office only to those who pursued hard study with the sweat of the brow.(18) He had in mind only candidates of theology.

The text-books in use for centuries were still popular, such as Cassiodorus, the Isagoge of Porphyry, Aristotle on the Categories; and his De interpretatione, Boethius on Music and the Consolations of Philosophy, Martianus Capella and the grammars of Priscian and Donatus.(19) A new movement, however, was distinctly perceptible, and nothing is more sure proof of it than the open use of the classics by some of the leading educators in their lectures and their use in the writings of the time.

The condemnation, passed by Jerome on the ancient classics, was adopted by Cassian and handed down to the later generations. The obscurantists had the field with little or few exceptions for centuries. It is not to Alcuin’s credit that, in his latter years, he turned away from Virgil as a collection of "lying fables" and, in a letter to a novice, advised him not to assoil his mind with that poet’s rank luxuriance.(20) It was argued by Leo, in his reply to Arnulf of Orleans, 991, that the Apostle Peter was not acquainted with such writers as Plato, Virgil, and Terence, or any of the pseudo-philosophers, and God had from the beginning not chosen orators and philosophers but ignorant and rustic men as His agents.(21) Peter the Venerable raised his voice against them. But such warnings(22) were not sufficient to induce all men to hold themselves aloof from the fascinations of the Latin writers.

Gerbert taught Virgil, Statius, Terence, Juvenal, Persius, Horace, and Lucan.(23) From these he passed on to the department of philosophy. Peter Damiani compared the study of the poets and philosophers to the spoiling of the Egyptians. They served to sharpen the understanding; the study of the writers of the Church to build a tabernacle to God. Anselm of Bee recommended the study of Virgil and other classics, counselling the exclusion of such treatises as contained suggestions of evil.(24) John of Salisbury’s teachers were zealous in reading such writings. John, who in the small compass of the Metalogicus quotes no less than seven classical poets, Statius, Martian, Virgil, Horace, Ovid, Catullus, and Persius, and some of these a number of times, says that if you search in Virgil and Lucan, you will be sure to find the essence of philosophy, no matter what philosophy you may profess.(25) He complained of the old school who compared the student of the classic poets and historians to the slow-going ass, and laughed at him as duller than a stone.(26) Abaelard gave to Virgil the esteem due a prophet. Peter of Blois, d. 1204, the English archdeacon, quotes Cicero, Sallust, Livy, Curtius, Tacitus, Suetonius, Seneca (Letters), and other writers. Grosseteste was familiar with Ovid, Seneca, Horace, and other classics. But the time for the full Renaissance had not yet come. In the earliest statutes of the University of Paris the classics were excluded from the curriculum of studies. The subtle processes of the Schoolmen, although they did not altogether ignore the classic compositions, could construct the great theological systems without their aid, though they drew largely and confidently upon Aristotle.

The Discipline of the schools was severe. A good flogging was considered a wholesome means of educational advancement. It drove out the evil spirits of intellectual dulness and heaviness. Degere sub virga, to pass under the rod, was another expression for getting an education. At a later date, the ceremony of inducting a schoolmaster included the presentation of a rod and required him, at least in England, to show his prowess by flogging a boy publicly.(27) If the case of Guibert of Nogent was a typical one, then the process of getting an education was indeed a painful piece of physical experience.

Guibert’s account of his experiences is the most elaborate description we have of mediaeval school life, and one of the most interesting pieces of schoolboys’ experience in literature.(28) The child, early sent to school by his widowed mother, was unmercifully beaten with fist and rod by his teacher, a man who had learned grammar in his advanced years. Though the teacher was an indifferent grammarian, Guibert testifies to the vigor of his moral purpose and the wholesome moral impression he made upon his pupils. The whipping came every day. But the child’s ardor for learning did not grow cold. On returning to his home one evening and loosening his shirt, his mother saw the welts and bruises on his shoulders, for he had been beaten black and blue that day;(29) she suggested, in indignation and pity, that her boy give up preparation for the priesthood, and offered to give him the equipment for the career of a knight. But Guibert, greatly excited, resented any such suggestion.

At Cluny the pupils slept near the masters, and if they were obliged to get up at night, it was not till they had the permission of a master. If they committed any offence in singing the Psalms or other songs, in going to bed, or in any other way, they were punished in their shirts, by the prior or other master, with switches prepared beforehand.(30)

But there were not wanting teachers who protested against this method. Anselm urged the way of affection and confidence and urged that a skilful artificer never fashioned his image out of gold plate by blows alone. With wise and gentle hand he pressed it into shape. Ceaseless beating only brutalizes. To an abbot who said "day and night we do not cease to chastise the children confided to our care and yet they grow worse and worse," Anselm replied: "Indeed! And when they are grown up, what will they become? Stupid dolts. A fine education that, which makes brutes of men!... If you were to plant a tree in your garden and were to enclose it on all sides, so that it could not extend its branches, what would you find when, at the end of several years, you set it free from its bounds? A tree whose branches were bent and scraggy, and would it not be your fault for having so unreasonably confined it?"(31)

The principle ruled that an education was free to all whose circumstances did not enable them to pay for it. Others paid their way. Fulbert of Chartres took a fee from the rapidly increasing number of students, regarding philosophy as worth what was paid for it. But this practice was regarded as exceptional and met with opposition.(32) The words of Alcuin, "If you desire to study, you will have what you seek without money," were inscribed on the convent of St. Peter at Salzburg.(33) It was the boast that the care given to the humblest scholar at Cluny was as diligent as the care given to children in the palace.(34)

§ 89. Books and Libraries.

Books and schools go together and both are essential to progress of thought in the Church. The mediaeval catalogue of the convent of Muri asserts strongly the close union of the intellectual and religious life. It becomes us, so it ran, always to copy, adorn, improve, and annotate books, because the life of the spiritual man is nothing without books.(35)

Happy was the convent that possessed a few volumes.(36) The convent and the cathedral were almost the sole receptacles for books. Here they were most safe from the vandalism of invaders and the ravages of fire, so frequent in the Middle Ages; and here they were accessible to the constituency which could read. It was a current saying, first traced to Gottfried, canon of St. Barbe-en-Auge, that a convent without a library is like a fortress without arms.(37) During the early Middle Ages, there were small collections of books at York, Fulda, Monte Cassino, and other monasteries. They were greatly prized, and ecclesiastics made journeys to get them, as did Biscop, abbot of Wearmouth, who made five trips to Italy for that purpose. During the two centuries and more after Gregory VII., the use and the number of books increased; but it remained for the zeal of Petrarch in the fourteenth century to open a new era in the history of libraries. The period of the Renaissance which followed witnessed an unexampled avidity for old manuscripts which the transition of scholars from Constantinople made it possible to satisfy.

To the convents of Western Europe, letters and religion owe a lasting debt, not only for the preservation of books, but for their multiplication. The monks of St. Benedict have the first place as the founders of libraries and guardians of patristic and classical literature. Their Rules required them to do a certain amount of reading each day, and at the beginning of Lent each received a book from the cloistral collection and was expected to read it "straight through." This direction shines as a light down through the history of the monastic institutions, though many a convent probably possessed no books and some of them had little appreciation of their value.

A collection of several hundred books was relatively as large a library as a collection of hundreds of thousands of volumes would be now. Fleury, in the twelfth century, had 238 volumes, St. Riquier 258.(38) The destruction of the English monastery of Croyland in the eleventh century involved the loss of "300 original and more than 400 smaller volumes." The conventual buildings were destroyed in the night by fire. The interesting letter of the abbot Ingulph, relating the calamity, speaks of beautiful manuscripts, illuminated with pictures and adorned with crosses of gold. The good abbot, after describing the loss of the chapel, infirmary, and other parts of the buildings, went on to say "our cellar and the very casks, full of beer, were also burnt up."(39)

Catalogues are preserved from this period. Edwards gives a list of thirty-three mediaeval catalogues of English libraries.(40) The catalogue of Prüfening in Salzburg, 1158, prepared by, "one who was a born librarian,"(41) arranged the volumes in three classes: copies of the Scriptures, the Fathers, and modern writers. The books, most frequently found, were the Bible, or parts of it, the liturgical books,—Augustine, Gregory the Great, Jerome, and Ambrose,—and among the writers of the Carlovingian age, Bede and Alcuin. The catalogue of Corbie, Picardy, dating from the twelfth century, gives 39 copies of Augustine, 16 of Jerome, 13 of Bede, 15 of Boethius, and 5 of Cicero, as well as copies of Terence, Livy, Pliny, and Seneca.(42) Of later mediaeval writers, the works of Anselm, Bernard, Hugo, and Abaelard are found most often, but many collections were without a single recent writer. The otherwise rich collection of St. Michelsberg, in Bamberg, had only a single recent work, the Meditations of Anselm. The Prüfening library had a copy each, of Anselm, Hugo, Abaelard, the Lombard and Gratian. Classical authors were common. The library at Durham had copies of Cicero, Terence, Virgil, Horace, Claudian, Statius, Sallust, Suetonius, Quintilian and other Latin authors.(43) Sometimes the classics were catalogued by themselves as at Neumünster.

Gifts of books were regarded as worthy benefactions. Peter, bishop of Paris, before starting out for the Holy Land, gave 300 works over to the care of the convent of St. Victor.(44) Grosseteste willed his collection to the Oxford Franciscans.(45) Gerbert, afterwards Sylvester II., says that the liberality of friends enabled him to buy a number of books in Rome, Italy, and Flanders.(46) The admiring chronicler treats it as a claim to fame, that Theodoric secured, for his abbey of St. Evroult, the books of the Old and New Testaments and an entire set of Gregory the Great. Others followed his good example and secured the works of Jerome, Augustine, Ambrose, and other Fathers.(47) Peter the Venerable declared that at Cluny books, notably the works of Augustine, were held more precious than gold.(48)

Libraries were sometimes given with the stipulation that the books should be loaned out. This was the case with Jacob of Carnarius who, in 1234, gave his library to the Dominicans of Vercelli on this condition. In 1270, Stephen, at one time archdeacon of Canterbury, donated his books to Notre Dame, Paris, on condition of their being loaned to poor theological students, and Peter of Joigny, 1297, bequeathed his collection directly to poor students.(49) In the following century Petrarch left his books to St. Marks, Venice, and Boccaccio willed his possessions of this kind to the Augustinian friars of Florence.

Manuscripts were sometimes offered at the altar or at the shrines of saints as offerings for the healing of the giver’s soul,—pro remedio animae suae.(50) On the other hand, in cases of emergency, books were put in pawn or sold. William of Longchamps, bishop of Ely, 1190, pawned 13 copies of the Gospels for the redemption of Richard I.(51) The abbot Diemo of Lorsch, 1139, needing money to pay for military equipments, sold three books ornamented with gold and precious stones.(52) Here and there, a tax was levied for the benefit of a library, as in the case of Evesham, 1215, and the synod of Lyons the same year adopted a like expedient.(53) Prince Borwin of Rostock, in 1240, gave the monastery of Dargun a hide of land, the proceeds of which were to be used for the needs of the library.(54)

Of all books, copies of the Scriptures were held in highest esteem. They were often bound in covers, inlaid with gold and silver, and sometimes ornamented with precious stones and richly illuminated. Paul, abbot of St. Albans, placed in the abbey-library eight Psalters and two Gospels highly ornamented with gold and gems, as well as a copy of the Collects, a copy of the Epistles, and 28 other books. In 1295, the dean of St. Paul’s found in the cathedral 12 copies of the Gospels adorned with jewels, and a thirteenth copy kept in a case with relics.(55)

Books were kept first in armaria or horizontal presses and the librarian was called armarius. About the fourteenth century shelves were introduced along the cloistral walls.(56) As early as the thirteenth century books were fastened by chains to protect them from being stolen by eager readers.(57) The statutes of Trinity College, Cambridge, 1350, required that certain books remain continually in the library, chained to their places, for the use of the fellows. This custom was still in vogue in England in the sixteenth century, when copies of the English Bible were kept chained to the reading desks in the churches. The old Benedictine rule was still enforced for the distribution of books. Lanfranc’s statutes for the English Benedictines, 1070, required the return of the books by the monks the first Sunday in Lent. They were then to be laid out on the floor and distributed for the ensuing year, one book to each monk. Any one failing to read his book was obliged to fall on his face and confess his neglect.(58) The loan of books was not uncommon. Bernard borrowed and lent as did Peter the Venerable.(59) The Cistercians provided for such loans to outside parties and the synod of Paris, 1212, insisted that convents should not recede from this good practice which it pronounced a work of mercy.

The book-room, or scriptorium, was part of a complete conventual building. It served as a place of writing and of transcribing manuscripts. Sometimes a monk had his own little book-room, called scriptoriolum, or kept books in his cell. Nicholas, Bernard’s secretary, described his little room as next to the infirmary and "filled with choice and divine books."(60) Peter of Celle, successor to John of Salisbury in the see of Chartres, spoke of his scriptoriolum as filled with books, where he could be free from the vanity and vexations of the world. The place had been assigned to him, he said, for reading, writing, meditating, praying, and adoring the Lord.(61)

Abbots themselves joined to their other labors the work of the copyist. So it was with Theodoric of St. Evroult, 1050–1057, a skilful scribe who, according to Ordericus Vitalis,(62) left "splendid monuments of his calligraphic skill," in copies of the Collects, Graduale, and Antiphonary which were deposited in the convent collection. Theodoric also secured the services of others to copy commentaries and the heptateuch.(63) Convents were concerned to secure expert transcribers. Copying was made a special feature of St. Albans by the abbot Paul, 1077–1093. He secured money for a scriptorium and brought scribes from a distance. In the latter part of the eleventh century, Hirschau in Southern Germany was noted for this kind of activity, through its abbot William, who saw that twelve good copyists were trained for his house. These men made many copies and William is said to have presented books to every convent he reformed. The scribe, Othlo of Emmeram, of the same century, has left us a list of the books he gave away.(64)

Diligence as a copyist sometimes stood monks in good stead when they came to face the realities of the future world. Of such an one, Ordericus makes mention.(65) This monk had copied with his own hand a bulky volume of Scripture, but he was a man of many moral offences. When the evil spirits laid claim to his soul, the angels produced the holy volume which the monk had transcribed. Every letter was counted and balanced against a sin. At last, it was found the letters had a majority of one. The devils tried to scrape up another sin, but in vain, and the Lord permitted the fortunate monk to return to the body and do proper penance.

Copying was sometimes prescribed as a punishment for cloistral offences and the Carthusian rules withheld wine from the monk who was able to copy and would not ply his art. It seems at times to have been a most confining and wearisome task. Lewis, a monk of Wessobrunn in Bavaria, had some of this feeling when he appended to a transcription of Jerome’s commentary on Daniel the following words and claimed the prayers of the reader: —

Dum scripsit friguit, et quod cum lumine solis

Scribere non potuit, perfecit lumine noctis.

"When he wrote he froze, and what he could not complete by the light of day, he finished by the light of the night."(66)

The price of books continued to be high till the invention of the printing-press. A count of Anjou paid for a copy of the homilies of Haimo of Halberstadt 200 sheep and a large quantity of provisions. In 1274, a finely written Bible sold for 50 marks, about $l70, when labor cost a shilling a day. Maitland computed that it would take a monk ten months to transcribe the Bible and that the labor would be worth to-day £60 or £70.(67) The prices, however, were often greatly reduced, and Richard of Bury, in his Philobiblion, says that he purchased from the convent of St. Albans 32 volumes for £50.

The copyists, like the builders of the cathedrals, usually concealed their names. It was a custom with them to close their task by appending some pious or, at times, some witty sentiment. A line, frequently appended, ran, finito libro, sit laus et gloria Christo. "The book is finished. Praise and honor be to Christ." The joy authors often feel at the completion of their writings was felt by a scribe when he wrote, libro completo, saltat scriptor pede leto. "Now the book is done, the scribe dances with glad foot." Another piously expressed his feelings when he wrote, dentur pro penna scriptori caelica regna. "May the heavenly reward be given to the scribe for his work with the quill."(68)

The pleasures of converse with books in the quiet of a library are thus attractively set forth by a mediaeval theologian, left alone in the convent when the other monks had gone off for recreation: —

"Our house is empty save only myself and the rats and mice who nibble in solitary hunger. There is no voice in the hall, no footstep on the stairs .... I sit here with no company but books, dipping into dainty honeycombs of literature. All minds in the world’s literature are concentrated in a library. This is the pinnacle of the temple from which we may see all the kingdoms of the world and the glory of them. I keep Egypt and the Holy Land in the closet next to the window. On the side of them are Athens and the empire of Rome. Never was such an army mustered as I have here. No general ever had such soldiers as I have. No kingdom ever had half such illustrious subjects as mine or subjects half as well disciplined. I can put my haughtiest subjects up or down as it pleases me .... I call Plato and he answers "here,"—a noble and sturdy soldier; "Aristotle," "here,"—a host in himself. Demosthenes, Pliny, Cicero, Tacitus, Caesar. "Here," they answer, and they smile at me in their immortality of youth. Modest all, they never speak unless spoken to. Bountiful all, they never refuse to answer. And they are all at peace together .... All the world is around me, all that ever stirred human hearts or fired the imagination is harmlessly here. My library cases are the avenues of time. Ages have wrought, generations grown, and all their blossoms are cast down here. It is the garden of immortal fruits without dog or dragon."

The university appears in Europe as an established institution in the twelfth century. It quickly became the restless centre of intellectual and literary life, the workshop of learning and scientific progress. Democratic in its constitution, it received men from every rank and sent them forth with new ideas and equipped to be the leaders of their age.

Origin. — The universities were a product of the mediaeval mind, to which nothing in the ancient world, in any adequate way, corresponded. They grew up on the soil of the cathedral and conventual studies, but there was no organic continuity between them and the earlier schools. They were of independent growth, coming into being in response to a demand, awakened by the changed circumstances of life and the revival of thought in Europe. No clatter and noise announced their coming, but they were developed gradually from imperfect beginnings into thoroughly organized literary corporations.

Nor were the universities the immediate creation of the Church. Church authority did not bring them into being as it did the Crusades. All that can be said is that the men who wrought at their foundations and the lower superstructures were ecclesiastics and that popes were wise enough early to become their patrons and, as in the case of Paris, to take the reins of their general administration into their own hands. The time had come for a specialization of studies in the departments of human knowledge, the arts, law, medicine, and theology, which last, according to Jacob of Vitry, "alone can be called a liberal art, since it alone delivers the human soul from its woes."

The universities owed their rise to the enthusiasm of single teachers(69) whose dialectic skill and magnetism attracted students wherever they happened to be. Bologna through Irnerius and other teachers, and Paris through a group of men, of whom Abaelard was the most prominent figure, were the centres where the university idea had its earliest and most substantial realization. These teachers satisfied and created a demand for specialization in education.

Due credit must not be withheld from the guilds whose organization furnished a pattern for the university, especially in the case of Bologna. The university was the literary guild, representing a like-minded community of intellectual interests and workers. It is also possible that some credit must be given to Arabic influences, as in the case of the school of medicine at Salerno.

The first universities arose in Italy, the earliest of all being Salerno and Bologna. These were followed by Paris and other French universities. England came next, and then Spain. Prague was the first to embody the idea in Central Europe. The distinctively German universities do not date beyond the second half of the fourteenth century, Vienna, 1365, Erfurt, 1379, Heidelberg, 1385, Cologne, 1388. The three Scotch universities, St. Andrews, Glasgow, and Aberdeen, were established in the fifteenth century. That century also witnessed the birth of the far northern Universities of Copenhagen and Upsala. By the end of the fifteenth century there were nearly eighty of these academic institutions. Some of these passed out of existence and some never attained to more than a local celebrity.

Salerno, Bologna, Paris, Padua, Oxford, Cambridge, and other universities owed their existence to no papal or royal charter. Toulouse, 1229 and Rome, 1244 were the first to be founded by papal bulls. The University of Naples was founded by the emperor, Frederick II., 1224. The Spanish Universities of Palencia, 1212, Salamanca, 1230, and Seville, 1254, were established by the kings of Castile. Prague, 1347, was founded by a double charter from the pope and Charles IV. Some universities had their origin in disaffection prevailing in universities already established: Padua started in a defection of students from Bologna; Cambridge, in 1209, in a defection of students from Oxford, and Leipzig, in 1409, grew out of the dissatisfaction of the German "nation" with its treatment at Prague. Heidelberg is the earliest institution of papal creation which went over to the Reformation.(70)

Organization. — A university originally signified not a body of studies or a place where studies were prosecuted, but an aggregation of teachers and students—universitas magistrorum et scholarium. The term "university" was used of any group of persons and was a common expression for "Your body" or "all of you"—universitas vestra.(71) In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries it was frequently applied to guilds. The literary guild, or university, denoted the group of persons carrying on studies. The equivalent in the Middle Ages for the term "university," as we use it, was studium and studium generale, "study" or, "general study." Thus the University of Bologna was called studium Bononie or Bononiense,—as it is still called studio Bolognese in Italy, Paris, studium Parisiense, Oxford, studium Oxoniense. The addition "general" had reference to students, not to a variety of branches of knowledge, and denoted that the studium was open to students from every quarter.(72) By the fifteenth century the term "university" had come to have its present meaning. The designation of a seat of learning as alma or alma mater dates from the thirteenth century.

A full university requires at least four faculties, the arts—now known at the German universities as the faculty of philosophy,—law, medicine, and theology. This idea was not embodied in the earliest foundations and some of the universities remained incomplete during their entire existence. Salerno was a medical school. Bologna was for more than a century only a school of law. Salamanca, the most venerable of existing Spanish educational institutions, did not have a faculty of theology till the end of the fourteenth century.(73) Paris, which began as a seat of theological culture, had no formal provision for the study of civil law till the seventeenth century, although civil law was taught there before 1219.(74) Nearly one half of the universities did not include theology in the list of studies. The Italian universities were, almost without an exception, at first confined to the study of jurisprudence and medicine. The reason for this may have been a purpose not to come into collision with the episcopal and conventual schools, which existed for the training of priests. The faculty of the arts, the lowest of the faculties, included the seven studies covered by the trivium and quadrivium, but was at a later period expanded so as to include metaphysical, linguistic, historic, and other studies not covered by the study of law, medicine, and theology. Theology was known as the highest and master study. Alexander IV., writing to Paris, 1256, said that theology ruled over the other studies like a mistress, and they followed her as servants.(75)

The university had its own government, endowments, and privileges. These privileges, or bills of rights, were of great value, giving the body of teachers and students protection from the usual police surveillance exercised by municipalities and included their exemption from taxation, from military service except in cases of exigency, and from the usual modes of trial before the municipal authorities. Suits brought against members of the University of Paris were tried before the bishop of Paris. In Bologna, such suits were tried before the professor of the accused student or the bishop. By the privilege of Philip Augustus, 1200, the chattels of students at Paris were exempt from seizure by the civil officer. The university was a state within the state, a free republic of letters.(76) The master and students formed, as it were, a separate class. When they felt that their rights were abused, they resorted to what was called cessation, cessatio, a suspension of the functions of the university or even removal to some other locality. In 1229 the University of Paris suspended for two years on account of the delay of Queen Blanche to give redress for the violent death of two students during the carnival. Many professors left Paris till not a single one of fame remained. The bishop of Paris launched excommunications against the chief offenders; but the university was victorious and the king made apology for the injuries inflicted and the pope revoked the ecclesiastical censures. Gregory IX., 1231, confirmed this privilege of suspending lectures.(77) This feature survives in the German universities which cling to Lehrfreiheit, the professor’s liberty to teach, as conscience dictates, without fear of interference from the state.

The Model Universities.—In the administration of their affairs the universities followed Bologna and Paris as models. In Bologna the students were in control, in Paris the masters in conjunction with the students. As for their relation to the pope and the authority of the Church, Bologna was always free, antipapal and anticlerical, as compared with her younger sister in France. The democratic principle had large recognition. The first element to be noticed is the part played by the different faculties. In Paris the faculties were fully organized by the middle of the thirteenth century. In 1281, the university as a body promised to defend each of its faculties.(78) Long before that time each faculty passed upon its own degrees, regulated its own lectures, and performed other special acts.

The second element was the part which the so-called nations had in the administration. In Bologna there were four nations, the Italians, English,(79) Provençals, and Germans. The students of Paris were likewise divided into four groups, representing France, Picardy (including the Netherlands), Normandy, and England, the last giving place, in 1430, to Germany. The distinctive organization at Paris goes back to the early years of the thirteenth century.(80) At first floating colonies brought together by national and linguistic affinities, the nations were developed into corporate organizations, each with a code of its own. They were in turn divided into provinces. An elective official, known as the rector, stood at the head of the whole corporation. At Bologna he was called, as early as 1194, "rector of the associations," rector societatum. He directed the affairs of the university in conjunction with a board of counsellors representing the provinces.

The first record calling the head of the University of Paris rector(81) occurs in a bull of Alexander IV., 1259, but the office, no doubt, existed long before. He was chosen by the proctors or presidents of the four nations. The rector had to be a master of arts and might be a layman, but must be a celibate. He performed on great occasions, and wore a striking costume. He was responsible to the body whose agent he was. The Paris rector was addressed as "your amplitude," vestra amplitudo.

At Paris there was also a chancellor, and he was the older officer. He stood at the head of the chapter of Notre Dame and was called interchangeably chancellor of the cathedral and chancellor of Paris. To him belonged the prerogative of giving the license to teach and confer degrees. His authority was recognized, time and again, by the popes, and also restricted by papal decree, so that what he lost the rector gained.(82) In Bologna, by the decree of Honorius III., 1219, the archdeacon of the diocese conferred the degrees.(83)

Degrees.—By 1264, at latest, each faculty at Paris had its own dean(84) and exercised the right to grant the license to teach in its own department. Such license,—jus docendi, or legendi,—when conferred by Bologna or Paris, carried with it the right to teach everywhere,—jus ubique docendi. Gregory IX., 1233, and other popes conferred the same prerogative upon the masters of Toulouse and other universities but it seems doubtful whether their degrees were respected. Even a degree from Oxford did not carry the right of lecturing at Paris without a reëxamination. When Alexander IV. granted to the masters of Salamanca the right of teaching everywhere, Bologna and Paris were expressly excepted.(85)

The question of mediaeval degrees offers much difficulty. There seem to have been three stages: bachelor, or baccalaureus, licentiate, and doctor or master. They corresponded to the three grades in the guilds: apprentice, assistant, and master. The bachelors were received after examination and did subordinate lecturing. The degree was not merely a testimonial of work done, but a certificate entitling the holder to ply the trade of reading or teaching. The titles, master, magister, doctor, dominus, and professor, scholasticus, were synonymous. "Doctor" was the usual title at Bologna, and "master" at Paris, but gradually "doctor" came to be used chiefly of the graduates in canon law at Paris, and "master" of graduates in theology.(86) In his charter of 1224, Frederick spoke of the "doctors and masters in each faculty," no doubt using the words as synonyms. The test for the degrees was called the "determination," determinance, the main part of which was the presentation of a thesis and its defence against all comers.

Eight years was fixed by Robert de Courcon, 1215, as the period of preparation for the theological doctorate, but in the beginning of the fourteenth century it was extended to fourteen years. In the department of jurisprudence a course of eight years,—in medicine a course of six years,—was required.

Teachers and Studies.—The teaching was done at first in convents and in private quarters. In 1253 there were twelve professors of theology in Paris, nine of them teaching in convents and belonging to the orders. University buildings were of slow growth, and the phenomenon presented by such great universities as Johns Hopkins, Cornell, and the University of Chicago, starting out fully equipped with large endowments and buildings, was unknown in the Middle Ages. Professors and students had to make their own way and at first no provision was made by king or municipality for salaries. The professor lived by lecture fees and the gifts of rich students. Later, endowments were provided, and cities provided funds for the payment of salaries.(87) Colleges were at first bursaries, or hostels, where students lived together, gratuitous provision being made for their support.(88) The earliest endowment of this kind, which still exists, is the college of the Sorbonne at Paris, founded by Robert of Sorbon, 1257, for sixteen secular students, four from each nation. The term "secular" was used in distinction from conventual. Another famous college was the college of Navarre on St. Genevieve, founded by the queen of Philip the Fair, Jeanne of Navarre, 1304. Rashdall, I. 478–517, gives a list of more than sixty colleges, or bursaries, founded in Paris before 1500. From being places of residence for needy students, the colleges came to include masters, as at Oxford and Cambridge. At Bologna the college system was never developed to the same extent as at Paris and in England.

With rare exceptions, the teachers in all the faculties were ecclesiastics, or, if laymen, unmarried. John XXII., in 1331, granted a dispensation to a married man to teach medicine in Paris, but it was an exception. Not till 1452 was the requirement of celibacy modified for the faculty of medicine in Paris, and till 1479 for Heidelberg; and not till a later date were the legal professors of Paris and Bologna exempted from this restriction. The Reformation at once effected a change in the universities under Protestant influence.(89)

The lectures were given in Latin and students as well as masters were required to use Latin in conversation. Learning of any kind was regarded as too sacred a thing to be conveyed in the vulgar dialects of Europe.(90) The studies at the University of Paris were authoritatively prescribed by the papal legate, Robert de Courcon, 1215.(91) Gregory IX., 1231, also took part in stating what the text-books should be. The classics had no place. Certain works of Aristotle were forbidden, as were also, at a later date, the writings of Amauri of Bena, David of Dinant, and other supposed or real heretics. Gregory IX. warned the divinity students against affecting philosophy, and to be satisfied with becoming "theodocts."(92)

Attendance and Discipline.—The attendance at the mediaeval universities has been a matter of much dispute. Some of the figures seem to be incredibly large.(93) No matriculation books exist for the earliest periods, and not till the end of the fourteenth century do we have actual records of the number of graduations in Paris. Odefridus, a writer of the thirteenth century, gives the number of students at Bologna two generations before, as 10,000. Paris was reported to have had 25,000 students, and Oxford as many as 30,000,(94) or at one time, to follow Wyclif, 60,000. Speaking of his own times Wyclif, however, gives the more reasonable figure, 3000. In his days of unobscured fame, Abaelard lectured to 3000 hearers, and this figure does not seem to be exaggerated when we consider the great attraction of his personality. In any estimate, it must be remembered that the student body included boys and also men well up in years. Rashdall makes 1500 to 3000 the maximum number for Oxford.(95)

There was no such thing as university discipline in the thirteenth century, as we understand discipline. The testimonies are unanimous that the students led a wild life.(96) Many of them were mere boys, studying in the department of arts. There were no dormitories, and the means of communication then at hand did not make it possible for parents to exercise the checks upon absent sons such as they may exercise to-day. Felix Platter, d. 1614, states in his autobiography that, as late as the middle of the sixteenth century, it required twenty days to make the journey from Basel to the school of Montpellier. At Paris students were excused from the payment of fees on account of the long distances from which they had come, the journeys often requiring several months and involving perils from robbers.(97) Complaint was made in Paris, 1218, of scholars who broke into houses, carrying off girls and women; and in 1269 a public proclamation denounced gangs of students who broke into houses, ravished women, and committed robberies and "many other enormities hateful to God."(98) In Paris the inns—tabernae — were numerous. English students were noted for their drinking, and "to drink like an Englishman and to sing like a Norman" became proverbial.(99) The duel was a common way of settling disputes, and Gregory IX., 1231, forbade students going through the streets carrying arms.(100)

The rescript given by Frederick Barbarossa to Bologna, 1158, presented a picture of students as those "who exile themselves through love of learning and wear themselves out in poverty." The facts do not support any rosy picture of social equality, such as we would expect in an ideal democracy. The number who were drawn to the universities from love of adventure and novelty must have been large. The nobleman had his special quarters and his servants, while the poor student begged his bread. It was the custom of the chancellor of Oxford to issue licenses for the needy to beg.(101) At Bologna the rich occupied the first seats.(102) Robert of Courçon commended the gift of garments and other articles to needy scholars.

The mediaeval universities were the centres of the ideals and hopes of the younger generation. There, the seeds were sown of the ecclesiastical and intellectual movements of after times and of the revolutions which the conservative groups pronounced scientific novelties and doctrinal heresies.

A mediaeval writer pronounced the three chief forces for the maintenance of the Catholic faith to be the priesthood, the empire, and the university. This was not always the case. From Paris went forth some of the severest attacks on the theory of papal absolutism, and from there, a century later, the reformers, Gerson and D’Ailly, proceeded. Hussitism was begotten at Prague. Wyclif’s teachings made Oxford a seat of heresy. Wittenberg, the last of the mediaeval universities to open its doors, protected and followed Luther. Basel, Pius II.’s creation, Heidelberg, Oxford, Cambridge, St. Andrews, and other universities became the bulwarks of the new ideas. On the other hand, the Sorbonne, Louvaine, and Cologne ordered Luther’s works burnt. As an agent of culture and the onward progress of mankind, the Middle Ages made no contribution to modern times comparable in usefulness to the university.

Bologna is the most venerable of European universities. Salerno, which preceded it in time, became sufficiently famous as a medical school to call forth from Petrarch the praise of being the fountain-head of medicine,—fons medicinae,—but its career was limited to two centuries.(103) The origin of Salerno is lost in obscurity. There seems to be no sufficient evidence to show that the school owed its origin to the convent of Monte Cassino which was eighty miles away. It was the outgrowth of the awakened interest in medicine in Southern Italy in which Greek and Arabic influences had a part.

In 1888, Bologna celebrated its eight hundredth anniversary and continues to be one of the most flourishing schools of Southern Europe. As early as the thirteenth century, the tradition was current that Theodosius II., in 433, had granted to it a charter. But its beginnings go no further back than the latter part of the eleventh or the earlier years of the twelfth century. At that period Irnerius, d. about 1130, was teaching the code of Justinian(104) and a little later the Camaldulensian monk, Gratian, taught canon law in the convent of St. Felix. These two masters of jurisprudence, civil and ecclesiastical, are looked upon as the fathers of the university.

Bologna became the chief school for the study of both laws in Europe. The schools of arts added 1221, of medicine 1260, and theology 1360—by a bull of Innocent VI.—never obtained the importance of the school of law.

On a visit to the city in 1155 Frederick Barbarossa granted the university recognition and in 1158, on the field of Roncaglia, gave it its first charter.(105) This is the oldest piece of university legislation. Thenceforth Bologna was a second and better Berytus, the nurse of jurisprudence, legum nutrix, and adopted the proud device, Bononia docet—"Bologna teaches." To papal patronage she owed little or nothing, and in this respect as in others her history did not run parallel with the University of Paris.(106) Students flocked to her by hundreds and thousands from all parts of Western Europe.

The student body, which was in control, was at first divided into four "universities" or guilds. The statutes of the German "nation" have been preserved and declare as its object fraternal charity, mutual association, the care of the sick and support of the needy, the conduct of funerals, the termination of quarrels, and the proper escort of students about to take the examination for the degree of doctor.(107) By the fourteenth century, the four "universities" had given place to the two groups called the Ultramontanes and the Cismontanes, each of which was subdivided into smaller groups presided over by counsellors, conciliarii.

The rectors of the faculties were elected for two years and were required to be secular clerics, unmarried, and wearing the clerical habit. The ceremonies of installation included the placing of a hood on their heads. The two rectors of the two jurist "universities" gave place to a single rector after the middle of the fourteenth century.

The professors took oaths to the student bodies, to follow their codes. If they wished to be absent from their duties, they were obliged to get leave of absence from the rectors. They were required to begin and close their lectures promptly at the ringing of the bell under penalty of a fine and were forbidden to skip any part of the text-books or postpone the answer to questions to the end of the lecture hour. Another rule required them to cover a certain amount of ground in a given period.(108) The professors were kept in awe by the threat which the student body held over them of migrating when there was cause for dissatisfaction. This sort of boycott was exercised a number of times as when Bolognese students decamped and departed to Vicenza 1204, Padua 1222, and for the last time to Siena 1321.

The professors, at first, were dependent upon fees and at times stopped their lectures because of the failure of the students to pay up. The jurist, Odefridus of Bologna, announced on one occasion that he would not lecture in the afternoons of the ensuing term because, "the scholars want to profit but not to pay." Professorial appointments were at first in the hands of the student body but afterwards became the prerogative of the municipality. This change was due in part to the obligation undertaken by the city government to pay fixed salaries.(109) Strange to say, about the middle of the thirteenth century, the professorships at Bologna became largely hereditary.

A noticeable, though not exceptional, feature of Bologna was the admission of learned women to its teaching chairs. Novella d’Andrea, 1312–1366, the daughter of the celebrated jurist Giovanni d’Andrea, lectured on philosophy and law, but behind a curtain, lest her face should attract the attention of the students from their studies. Among other female professors have been Laura Bassi, d. 1778, doctor and professor of philosophy and mathematics; Chlotilda Tambroni, who expounded the Greek classics, 1794–1817; and Giuseppina Cattani, who, until a few years ago, lectured on pathology. In Salerno, also, women practised medicine and lectured, as did Trotula, about 1059, who wrote on the diseases of women. In Paris, as we have been reminded by Denifle, the daughters of one Mangold taught theology in the latter part of the eleventh century.(110)

On the other hand, due care was taken to protect the students of Bologna against the wiles of women. The statutes of its college, founded by Cardinal Albornoz, 1367, for Spanish students, forbade them dancing because "the devil easily tempts men to evil through this amusement," and also forbade women to "enter the premises because a woman was the head of sin, the right hand of the devil, and the cause of the expulsion from paradise."(111)

A graduate of civil law was required at Bologna to have studied seven years, and of canon law six years. To become a doctor of both laws, utriusque juris, a term of ten years was prescribed. In 1292, Nicholas IV. formally granted the Bolognese doctors the right to lecture everywhere, a right they had exercised before. The promotion to the doctorate was accompanied with much pageantry an involved the candidate in large outlay for gifts and banquets.(112)

The class rooms in canon and civil jurisprudence at Bologna became synonymous with traditional opinions. There was no encouragement of originality. With the interpretation of the text-books, which had been handed down, the work of the professor was at an end. This conservatism Dante may have had in mind when he made the complaint that in Bologna only the Decretals were studied. And Roger Bacon exclaimed that "the study of jurisprudence has for forty years destroyed the study of wisdom [that is philosophy, the sciences, and theology], yes, the church itself and all departments."(113) When the Renaissance came, it did not start with Bologna or any of the other Italian universities but in the courts of princes and popes and especially in the city of Florence. The universities produced no Savonarola and encouraged no religious or doctrinal reform.

The lustre of the University of Paris filled all Western Europe as early as the first years of the thirteenth century. It continued to be the chief seat of theological and general learning till the Reformation. In 1231 Gregory IX. called Paris "the parent of the sciences, another Kerieth Sepher, a city of letters, in which, as in a factory of wisdom, the precious stones and gold of wisdom are wrought and polished for the Church of Christ."(114) In the same strain Alexander IV., 1256, eulogized the university(115) as "that most excellent state of letters, a famous city of the arts, a notable school of erudition, the highest factory of wisdom,—officina sapientiae — and the most efficient gymnasium of study. There, a clear spring of the sciences breaks forth at which the peoples of all nations drink." Three hundred years later, in 1518, Luther, in his protest to Cajetan, expressed his willingness to have his case go before the University of Paris to which he referred, "as the parent of studies and from antiquity ever the most Christian University and that in which theology has been particularly cultivated."

The old tradition, which traced the origin of the university back to Charlemagne, the pride of the French has been slow to abandon. Du Boulay devoted an entire volume to its assumed history before the year 1000. Not even was Abaelard its founder. The most that can be said is, that that brilliant teacher prepared the way for the new institution,(116) whose beginnings belong to the period 1150–1170.

From its earliest era of development, the university received the recognition of royalty and the favor of popes who were quick to discern its future importance. In the year 1200 Philip Augustus, king of France, conferred upon it a valuable privilege, granting the students and teaching body independent rights over against the municipal government. Among its venerable documents are communications from Innocent III., his legate, Robert of Courçon, Honorius III. and Gregory IX., 1231. From that time on, the archives abound in papal letters and communications addressed to the pope by the university authorities.

In Paris, as has already been said, the masters were the controlling body. The first use of the expression "university of masters and scholars" occurs in 1221.(117) The earliest example of statutes is found in a bull of Innocent III., written about 1209.(118) Later, Innocent recognized the corporate rights of the body when he permitted it to have a representative at Rome and ordered an expelled master to be readmitted. The statutes of Robert of Courçon, 1215, prescribed text-books and other regulations. A university seal was used as early as 1221.(119) Disputes between the university and the chancellor of the cathedral and other church authorities of Paris date back as far as 1213.

There has been much difference of opinion as to what was the original norm of the organization of the university. Denifle, the leading modern authority, insists against Du Boulay that it was the four faculties and not the "nations," and he finds the faculties developed in the earliest years of the thirteenth century.(120) Some association of masters existed as early as 1170, about which time, John of Celle, abbot of St. Albans, 1195–1214, was admitted into its membership.(121) In 1207, Innocent III. spoke of the "body of masters," and in 1213 he recognized the right of the masters to insist upon the conferring of the license to teach upon the candidates whom they presented. The chancellor was left no option in the matter.(122) In the middle of the thirteenth century, his authority was still more curtailed by the withdrawal of some of the masters to the hill of St. Genevieve on the western bank of the Seine. The abbot of St. Genevieve, who began to be styled, "Chancellor of St. Genevieve" in 1255, assumed the right to confer licensures or degrees and the right was recognized by papal decree.(123)

The four nations seem to have been developed out of the demand for discipline among the students of cognate regions and for mutual protection against the civil authorities. It is quite possible the example set in Bologna had some influence in Paris.

The bull of Gregory IX., 1231, parens scientiarum, called by Denifle the "magna charta of the university," recognized and sealed its liberties. It was called forth by the suspension of lectures which had lasted two years. The trouble originated in a brawl in an inn, which developed into a fight between gown and town. The police of the city, with the assent of Queen Blanche, interfered, and killed several of the students. The professors ordered a "cessation" and, when they found that justice was not done, adjourned the university for six years. Some of them emigrated to England and were employed at Oxford and Cambridge.(124) Others settled down at other schools in France. The trouble was brought to an end by Gregory IX., who ratified the right of the masters to secede, and called upon Blanche to punish the offending officials, forbade the chancellor to have any prisons, and the bishop from imposing mulcts or imprisoning students.

It is possible that the office of rector goes back as far as 1200, when an official was called "the head of the Paris scholars."(125) As early as 1245 the title appears distinctly and the rector is distinguished from the proctors.(126) At a later time it was the proper custom, in communicating with the university, to address the "rector and the masters." The question of precedence as between the rector and other high dignitaries, such as the bishop and chancellor of Paris, was one which led to much dispute and elbowing. Du Boulay, himself an ex-rector, takes pride in giving instances of the rector’s outranking archbishops, cardinals, papal nuncios, peers of France, and other lesser noteworthies at public functions.(127)

The faculties came to be presided over by deans, the nations by proctors. In the management of the general affairs of the university, the vote was taken by faculties.

The liberties, which the university enjoyed in its earlier history, were greatly curtailed by Louis XI. and by his successors in the latter half of the fifteenth century. The university was treated to sharp rebukes for attempting to interfere with matters that did not belong to it. The right of cessation was withdrawn and the free election of the rectors denied.(128) The police of the city were invested with larger jurisdiction, and the sovereign’s will was made a controlling element.

The fame of the University of Paris came from its schools of arts and theology. The college of the Sorbonne, originally a bursary for poor students of theology, afterwards gave its name to the theological department. It was founded by Robert of Sorbon, the chaplain of St. Louis, the king himself giving part of the site for its building. In the course of time, its halls came to be used for disputations, and the decisions of the faculty obtained a European reputation. Theological students of twenty-five years of age, who had studied six years, and passed an examination, were eligible for licensure as bachelors. For the first three years they read on the Bible and then on the Sentences of the Lombard. These readers were distinguished as Biblici and Sententiarii. The age limit for the doctorate was thirty-five.

One of the most interesting chapters in the history of the university is the struggle over the admission of the mendicant friars in the middle of the thirteenth century. The papacy secured victory for the friars. And the unwilling university was obliged to recognize them as a part of its teaching force.

The struggle broke out first at the time of the "cessation," 1229, when, as it would seem, the Dominicans secretly favored the side of the civil magistrates against the university authorities, and poisoned the court against them. The Dominicans were established in Paris, 1217 and the Franciscans, 1220, and both orders, furnished with letters of commendation by Honorius III., were at first well received, so the masters themselves declared in a document dated 1254.(129) But they soon began to show arrogance and to demand the right to degrees for their students without promising submission to the statutes of the university. One of the first two Dominican masters to teach at the university was the Englishman, John of Giles. After preaching on poverty in St. Jacques, John descended from the pulpit and put on the Dominican robes.

At the "cessation" of 1251 the two Dominicans and one Franciscan, who were recognized as masters by the university, refused to join with the other authorities, and, after the settlement of the difficulty, the two Dominicans were refused readmittance. A statute was passed forbidding admission to the fellowship, consortium, of the university for those who refused to take the oath to obey its rules. The friars refused to obey the statute and secured from Alexander IV. an order requiring the university to receive them, and setting aside all sentences passed against them.(130)

The friction continued, and the seculars sought to break the influence of the Franciscans by pointing out the heresies of Joachim of Flore. The friars retorted by attacking William of St. Amour whose work, The Perils of the Last Times, was a vigorous onslaught upon mendicancy as contrary to Apostolic teaching. William’s book, which called out refutations from Thomas Aquinas and Bonaventura, was burnt, and refusing to recant, the author was suspended from teaching and banished from France.(131) The friars were hooted on the streets and beaten. By 1257 tranquillity was restored, as we are assured by Alexander IV. Thus the papacy made repayment to the university for its readiness from of old to accept its guidance by depriving the institution of its liberties.(132)

From the middle of the fourteenth century, the University of Paris played no mean part in the political affairs of France. More than once she spoke before the court and before the peers of the realm, and more than once was she rebuked for her unsolicited zeal.(133) French kings themselves styled her "the daughter of the king." She was actively zealous in the persecution of Joan of Arc.(134)

As a factor in the religious history of Europe, the university figured most prominently during the Western schism—1378–1418. She suggested the three ways of healing the rupture and, to accomplish this result, sent her agents through Western Europe to confer with the kings and other powers. Under the guidance of her chancellors, Gerson and D’Ailly, the discussions of the Reformatory councils of Pisa and Constance were directed, which brought the papal schism to an end. The voting by nations at Constance was her triumph.

As for disputes on distinctly doctrinal questions, the university antagonized John XXII. and his heresy, denying the beatific vision at death. In 1497 she exacted from all candidates for degrees an oath accepting the dogma of the immaculate conception. When the Protestant Reformation came, she decided against that movement and ordered the books of Luther burnt.

Next to Paris in age and importance, as a school of philosophy and theology, is the University of Oxford, whose foundation tradition falsely traces to King Alfred. The first historical notice of Oxenford, or Oxford, occurs in 912. Three religious institutions were founded in the town, from any one of which or all of which the school may have had its inception: the priory of St. Frideswyde, Osseney abbey, and the church of the canons regular of St. George’s in the Castle. The usually accepted view connects it with the first. But it is possible the university had its real beginning in a migration from Paris in 1167. This view is based upon a statement of John of Salisbury, that France had expelled her alien scholars and an order of Henry II. forbidding clerks to go to the Continent or to return from it without a license from the justiciar.(135) Before that time, however, there was teaching in Oxford.

The first of the teachers, Thibaut d’Estampes, Theobaldus Stampensis, moved from St. Stephen’s abbey, Caen, and taught in Oxford between 1117 and 1121. He had a school of from sixty to a hundred pupils, and called himself an Oxford master, magister oxenfordiae. He was ridiculed by a monk as a "petty clerk" tantillus clericellus, one of those "wandering chaplains, with pointed beards, curled hair, and effeminate dress, who are ashamed of the proper ecclesiastical habit and the tonsure," and was also accused of being "occupied with secular literature."

The University of Cambridge, which first appears clearly in 1209,(136) did not gain a position of much rank till the fifteenth century and can show no eminent teacher before that time. The first papal recognition dates from the bull of Gregory IX., 1233, which mentions a chancellor.(137)

During the Reformation period, Cambridge occupied a position of note and influence equal to Oxford. Fisher, bishop of Rochester, martyred by Henry VIII. and one of the freest patrons of learning, was instrumental in the foundation of two colleges, Christs, 1505, and St. John, 1511. Among its teachers were Erasmus, and later Bucer and Fagius, the Continental Reformers. Tyndale, the translator of the first printed English New Testament, and Thomas Bilney, both of them martyrs, were its scholars. So were the three martyrs, Cranmer, Latimer, and Ridley, though they were burnt at Oxford. During the Elizabethan period, the university was a stronghold of Puritanism with Cartwright and Travers occupying chairs. Cudworth and the Neo-Platonists flourished there. And in recent years its chairs have been filled by such representatives of the historical and exegetical schools as Bishop Lightfoot, Westcott, his successor at Durham, Ellicott, bishop of Gloucester and Bristol, and John Anthony Hort.

Oxford and Cambridge differ from the Continental universities in giving prominence to undergraduate studies and in the system of colleges and halls, and also in the closer vital relations they sustain to the Church.

In 1149 the Italian, Vacarius, introduced the study of civil law in Oxford, if we are to follow the doubtful testimony of Gervaise of Canterbury, though it is more probable that he delivered his lectures in the household of the archbishop of Canterbury, Theobald.(138) He wrote, it is said, a digest of laws "sufficient for deciding all legal problems which are wont to be discussed in the schools."

One of the very earliest notices of Oxford as a seat of study is found in a description by Giraldus Cambrensis, the Welsh traveller and historian. About the year 1185 he visited the town and read "before the faculties, doctors, and students" his work on the Topography of Ireland.(139) The school was evidently of some importance to attract such a man. Walter Map, archdeacon of Oxford, is called by Giraldus "an Oxford master." The first degree known to have been conferred was given to Edmund Rich, afterwards archbishop of Canterbury. From Geraldus it is evident that the masters were grouped in faculties. As early as 1209 and in consequence of the hanging of three students by the mayor, there was a migration of masters and students, said to have been three thousand in number, from which the University of Cambridge had its beginning.(140)

The University of Oxford was less bound by ecclesiastical authority than Paris. An unsuccessful attempt was made by the bishop of Lincoln, in whose diocese it was located, to assert supervisory authority. The bull of Innocent IV., issued 1254, was the nearest approach to a papal charter and confirmed the university in its "immunities and ancient customs." In 1201 a chancellor is mentioned for the first time. From the beginning this official seems to have been elected by the university. He originally held his office for a term of two years. At the present time the chancellor is an honorary dignitary who does not pretend to reside in Oxford.

In 1395, the university was exempted by papal bull from all control of bishops or legati nati. This decree was revoked in 1411 in consequence of the disturbances with Wyclif and his followers, but, in 1490, Sixtus IV. again renewed the exemption from ecclesiastical authority.

The university was constantly having conflicts with the town and its authorities. The most notable one occurred in 1354. As usual, it originated in a tavern brawl, the keeper of the place being supported not only by his fellow-townsmen but by thousands from the neighboring country.(141) The chancellor fled. The friars brought out the host and placed it between the combatants, but it was crushed to the earth and a scholar put to death while he was clinging to the friar who held it. Much blood was shed. The townsmen, bent upon paying off old scores, broke into twenty college inns and halls and pillaged them. Even the sanctity of the churches was not respected, and the scholars were hunted down who sought shelter in them. The students left the city. The chancellor appealed the case to the king, and through his authority and the spiritual authority of the bishop the town corporation was forced to make reparation. The place was put under interdict for a year. Officials were punished and restitution of goods to the students was made. The interdict was withdrawn only on condition that the mayor, bailiffs, and sixty burghers should appear in St. Mary’s church on the anniversary of the breaking out of the riot, St. Scholastica’s day, and do penance for the slaughtered students, each burgher laying down a penny on the high altar, the sum to be divided equally between poor students and the curate. It was not till 1825, that the university agreed to forego the spectacle of this annual penance which had been kept up for nearly five centuries. Not for several years did the university assume its former aspect.(142) Among the students themselves peace did not always reign. The Irish contingent was banished, 1413, by act of parliament for turbulence.(143)

The arrival of the Dominicans and Franciscans, as has been said in other places, was an event of very great interest at Oxford, but they never attained to the independent power they reached in Paris. They were followed by the Carmelites, the Augustinians, and other orders.

The next important event was the controversy over Wyclif and the doctrines and persons of the Lollards, which filled the years of the last quarter of the fourteenth century and beyond.

At the English universities the college system received a permanent development. Endowments, established by the liberality of bishops, kings, and other personalities, furnished the nucleus for corporations and halls consisting of masters and students, each with a more or less distinct life of it sown. These college bodies and their buildings continue to impart to Oxford and Cambridge a mediaeval aspect and to recall on every hand the venerable memories of past centuries. Twenty-one of these colleges and five halls remain in Oxford. The oldest are University College founded by a bequest of William of Durham at his death, 1249; Merton, 1264; Balliol founded by the father of the Scotch king, 1266; Exeter, 1314; Oriel, 1324; Queen’s College, 1340; the famous New College, 1379, founded by William of Wykenham, bishop of Winchester; All Souls, 1438; Magdalen, 1448, where Wolsey was fellow. Among the illustrious men who taught at Oxford, in the earlier periods, were Edmund Rich, Roger Bacon, Grosseteste, Adam Marsh, Duns Scotus, Ockam, Bradwardine, Richard of Armagh, Wyclif.

As a centre of theological training, Oxford has been closely identified with some of the most important movements in the religious history of England. There Wyclif preached his doctrine and practical reforms. There the Humanists, Grocyn, Colet, and Linacre taught. The school was an important religious centre in the time of the Reformation, in the Commonwealth period, and the period of the Restoration. Within its precincts the Wesleys and Whitefield studied and the Methodist movement had its birth, and there, in the first half of the last century, Pusey, Keble, and Newman exerted the spell of their influence, and the Tractarian movement was started and fostered. Since the year 1854 Oxford and Cambridge have been open to Dissenters. All religious tests were abolished 187l. In 1885 the spiritual descendants of the Puritans, the Independents, established Mansfield College, in Oxford, for the training of ministers.

§ 94. The Cathedrals.

This enthusiastic admirer of architecture, especially the Gothic, judged art from the higher standpoint of morality and religion.

The cathedrals of the Middle Ages were the expression of religious praise and devotion and entirely the product of the Church. No other element entered into their construction. They were hymns in stone, and next to the universities are the most imposing and beneficent contribution the mediaeval period made to later generations. The soldiery of the Crusades failed in its attempt at conquest. The builders at home wrought out structures which have fed the piety and excited the admiration of all ages since. They were not due to the papacy but to the devotion of cities, nobles, and people.

It was a marked progress from the triclinia, or rooms in private houses, and crypts, in which the early Christians worshipped, to the cathedral of St. Sophia, at the completion of which Justinian is said to have exclaimed, "O Solomon, I have excelled thee." And what a change it was from the huts and rude temples of worship of Central and Northern Europe to the splendid structures dedicated to Christian worship,—the worship which Augustine of Canterbury, and Boniface, and St. Ansgar had introduced among the barbarous Northern tribes!

It is also characteristic that the great mediaeval structures were not palaces or buildings devoted to commerce, although the Gothic palace of the doges, in Venice, and the town halls of Brussels, Louvaine, and other cities of Belgium and Holland are extensive and imposing. They were buildings devoted to religion, whether cathedral or conventual structures. They were often, as in France, placed on an elevation or in the centre of the city, and around them the dwellings clustered as if for protection.

The great cathedrals became a daily sermon, bearing testimony to the presence of God and the resurrection of Christ. They served the people as a Bible whose essential teachings they beheld with the eye. Through the spectacle of their walls and soaring spires, their thoughts were uplifted to spiritual things. Their ample spaces, filled or dimly lit with the sunlight piercing through stained-glass windows, reminded them of the glory of the life beyond, which makes itself known through varied revelations to the lonely and mysterious existence of the earth. The strong foundations and massive columns and buttresses typified the stability of God’s throne, and that He hath made all things through the word of His power.

Their construction occupied years and, in cases, centuries were necessary to complete them. Who can estimate the prayers and pious devotion which the laying of the first stones called forth, and which continued to be poured out till the last layer of stones was laid on the towers or fitted into the finial? Their sculpture and stained-glass windows, frescoes, and paintings presented scenes from Scripture and the history of the Church. There, kings and queens, warriors, and the men whom the age pronounced godly were laid away in sepulture, a custom continued after the modern period had begun, as in the case of Luther and Melanchthon, whose ashes rest in the Castle Church of Wittenberg. In spite of frequent fires consuming parts of the great churches or the entire buildings, they were restored or reconstructed, often several times, as in the case of the cathedrals of Chartres, Canterbury, and Norwich. Central towers collapsed, as in the case of Winchester, Peterborough, Lincoln, and other English cathedrals, but they were rebuilt. In the erection of these churches princes and people joined, and to further this object they gave their contributions of material and labor. The women of Ulm gave up all their ornaments to advance the work upon the cathedral of that city, and to the construction of the cathedral in Cologne Germans in all lands contributed.

The eleventh century is the beginning of one of the most notable periods of architecture in the world’s history, lasting for nearly three centuries. It has a distinct character of its own and in its service high talent was consecrated. The monks may be said to have led the way by their zeal to erect strong, ample, and beautiful cloistral establishments. These called forth in France the ambition of the bishops to surpass them. Two styles of architecture are usually distinguished in this period, the Romanesque, called in England also the Norman, and the Gothic. Writers on architecture make a number of subdivisions and some have included all the architecture of the twelfth to the fifteenth centuries under the title Gothic, or Christian Pointed. During these centuries Europe, from the South to far Northern Scotland and Sweden, was dotted with imposing structures which on the one hand vied with St. Sophia of Constantinople, and on the other have been imitated but not equalled since.

In Rome as late as the thirteenth century, when Honorius III. began the construction of San Lorenzo, the old basilica style continued to rule. The Romanesque style started from Northern Italy and, in the beginning of the eleventh century, crossed the Alps, where it had its most glorious triumphs. In Italy, the cathedral of Pisa represents the blending of the old and the new, the cruciform shape and the dome. In Germany, the cathedrals of Spires, Worms, and Mainz belong to this period, and in England its earlier cathedrals, or portions of them, like Winchester, begun about 1070, Worcester about 1084, Peterborough about 1120, Norwich about 1096, Ely about 1083, Durham about 1099.

For the fundamental ground plan of the basilica was substituted the form of the cross. The size of the choir was increased and the choir was elevated. It was the age of the priesthood, and sacerdotalism was represented in the enlargement of the altar, in increased and rich stalls for the clergy, and spaces at the rear of the altar. These features also belong to the preceding period, but now receive greater emphasis. The large end of the cross, or nave, especially in the English cathedrals, was greatly extended so that the altar and its furniture were seen from afar, for the chief doors were in that end, which faced the west. In England, the transepts, or arms of the cross, became long and spacious. The tower became a prominent feature, and buttresses were added to the walls. In Italy, the tower took the shape of a campanile, which was built in addition to the dome, and was sometimes a separate building and never an essential part of it. The vaulted and groined roof took the place of the flat roof.

The Gothic style, so called in Italy from its reputed barbaric features, found altogether its highest development in the North, and started in Northern France. It is the grandest style of church architecture ever wrought out. It was shown in the height of the church walls and in spires struggling to reach to the very throne of God itself. The vault of the cathedral of Amiens is 147 feet above the floor, of Beauvais 157 feet, of Cologne 155 feet. This style developed the pointed arch, perpendicular lines, the lancet window. It had some of the features of the Lombardy poplars, soaring, stern, solemn. In its strong, ramparted buttresses, its towers, and its massive columns, it represented the hardihood and strength of the northern forest. Its pointed roofs were adapted to receive the storms of snow common to the North. Its flying buttresses and elaborate carvings within, and its splendid entrances, especially in the French cathedrals, typified the richness of Christian promise and hope.

The Gothic style started in France in the thirteenth century. Notable examples are found in Rheims, begun 1211, Amiens, Laon, and in Notre Dame, Paris, begun in 1163. The arches are less pointed than in England and the portals are on a much grander scale and more highly ornamented. At Notre Dame we have one of the finest specimens of flying buttresses. In its case and most cases of French Gothic there are towers. The cathedrals of Paris, Amiens, and Rheims have unfinished towers. The Sainte Chapelle in Paris is a splendid piece of pure Gothic.

In Germany, fine examples of Gothic are found in the church at Marburg dedicated to St. Elizabeth, in Nürnberg, Bamberg, Freiburg, Strassburg, and other cities. The cathedral of Cologne is said to be the most perfect specimen of Gothic in existence. Its choir was begun in 1248, Konrad of Hochsteden laying the corner-stone in the presence of the newly elected emperor, William of Holland, and many princes. The choir was dedicated in 1322. By 1437 one of the towers was finished up to one-third of its present height. At the time of the Reformation the roof was covered with boards. In the nineteenth century the original plans were discovered and the completion of the edifice, including the two spires, was made a national undertaking. The work was finished in 1880.

England is rich in memorials of mediaeval architecture which began with the arrival of the Normans. The nation’s life is interwoven with them, and Westminster Abbey is perhaps the most august place of sepulture in the world. In addition to the cathedrals already mentioned, Lincoln, Canterbury, York, Salisbury, and other great churches were begun in this period. Addition after addition was made till the noble churches of England got their final shape. The tower is one of the prominent features of the English cathedral, Lichfield being probably the most important with spires. The finest outside impression is made by Salisbury and Lincoln minsters. Many of these cathedrals were built by Benedictine monks, such as Canterbury, Durham, Ely, and Norwich, and by the canons regular of St. Augustine, as Carlisle and Bristol. Lincoln, Chichester, Salisbury, York, St. David’s, and others were served by secular priests.

The architects of Scotland seem to have come from England and to have built after English models. The noblest of her mediaeval churches are Glasgow, St. Andrews, Dumblane, and Elgin, and among her convents, Kelso, Dryburgh, Holyrood, and Melrose.

In Spain, great minsters at Toledo, Burgos, and other cities were built in Gothic style in the thirteenth century, and Seville, which offers the largest floor surface of all the Christian churches, and is also of the same type, was begun in 1401 and completed 1520.

In Italy, Gothic was never fully at home. The cathedrals of Milan, Florence, and Siena are regarded as its finer specimens. Siena was begun in 1243. The minster of Milan was not begun till 1385. It is the largest Christian church after Seville and St. Peter’s. Its west façade is out of accord with the rest of the structure, which is pure Gothic. It is built of white marble and soars up to the clouds in hundreds of spires. Within full sight of the Milan cathedral are the Alps, crowned with snow and elevated far above the din of human traffic and voices; and in comparison with those mightier cathedrals of God, the creations of man seem small even as man himself seems small in comparison with his Maker.
